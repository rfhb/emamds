## 2. Scientific discussion

## 2.1. Introduction

## 2.1.1. Problem statement

## Disease or condition

Polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) is an inflammatory rheumatic disorder affecting individuals 50 years and older and characterised by acute onset of muscle pain and stiffness, primarily in the shoulders and pelvic girdle. PMR is associated with inflammation of extra-articular structures, in particular bursitis and synovitis; however, the pathophysiology of PMR is not fully understood. Pain usually develops abruptly but may also occur progressively over the course of weeks, and it is typically more intense during the night and first part of the day. Constitutional signs and symptoms such as malaise, fever and weight loss are frequently observed in individuals with PMR. However, clinical symptoms alone may be inconclusive when establishing the initial diagnosis. There is no specific diagnostic test that can diagnose PMR with absolute certainty. Typically, PMR is diagnosed based on an array of clinical features, such as age (≥ 50 years), pain with restricted range of motion and stiffness of the shoulder girdle, presence of acute phase reactants, e.g. erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and highsensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), and exclusion of differential diagnoses. Usually, ESR and hsCRP are elevated in untreated, newly diagnosed patients, but may also be normal in up to 22% of patients, however, a combination of normal ESR and hsCRP is rare in untreated PMR patients. The provisional American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) classification has shown 68% sensitivity and 78% specificity for PMR. There is a close relation between PMR and giant cell arteritis (GCA). PMR can occur independently or can also be associated with GCA, which is the most common form of primary vasculitis.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## State the claimed therapeutic indication

The claimed indication is:

Cosentyx is indicated for the treatment of polymyalgia rheumatica in adults who have had an inadequate response to glucocorticoids or who experience a relapse during glucocorticoid taper.

## Epidemiology

The peak incidence of PMR is observed in the 70-to-79-year age group, with women being affected 2 to 3 times more frequently than men. People of northern European descent have the highest incidence, with 34 to 113 new cases per 100,000 persons each year among those ≥50 years of age vs. 3 to 27 new cases per 100,000 persons of southern European descent.

## Aetiology and pathogenesis

There is limited understanding of the pathogenesis of PMR and the exact triggers of the disease and the factors contributing to chronicity or relapse remain unclear. IL-17 pathway has been suggested to play a role, and studies have shown elevated levels of IL-17A in PMR patients. IL-17A has the potential to induce IL-6 in various cell types, including synovial fibroblasts. Downregulating IL-17 in PMR has the potential to influence downstream effector cytokines, such as IL-6, implicated in PMR pathogenesis.

## Management

The main objective in treating PMR is to manage symptoms and prevent relapses. Although oral GC is the standard of care, an evidence-based GC treatment regimen for PMR has not yet been established. The recommended treatment by the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology-American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) for PMR starts with a prednisone dose of 12.5 to 25 mg daily, tapered down to 10 mg/day after 4-8 weeks and then gradually reduced until discontinuation after a minimum of 12 months of treatment, given that remission is maintained. While most patients experience rapid symptom relief, relapse rates remain high between 22% and 67% during GC tapering, and many require years of ongoing therapy, leading to substantial cumulative GC exposure and a high incidence of side effects.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

In terms of non-GC therapies, methotrexate (MTX) is conditionally recommended for the management of PMR, according to the 2015 EULAR/ACR recommendations, as an add-on therapy to GC, particularly in patients with high risk of relapse and those with high risk of GC side effects, as well as in those with prevalent relapse.

Regarding biologic therapies, an anti-IL-6 monoclonal antibody (mAb) sarilumab was approved by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in November 2024 for the treatment of adults with PMR for whom GCs have proved inadequate or who could not tolerate a GC taper. Despite recent developments, there remains a significant unmet medical need for GC-sparing therapies in PMR to improve the likelihood of achieving sustained remission while minimizing long-term GC use and associated toxicity.

## 2.1.2. About the product

Secukinumab (AIN457, Cosentyx) is a recombinant, fully human monoclonal anti-human IL-17A antibody of the IgG1/κ-class. IL-17A is produced by several cell types including memory CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes and is recognised as one of the principal pro-inflammatory cytokines in immunemediated inflammatory diseases.

Secukinumab was first registered in Japan on 26-Dec-2014, and is approved in more than 110 countries worldwide, including the EU/EEA, US, Japan, and China.

In the EU, secukinumab was approved on 15-Jan-2015 for the treatment of moderate to severe plaque PsO in adult patients, and subsequently for the following indications:

- Active psoriatic arthritis (PsA) and active ankylosing spondylitis (AS) in adults.
- Non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis (nr-axSpA) in adults.
- Paediatric moderate to severe plaque psoriasis (PsO).
- Enthesitis-related arthritis (ERA) and juvenile PsA (jPsA).
- Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) in adults.

Secukinumab is available in 150 mg powder for solution for injection, 150 mg/1 mL and 300 mg/2 mL solution for injection (available in PFS or AI), and 75 mg/0.5 mL solution for injection (available in PFS).

## 2.1.3. The development programme/compliance with CHMP guidance/scientific advice

The MAH received scientific advice from the CHMP (EMA/SA/0000087112) on 23 June 2022 pertaining to the clinical development, as well as from the Finnish Medicines Agency (Fimea) on 18 Dec 2025 pertaining to the adequacy of the overall content of the eCTD submission dossier. The advice provided by the CHMP was not fully followed and key deviations are discussed in the relevant sections below.

Most of the recommendations by CHMP on 23 June 2022 (EMA/SA/0000087112) were followed by the MAH. These included, e.g., details on inclusion and exclusion criteria, definitions of relapse and remission, and importantly, recommendation to test both doses of 150 mg and 300 mg by including 3rd 150 mg dose study arm in the pivotal clinical study.

However, in the scientific advice, the CHMP recommended a 52-week prednisone taper instead of the proposed 24-week prednisone taper whereas FDA accepted the 24-week tapering regimen in the protocol. As there was conflicting advice, the MAH proceeded with the 24-week prednisone taper and justifies the decision by the following reasons:

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

- The current PMR guidelines (e.g., EULAR/ACR 2015) recommend slow Glucocorticoids (GC) tapering, but these were established before GC-sparing therapies. However, these guidelines also recommend minimum effective individualised GC treatment duration, do not mandate extending GC therapy an additional year after each relapse, and advise shorter tapers especially for those with comorbidities. Recent biologic studies support reduced GC exposure.
- Literature evidence indicates that prolonged GC exposure, even at low dose, significantly increases health risks. Hence, a 24-week prednisone taper was used to minimise steroid toxicity particularly in older patients with relapsing PMR who often need long-term GC treatment.
- Additionally, comparing secukinumab 300 mg and 150 mg subcutaneously (s.c.) with the same 24week prednisone taper in the placebo arm allowed for meaningful assessment of secukinumab efficacy in PMR while providing an optimal balance between symptom control and cumulative GC exposure in an elderly patient population. Thus, the studied 24-week prednisone taper regimen was selected to balance the GC benefits and risks and allow for a meaningful comparison of secukinumab versus placebo.

In the scientific advice on 18 Dec 2025, Fimea expressed interest in understanding efficacy beyond 1 year. The MAH clarified that 6-month treatment-free follow-up data from the study C22301 would not be available for initial submission but that it could be provided during review, if requested. Fimea emphasised the need for a clear scientific and regulatory justification, noting that while the data look encouraging, the dossier will need to explain why a single pivotal study is sufficient. Fimea emphasised the importance of justifying the benefit/risk of proposed posology for patients older than 75 years old.

Fimea asked for clarification on the reasons for the Giant Cell Arthritis (GCA) study (CAIN457R12301) failure, given the similarity of GCA and PMR as a disease. They also requested additional insight into the subset of GCA patients who presented PMR symptoms. The MAH clarified that the Phase 3 trial R12301 for secukinumab in GCA did not achieve statistical significance when comparing secukinumab with a 26-week prednisone taper to placebo with a 52-week prednisone taper on both primary and secondary endpoints. However, the 300 mg arm showed numerical improvement in the primary endpoint and certain secondary endpoints. The MAH described that it is difficult to assess the exact reason, but it could be due to the different sites of inflammation in each of these two diseases.

In the application, no further discussion of reasons for failure of the phase 3 GCA study CAIN457R12301 was provided. Safety data from that study were, however, used as supportive data for assessment of benefit-to-risk (B/R) of the PMR indication, and the CSR was included in the submission.

## 2.3.2. Pharmacokinetics

## Bioanalytical methods

Quantification of secukinumab (AIN457) plasma concentration was initially performed with ELISA based method at Novartis Pharma AG. This analytical method was later transferred to SGS France, where it was cross validated. Later, the method was changed to ECLIA-based method which was validated at ICON Netherlands. ECLIA based method was then transferred to SGS France and the cross-validation report at SGS France is provided in this submission.. ## Population PK analysis

## Objectives

The objective of the population analysis was to characterise the PK properties of secukinumab in PMR, using population PK modelling.

## Model development

The population PK SpA model developed previously to describe the PK of secukinumab in patients with PsA, AS, and nr-axSpA was used to assess the adequacy of the model in describing the PMR PK data. It was built upon 15 studies including two i.v. Phase 3 Studies P12301 and P12302. The model described the PK of secukinumab by means of a two-compartment disposition model with first-order elimination and first-order absorption for the s.c. administration, and constant rate infusion for the i.v. administration. The model included covariate effects of body weight on clearance (CL), central volume (Vc), peripheral volume (Vp), and race (Asian vs. others) on CL. All parameters were estimated with good precision (relative standard error (RSE)% ranged from 0.783% to 7.44%). The estimate of KA was fixed at 0.180 1/day (Table 5).

## 2.3.5. Discussion on clinical pharmacology

The MAH has provided pharmacokinetic data from one pivotal Phase 3 study CAIN457C22301 (Study C22301) and from one open-label Phase 1b study CAIN457E22101. In addition, the MAH provided an exposure-response analysis report which included population pharmacokinetic analysis.

## Bioanalytical methods

The quantification of secukinumab plasma concentration of clinical samples (clinical studies C22301 and E22101) was performed using the ECLIA-based method cross-validated at SGS France in line with ICH M10 guideline. Method validation showed acceptable within- and between-run accuracy, precision and dilutional linearity. The specificity of the method was appropriately confirmed. No matrix effect was observed in healthy human, PMR patient serum, or in lipidemic serum. However, some matrix effect was observed in haemolysed serum but only in high level QC samples and since the number of haemolysed clinical samples to be tested was low no further concern is raised. The samples retained their stability during freeze/thaw of 5 cycles, storage at room temperature (about 25 hours), and longterm storage at -24°C and -75°C up to 799 days covering the storage time of clinical samples. Also, the cross-validation between ECLIA methods at ICON Netherlands and SGS France is appropriately

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

demonstrated. Upon the CHMP request the comparability between the previously used ELISA- and the current ECLIA-methods has been appropriately demonstrated.

The bioanalytical reports for clinical studies C22301 and E22101 are provided. The analysis of clinical samples in terms of secukinumab plasma concentration was reliable within the given accuracy and precision ranges. The reasons for repeat analysis were acceptable and the required criteria for incurred method analysis was met.

To be noted that the same electrochemiluminescence method and ELISA method was used for the detection of anti-secukinumab antibody (ADA) and neutralising anti-secukinumab antibody (NAb) formation in human serum of PMR patients, respectively, as in previous submissions.

## Observed PK in patients with polymyalgia rheumatica

Noncompartmental analysis of the observed PK data in study C22301 demonstrated that exposure to secukinumab increased in proportion with dose in patients with PMR. Dose proportionality has previously been shown in other target populations.

## Population PK analysis

## Strategy

The MAH used previously developed population PK SpA model of secukinumab to assess the adequacy of the model in describing the PMR PK data. The previous population PK model was built upon 15 studies including two i.v. Phase 3 Studies P12301 and P12302. The current data set of PMR PK used for assessing the adequacy of the model included total of 2248 measurements from 252 PMR patients treated with secukinumab. Only one PK sample (pre first dose BLQ) was excluded. This is acceptable.

Firstly, the MAH used the POP PK model to simulate secukinumab concentrations for hypothetical patients with the same weight and same race (Asian vs. Non-Asian) as the PMR patients in Study C22301 and administered with the two PMR regimens. These simulated values were compared to observed secukinumab concentrations measured in Study C22301. This approach can be considered acceptable. The results showed that PMR secukinumab concentrations for both dosing regimens (150 mg s.c. and 300 mg s.c.) are largely consistent with the population PK SpA model simulations, with only a slight overprediction at Week 1.

Secondly, the MAH used the population PK SpA model to conduct an external validation using PMR PK data from Study C22301. PMR observed PK data were reproduced by simulation from the population PK SpA model accounting for the actual dosing history, PK sampling times, and covariates of the patients in Study C22301. The observed and simulated data were compared using VPC diagnostic plot. This approach is acceptable. The resulting VPC plots for both dosing regimens (150 mg s.c. and 300 mg s.c.) showed that the population PK SpA model was able to adequately describe the observed data without any systematic bias.

Overall, the population PK SpA model can be considered to adequately describe the PMR concentration data from Study C22301.

## PK metrics for exposure-efficacy and exposure-safety analysis

Secukinumab exposure metrics (Cavg,ss for safety analysis and Cmin,ss at week 52 for efficacy analysis) were derived using the population PK SpA model from their individual secukinumab time profile predicted, using Empirical Bayes Estimates (EBEs).

## Exposure-efficacy analysis

## Sustained Remission at Week 52

The proportion of participants achieving sustained remission at Week 52 was the primary efficacy endpoint in Study C22301. The MAH estimated exposure-efficacy relationship between Cmin,ss calculated according to per-protocol dosing regimen at Week 52 and sustained remission at week 52 using the logistic regression model. It is agreed that the observed exposure-response relationship did not show sufficient curvature to support reliable estimation of a sigmoid Emax model. Each parametercovariate relationship was tested in a stepwise manner using first forward selection and after that backward elimination step was done. Only one covariate was found significant (Region: Japan vs nonJapan). This modelling approach is considered acceptable.

The results of analysis between exposure (Cmin,ss) and sustained remission at week 52 showed that there was a trend where higher exposure sextiles had increase in sustained remission at week 52 compared to lowest sextile and placebo. The observed values of sustained remission for the second lowest exposure sextile were as high as sustained remission for the highest exposure textiles. When looking at relationships between exposure and sustained remission at week 52 for different subgroups, no clear difference for sustained remission can be observed between exposures after 150 mg s.c. or 300 mg s.c.. For Japanese patients there seems to be a reduction in sustained remission which was a covariate in logistic regression model. Also, there seems to be no clear trend between exposure and sustained remission for Japanese patients. However, it should be noted that the number of Japanese patients is relatively small (n=32), and therefore comparison of sustained remission between different exposure sextiles should interpreted with caution. The SmPC section 4.2 provides dosing recommendation and indicates when the maintenance dose can be increased based on the clinical response.

## Adjusted annual cumulative GC dose

The MAH estimated exposure-safety relationship between Cmin,ss and adjusted annual cumulative GC dose using the linear regression model. It is agreed that the observed exposure-response relationship did not show sufficient curvature to support reliable estimation of a sigmoid Emax model. Each parameter-covariate relationship was tested in a stepwise manner using first forward selection and after that backward elimination step was done. Two covariates were found significant (baseline prednisone Dose of 10mg/day, baseline Age ≥75 years old). This modelling approach is considered acceptable.

The results of analysis between exposure and adjusted annual cumulative GC dose showed that there was only a slight trend where higher exposure sextiles had decrease in adjusted annual cumulative GC dose compared to lower sextiles, while placebo had visibly higher cumulative GC dose. Baseline age and baseline prednisone dose were associated with the adjusted annual cumulative GC dose. For the subgroup of patients with age ≥75 years old, and patients with baseline prednisone dose of 15 mg/day the adjusted annual cumulative GC dose was greater than the other respective subgroups. Especially, for the subgroup of patients with baseline prednisone dose of 15 mg/day this would seem reasonable since these patients start on a higher prednisone dose and are thus likely to have more severe disease. When looking at the relationship between exposure and the adjusted annual cumulative GC dose for different subgroups, it was difficult to observe any clear difference in cumulative GC dose between 150 mg s.c. and 300 mg s.c. dose groups. This is reflected in the SmPC section 5.1, \"Treatment with secukinumab (150 mg or 300 mg) with a 24-week prednisone taper showed superiority in achieving glucocorticoid-free sustained remission at week 52 compared to placebo with a 24-week prednisone taper.\" The effect of baseline characteristics (age and prednisone dose) on cumulative GC dose appeared to be more substantial than exposure to secukinumab.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## Exposure-safety analysis

## Serious adverse events

The MAH estimated exposure-safety relationship between Cavg,ss and serious adverse events (SAEs) at Week 52 using the logistic regression model. Each parameter-covariate relationship was tested in a stepwise manner using first forward selection and after that backward elimination step was done. Only one covariate was found significant (Baseline Age 50 to 64 years old). Exposure and serious adverse events relationship was also evaluated by subgroups (age, body weight, baseline prednisone dosing, baseline CRP, region, gender). This approach is considered acceptable.

Results showed that the predicted SAE rate at the geometric mean Cavg,ss of 150 mg s.c. and 300 mg s.c. are on the plateau of the exposure-response relationship and comparable to placebo. No association between exposure and serious adverse events was identified in the analyses stratified by the aforementioned subgroups.

Even though age from 50 to 64 yrs was a covariate in the model, the incidence of serious adverse events was very low across active treatment groups within the 50-64-year age subgroup (no SAEs in 300 mg s.c. regimen, two SAEs in 150 mg s.c. regimen). There were only limited number of events and no consistent pattern. Therefore, the available data do not indicate a meaningful age-related association.

## Hypersensitivity

The MAH estimated the exposure-safety relationship between Cavg,ss and hypersensitivity at Week 52 using the logistic regression model. Each parameter-covariate relationship was tested in a stepwise manner using first forward selection and after that backward elimination step was done. No covariates were found significant after these steps. Exposure and hypersensitivity relationship was also evaluated by subgroups (age, body weight, baseline prednisone dosing, baseline CRP, region, gender). This approach is considered acceptable.

The analysis results showed that the predicted hypersensitivity AE rate at the geometric mean Cavg,ss of 150 mg s.c. and 300 mg s.c. are on the plateau of the exposure-response relationship. Similar conclusions can be made when stratifying by aforementioned subgroups.

## Candida infections and serious infections

No formal analysis could be done to characterise the exposure-response relationship for candida infections or serious infections due to the limited number of patients reporting these endpoints. Exploratory graphical analysis for these two endpoints showed that secukinumab exposure (Cavg,ss) was similar between patients with or without occurrence of these infections. In addition, Wilcoxon test showed that there was no statistically significant relationship between secukinumab exposure and candida infections or serious infections between patients with or without occurrence of these infections.

## 2.3.6. Conclusions on clinical pharmacology

The previously developed population PK SpA model accurately described observed PMR PK data from study C22301. The population PK model was also used to provide individual exposure estimates for exposure-efficacy and exposure-safety analysis which was found acceptable by the CHMP.

Exposure-efficacy analyses did not show any clear difference for the evaluated efficacy endpoints at exposure levels after 150 mg s.c. and 300 mg s.c., nor did it show any increasing trend for the evaluated safety endpoints as exposure increased. The posology including the recommendations to increase the maintenance dose from 150 to 300 mg is described clearly in the section 4.2 of the SmPC. The application is approvable from the clinical pharmacology perspective.

## 2.4. Clinical efficacy

## 2.4.1. Dose response study(ies)

No specific dose-range finding studies were conducted in PMR. The GCA Phase 2 proof-of-concept study CAIN457ADE11C (TitAIN), secukinumab 300 mg s.c. every 4 weeks provided evidence of efficacy in GCA, including GCA patients with PMR symptoms (post-hoc subgroup analysis). Consequently, secukinumab 150 mg and 300 mg 4-weekly regimens were investigated as compared to placebo, combined with a 52-week GC tapering regimen, in the Phase 3 GCA study CAIN457R12301 (in this AR, \"R12301\"). However, the phase 3 study R12301 in GCA patients did not reach its primary endpoint with either 150 mg or 300 mg dose of secukinumab.

Given that CGA and PMR are overlapping conditions, the MAH expected secukinumab 300 mg dose to be efficacious in PMR patients based on the phase 2 study CAIN457ADE11C, and thus it was chosen by the MAH to be tested in Phase 3 study C22301. Based on regulatory feedback, including EMA scientific advice (EMA/SA/0000087112), an additional treatment arm (secukinumab 150 mg) was added to study C22301.

## 2.4.2. Main study - C22301

The key efficacy data to support the current application in adult PMR patients with a recent relapse is provided from the Phase 3 study C22301. The study was ongoing at the time of submission, and an interim CSR with data cut-off date at last participant Week 52 visit (10-Sep-2025) was submitted.

An extension period was ongoing, data for which were not yet available. Participants who completed the Treatment Period up to Week 52 (such as participants in sustained remission and on escape treatment) and who experience a relapse during the treatment-free follow-up period were eligible to enter an extension study where available. For participants who do not enter the extension study, the minimum follow-up is 20 weeks after last study drug administration; participants on rescue treatment are not eligible to roll-over into the extension study.

## Title of Study

A randomised, parallel-group, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre Phase III trial to evaluate efficacy and safety of secukinumab administered subcutaneously versus placebo, in combination with a glucocorticoid taper regimen, in patients with polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR).

## Methods

## Study design

Study C22301 was designed to evaluate the efficacy of secukinumab in combination with 24-week prednisone taper vs. placebo (plus 24-week prednisone taper) in adult patients with a recent PMR relapse, as well as to evaluate the safety profile of secukinumab treatment in this patient population.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The study consisted of a ≤6-week screening period (or up to 8 weeks in the event of a major healthcare disruption or a need to complete screening requirements, e.g., tuberculosis testing and work-up), a 52-week treatment period, and a follow-up period of up to 24 weeks (Figure 14).

The screening period, which assessed the eligibility of patients before randomisation and during which time patients were treated with GCs as per the Investigator's judgement. At the end of the screening period, patients were switched to prednisone (15 mg/day or 10 mg/day that was considered to be medically appropriate as per Investigator judgment) in order to follow the protocol-defined prednisone tapering regimens initialised at baseline of the treatment period.

## Study participants

The target population was patients ≥ 50 years of age with a diagnosis of PMR who had at least one episode of PMR relapse within 12 weeks prior to baseline while on a GC dose of at least 5 mg/day (prednisone equivalent), and who had a history of being treated for at least 8 consecutive weeks with GC at a dose of ≥ 10 mg/day (prednisone equivalent). - Male or non-pregnant, non-lactating female participants at least 50 years of age.
- Diagnosis of PMR according to the provisional ACR/EULAR classification criteria: Participants ≥ 50 years of age with a history of bilateral shoulder pain accompanied by elevated CRP concentration (≥ 10 mg/L) and/or elevated ESR (≥ 30 mm/hr) who scored at least 4 points from the following optional classification criteria:
- o Morning stiffness &gt; 45 minutes (min) (2 points)
- o Hip pain or restricted range of motion (1 point)
- o Absence of rheumatoid factor and/or anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (2 points)

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

- o Absence of other joint involvement (1 point)
- Participant must have a history of being treated for at least 8 consecutive weeks with prednisone (≥ 10 mg/day, or equivalent dose of another GC) at any time prior to screening.

- o Recurrence of bilateral shoulder girdle and/or bilateral hip girdle pain associated with inflammatory stiffness with or without additional symptoms indicative of PMR relapse (such as constitutional symptoms) within 12 weeks prior to BSL that are in the opinion of the Investigator not due to other diseases that may mimic PMR such as osteoarthritis in shoulders or hips, polyarticular calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease, rotator cuff disease, adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder) or fibromyalgia.

- Participants must have been treated as per local treatment recommendations following the latest PMR relapse and must be on prednisone of at least 7.5 mg/day (or equivalent) and not exceeding 25 mg/day at screening and during the screening period.
- A prednisone dose of 15 mg/day or 10 mg/day at BSL is medically appropriate as per Investigator judgment.

The total duration of the tapering regimen could not exceed 28 weeks (for participants with a baseline prednisone dose of 15 mg/day) and 24 weeks (for participants with a baseline prednisone dose of 10 mg/day), respectively.

Further modifications to the prednisone taper (at or after Week 12) were not permitted; however, all patients whose PMR symptoms did not adequately improve or who experienced a PMR relapse while following the prednisone taper could receive escape or rescue treatment.

If a patient's symptoms did not adequately improve, or if a patient experienced a PMR relapse, or if a patient was newly diagnosed with GCA while receiving prednisone as per specified tapering schedule or after the end of the pre-specified tapering regimen, the patient was managed using 'escape treatment' or 'rescue treatment' as follows:

- Escape treatment: The GC dose could be increased/re-initiated as determined by the Investigator in accordance with standard of care. The patients who received escape treatment stopped the protocol specified taper regimen (if still on), remained on blinded study treatment (secukinumab or placebo) in the study and continued to attend all subsequent scheduled visits until the end of the study.
- Rescue treatment: Alternatively, Investigators could decide to discontinue patients from study treatment to manage otherwise uncontrolled PMR with alternative medication(s) with or without GCs with dosing determined by the Investigator in accordance with standard of care. Rescue treatment could only be given if patients did not adequately respond to escape treatment or if study treatment was discontinued. Patients on rescue treatment stopped the protocol specified taper regimen, discontinued study treatment but continued to attend all subsequent scheduled visits and assessments up to Week 52 or 20 weeks after the last administration of study treatment, whichever was later.

Patients who completed the Week 52 end-of-treatment visit (last administration of study treatment being at Week 48), such as patients in sustained remission and on escape treatment, entered a treatment-free follow-up period for up to 24 weeks (until Week 76). The CSR for study C22301 covering the treatment-free follow-up period through Week 76 was submitted with the response to 1 st round RSI.

If patients experienced a relapse during the treatment-free follow-up period, they were eligible to enter the extension study CAIN457C22301E1 (ongoing as of 10 July 2026).

## Concomitant treatment

Participants were allowed to be on short-term (maximum of 2 weeks throughout the duration of the study) GCs in addition to the protocol-defined prednisone taper, if deemed necessary for the management of conditions other than PMR as per medical judgment of the Investigator. The additional cumulative systemic (oral, intravenous or intramuscular) GC use should not have exceeded prednisone 100 mg or equivalent. Intraarticular GC injections in large joints (e.g. knee, shoulder) were not allowed.

Participants taking MTX (≤ 15 mg/week) at study entry were allowed to continue their medication provided they had taken it for at least 2 months and were on a stable dose for at least 4 weeks prior to BSL and throughout the study. Participants on MTX were to take folic acid supplementation before BSL and during the trial to minimise the likelihood of MTX-associated toxicity.

Vitamin D (at 1000 I.U. per day) and calcium supplements were strongly recommended during the study for osteoporosis prevention.

## Signs and symptoms of PMR

Typical symptoms of active PMR were bilateral pain in the shoulder girdle and/or hip girdle associated with moderate to severe stiffness after inactivity.

Atypical PMR symptoms were swelling, pain and stiffness in both hands attributed to PMR without the presence of PMR symptoms in shoulder girdle and hip girdle.

In addition, if present, an assessment of whether these typical or atypical symptoms of PMR had resulted in re-initiation or increase of GC dose (escape treatment) or change in treatment (rescue treatment) was conducted.

Additional symptoms associated with active PMR (only in combination with typical or atypical symptoms of PMR) could be:

- Difficulty elevating the arms attributed to PMR.
- Swelling and pain of hands symptoms attributed to PMR in combination with shoulder girdle and/or hip girdle.
- Constitutional symptoms (including fatigue/malaise, elevated body temperature [&gt; 37.5°C], sweats, unintended weight loss).

## Signs and symptoms of new-onset GCA

The signs of a new-onset GCA included the following:

- Localised headache, temporal artery or scalp tenderness.
- Visual signs or symptoms such as acute or subacute vision loss due to arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (A-AION), transient blurry vision (generally monocular or at least affecting one eye at a time, but potentially affecting both eyes) due to GCA.
- Jaw or mouth pain.
- New or worsened extremity claudication.
- Fever (&gt; 38°C) due to GCA.
- Other features judged by the clinician-investigator to be consistent with GCA diagnosis.
- GCA confirmed by histology or imaging (outside of the study).

If an Investigator detected signs and symptoms that were suggestive of new-onset GCA, it was encouraged to confirm the diagnosis as per standard of care. In addition, if present, an assessment of

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

whether these signs and symptoms of GCA had resulted in re-initiation or increase of GC dose (escape treatment) or change in treatment (rescue treatment) was done.

## Definition of remission vs relapse of PMR

Remission was defined as absence of signs of active PMR (based on the assessment of typical and atypical symptoms as detailed above) and the absence of signs/symptoms of a new-onset GCA that would require an increase of the GC dose, re-initiation of GC treatment or other changes in treatment of PMR (i.e., requiring escape or rescue treatment). Conversely, a relapse was defined as a recurrence of symptoms of PMR or as the detection of signs and symptoms of a new-onset GCA that requires an increase of GC dose, re-initiation of GC treatment, or other changes in treatment of PMR.

## Inflammatory markers

ESR and CRP were not part of the definition of the primary endpoint, but sustained (clinical) remission in combination with normal/not clinically significantly elevated ESR and hsCRP were assessed as a secondary endpoint (\"complete sustained remission\").

A patient achieved complete sustained remission if the following were met:

- no elevation of ESR to ≥40 mm/hr at ≥2 consecutive scheduled visits from Week 12 to Week 52;
- normalisation of hsCRP (&lt;10 mg/L) without elevation to ≥10 mg/L at ≥2 consecutive scheduled visits from Week 12 to Week 52.

Access to the values of the inflammatory biomarkers was considered to potentially unblind patients and Investigators and impact the assessment of the signs and symptoms of disease, thus inducing bias in the assessment of the primary endpoint. These inflammatory markers were handled as follows:

- The hsCRP test was performed by the Central Laboratory and the results were blinded to the Investigator, study personnel and Clinical Trial Team (CTT) following the BSL visit.
- The ESR test was performed locally by personnel who were not involved in any other study procedures using the kit provided by the central laboratory and the results were blinded to the Investigator, study personnel (except for Laboratory Test Reviewer) and CTT following the BSL visit.

Only in case of markedly elevated ESR values (≥80 mm/hr) or if ESR value was &gt;60 mm/hr at two consecutive scheduled visits, the ESR threshold (but not the exact value) was shared with the Investigator.

## Adjusted annual cumulative GC dose

The cumulative GC dose through Week 52, defined as the total cumulative exposure to prednisone or other GCs (conversion to prednisone equivalent dose) through Week 52, was calculated by combining the total commercial GC dose (used for escape treatment of PMR or for treating any other condition) and protocol specified prednisone taper through Week 52.

## Time to first use of escape treatment or rescue treatment

Time to first use of escape treatment or rescue treatment was defined as the date from randomisation to the date of the first time when the escape treatment or rescue treatment was used as measured in days through Week 52.

In general, patients who prematurely discontinued from the study were censored on the date of discontinuation.

## Sample size

A (two-sided) α of 5% was used to control the experimental-wise type I error rate. Sequential testing approach was employed for multiplicity control. A total sample size of 360 patients (120 patients for each of the secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg, and placebo groups) was deemed sufficient to achieve adequate power for testing the primary and secondary endpoints between the secukinumab 300 mg vs. placebo groups, and between the secukinumab 150 mg vs. placebo groups.

In terms of the primary endpoint sustained remission, the sample size of 360 patients was based on an assumed 50% response rate with secukinumab 300 mg/150 mg and 29% response rate to achieve 90% power (α=0.05, two-sided).

## Randomisation

Patients were randomised in a 1:1:1 ratio into one of the three treatment arms via Interactive Response Technology (IRT). Patients were stratified at randomisation by Japan vs. Non-Japan, and within the non-Japan strata, patients were further stratified by starting GC dose at baseline (10 mg/day or 15 mg/day).

## Blinding (masking)

This was a double-blind randomised clinical trial. Patients, investigators, site personnel, and those performing the assessments were to remain blinded to treatment assignment from the time of randomisation until the final database lock (DBL) at end of study.

At the time of the primary endpoint analysis DBL, after all participants completed Week 52, Sponsor personnel involved in study report submission preparation had access to the unblinded results. Blinded Sponsor personnel continued to be involved in study conduct and oversight.

## Statistical methods

## Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint was sustained remission at Week 52. The primary estimand used a composite strategy, classifying patients as non-responders if they discontinued treatment or used prohibited medications. Escape and rescue treatments were already incorporated in the endpoint definition, i.e., considered as non-response. A missing remission assessment, not related to an intercurrent event, was imputed as non-response except for an intermittent missing assessment between two assessments when participant was observed to have remission.

Differences in response proportions between secukinumab and placebo arms were analysed using logistic regression, adjusting for baseline prednisone dose (15 mg/day vs. 10 mg/day), region (Japan vs. Non-Japan), and sex. Difference in marginal response proportions with p-value and respective 95% confidence interval were estimated from the logistic regression model using the marginal standardisation method (g-computation) with sandwich variance estimator. In this method, the logistic regression model is used to predict, based on the specified baseline covariates, each participant's probability of sustained remission at Week 52 (i.e. 'response') under each of the comparative treatments. Averaging these predictions for each treatment group provides the estimate of the mean response rate for each treatment group. Then the difference will be derived based on the estimated mean response rates comparing secukinumab vs. placebo.

A tipping point analysis was conducted for the primary endpoint to evaluate sensitivity of the result to assumptions about missing primary endpoint assessment.

## Secondary endpoints

## Complete sustained remission

The same logistic regression model utilised for the primary endpoint was applied to the secondary endpoint of complete sustained remission. The same estimand and missing data imputation methods as for the primary endpoint were used.

## Adjusted annual cumulative GC dose

The primary estimand used treatment policy strategy for escape treatment or treatment discontinuation before Week 52. Composite strategy was applied to handle rescue or prohibited medication use before Week 52: When rescue treatment or prohibited medication occurred before Week 52, the post-event GC dose was imputed based on 87.5 percentile of average daily dose amongst participants who used escape (but no rescue or prohibited treatments), while observed GC data were retained up to the time of the intercurrent event.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The results, presented in this assessment report with the abbreviated label \"Cumulative GC dose\", are based on a supplementary analysis, requested by the CHMP, applying treatment policy strategy across the use of escape or rescue treatment, prohibited medications, or treatment discontinuation prior to Week 52. All observed GC use were included in the analysis. For missing data due to study dropout, or other reasons, multiple imputation under a copy-increment-reference (CIR) assumption was implemented on the log-transformed cumulative GC dose, using a Bayesian MMRM model with treatment, analysis time point (4-week intervals), baseline prednisone dose (10 mg/day vs. 15 mg/day), sex, region (Japan vs. Non-Japan), and treatment by analysis time point interaction in the model.

The ANOVA model with Huber-White \"sandwich\" standard error, adjusting for treatment group, stratification factors of baseline prednisone dose (10 mg/day vs. 15 mg/day), region (Japan vs. NonJapan), and sex was used to compare mean adjusted annual cumulative GC doses between secukinumab and placebo arms. The treatment-specific marginal means (\"LSmeans\") are based on marginal standardisation using the observed distribution of afore-mentioned stratification factors in the study population.

## Time to first use of escape treatment or rescue treatment

The primary estimand for this endpoint addressed the use of prohibited medications prior to Week 52 with a composite strategy, i.e., their initiation was considered as event similarly to initiation of escape and rescue medication. Treatment discontinuation was handled with a treatment policy strategy. Timeto-event curves for the secukinumab and placebo arms were compared using a log-rank test, stratified by baseline prednisone dose (10 mg/day vs. 15 mg/day).

Cox proportional hazards model, adjusted for baseline prednisone dose (10mg/day vs. 15 mg/day), region (Japan vs. non-Japan) and sex, estimated the hazard ratio and 95% CI between treatment arms.

## PROs (FACIT-Fatigue, HAQ-DI) score at Week 52

The primary estimand for assessing change from baseline in FACIT-Fatigue and Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability Index (HAQ-DI) score at Week 52 used a composite strategy to handle the intercurrent events (escape treatment, rescue treatment, use of prohibited medications, or treatment discontinuation prior to Week 52). Under this strategy, the worst observed baseline value across all treatment arms was assigned as the patient's Week 52 value which was justified as follows: This strategy reflects that intercurrent events of a participant who takes escape treatment, rescue treatment, who discontinues from the study treatment prematurely, or who uses prohibited medication are in themselves considered informative about the participant's outcome of not being successfully treated, and therefore, Week 52 FACIT-Fatigue/HAQ-DI data after these intercurrent events are assigned the worst observed baseline value.

The results included in this assessment report are based on a supplementary estimand, for which PROs were considered irrespective of whether study treatment was discontinued or prohibited medication used. For the three participants whose treatment assignment unblinded before Week 52, the composite strategy was applied, as described above. If PRO was missing in absence of prior intercurrent events, the data were multiple imputed using copy increment in reference method.

The ANCOVA model with Huber-White \"sandwich\" standard errors, adjusting for treatment group, baseline prednisone dose (10 mg/day vs. 15 mg/day), region (Japan vs. Non-Japan), sex, and baseline PRO score (FACIT-Fatigue or HAQ-DI score) was applied. The analysis compared mean changes from baseline between secukinumab and placebo arms, providing the difference and 95% CI.

Baseline characteristics were mostly similar across treatment arms. However, differences were observed for weight, with a median weight of 76,9 kg, 73.0 kg and 70 kg in the secukinumab 300mg, 150 mg and placebo arms, respectively. The proportion of subjects with weight &lt;70 kg was also highest in the placebo group (48.4%) vs. secukinumab 300mg (32.3%) and 150mg (40.9%) groups. At baseline, hsCRP was &lt;10 mg/L and ESR &lt;30 mm/hr in a somewhat larger proportion of subjects in the placebo vs. secukinumab groups (Table 12).

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The most commonly reported (&gt; 20% in any treatment arm) historical or concurrent conditions were hypertension (56.7%, 54.3%, 58.3%), osteoarthritis (26.8%, 32.3%, 28.3%), and hypercholesterolaemia (21.3%, 15.0%, 13.4%) in the secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg, and placebo arms, respectively.

The most frequent pre-existing medical conditions considered by the MAH as possibly related to GC toxicity were reported as follows:

- Hypertension: secukinumab 300 mg: 56.7%; secukinumab 150 mg: 54.3%; placebo: 58.3%;
- Insomnia: secukinumab 300 mg: 18.1%; secukinumab 150 mg: 16.5%; placebo: 17.3%;
- Diabetes (including steroid diabetes): secukinumab 300 mg: 15.0%; secukinumab 150 mg: 17.3%; placebo: 17.3%;
- Cataract (including cataract subcapsular and cataract nuclear): secukinumab 300 mg: 16.5%; secukinumab 150 mg: 16.5%; placebo: 17.3%;
- Depression (including major depression): secukinumab 300 mg: 12.6%; secukinumab 150 mg: 11.0%; placebo: 12.6%.

Medical history of fractures was reported in 15.7%, 11.0%, and 7.1% of participants in the secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg, and placebo arms, respectively; some participants had multiple fractures in their medical histories.

The starting dose of prednisone (mg/day) at baseline was 10 mg in the secukinumab 300mg, secukinumab 150 mg and placebo groups, respectively: 88/127 (69.3%), 87/127 (68.5%) and 92/127 (72.4%) subjects; and 15 mg/day 38/127 (29.9%), 39/127 (30.7%) and 35/127 (27.6%) subjects, respectively.

## Prior therapy

The most commonly used classes of prior medications by ATC Level 1 in the secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg, and placebo arms were systemic hormonal preparations (99.2% 99.2%, and 98.4%), antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents (22.8%, 19.7%, and 13.4%), musculoskeletal system agents (15.7%, 15.7%, and 10.2%), and nervous system agents (12.6%, 10.2%, and 11.0%).

Any prior treatment of PMR in addition to GC was more frequent in both secukinumab groups than placebo group, especially the use of methotrexate (MTX) (Table 12).

In the secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg, and placebo treatment arms, 57.5%, 55.1%, and 56.7% of participants, respectively, had undergone at least 1 surgery or non-drug procedure prior to study enrolment.

## Numbers analysed

Out of all randomised patients, two were mis-randomised (one mis-randomised patient in each secukinumab group) and did not receive treatment; these patients were not included in the Full Analysis Set (FAS) and Safety set. Thus, the FAS comprised of 379 patients with 126 patients each in the secukinumab 300 mg and 150 mg arms, and 127 patients in the placebo arm.

## Exposure to study treatment and concomitant therapy

## Exposure to study treatment

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The median durations of exposure to secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg, and placebo were 420.0 days, 411.5 days, and 393.0 days, respectively. The number of patients exposed for ≥ 52 weeks were as follows: 107 participants (84.9%) in the secukinumab 300 mg arm, 100 participants (79.4%) in the secukinumab 150 mg arm and 90 participants (70.9%) in placebo. Altogether 3 participants in secukinumab 150 mg group and 2 participants in placebo group had protocol deviations related to treatment deviations.

## Concomitant therapy

The most common (&gt;30% in any treatment arm) concomitant medications received by patients were cholecalciferol (secukinumab 300 mg arm: 38.1%; secukinumab 150 mg arm: 33.3%; placebo arm: 31.5%), and paracetamol (secukinumab 300 mg arm: 36.5%; secukinumab 150 mg arm: 38.9%; placebo arm: 32.3%). Concomitant methotrexate was used by 20.6%, 19.8% and 20.2% of the participants in secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg and placebo groups, respectively.

Concomitant prednisone, including prednisone used as escape/rescue treatment, as well as prednisone used for conditions other than PMR, was received by 38.1%, 35.7% and 51.2% of participants in secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg and placebo groups, respectively.

## Supportive study

The current submission includes CSRs of two studies conducted in patients with giant cell arteritis (GCA), one phase 2 study and one phase 3 study.

Of these, the MAH has named the phase 3 study CAIN457R12301 (in this AR, \"R12301\") to be supportive for the application, and the safety data have been used in assessment of safety (see section 2.4 of this AR). The MAH indicates that the study did not achieve its primary endpoint; however, efficacy data are not separately discussed by the MAH within this submission. No discussion is provided by the MAH on the phase 2 study CAIN457ADE11C. A short description of the GCA studies in included below.

The phase 2 study (CAIN457ADE11C) was a randomised, parallel-group, double-blind, placebocontrolled, multicentre trial conducted to investigate the safety and efficacy of secukinumab (AIN457) in patients with GCA. In total 65 patients were randomised to receive either secukinumab 300 mg SC weekly from week 0 to 4 and then every 4 weeks from week 4 to week 48 (27 patients) or corresponding placebo injection SC (25 patients). The treatment regimen included co-administered prednisolone to all patients in a tapered regimen from their baseline dose (25 mg to 60 mg) to 1 mg at Week 26 (last dose of prednisolone, 0 mg from Week 27 onwards).

The study indicated efficacy of secukinumab, since 22 patients (81.5%) in the secukinumab group vs. 12 patients (48.0%) in the placebo group were in remission at Week 12. Furthermore, 59.3% of patients in the secukinumab group vs. 8.0% in the placebo group were in sustained remission until Week 52.

The majority of patients in the secukinumab group had tapered down to prednisolone dose ≤ 5 mg/day at Week 19, Week 28 and Week 52 (88.0%, 82.6% and 90.5%) whereas a lower percentage of patients had tapered down to this dose of prednisolone in the placebo group at each respective time point (50.0%, 45.0%, and 76.5%).

The phase 3 study (R12301) was a randomised, parallel-group, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre trial to investigate the efficacy and safety of secukinumab 300 mg and 150 mg administered subcutaneously versus placebo, in combination with a glucocorticoid taper regimen, in patients with GCA.

The study consisted of screening period of up to 6 weeks, a 56-week treatment period (Treatment Period 1), a second treatment period of 52 weeks (Treatment Period 2), long-term extension period with variable duration up to 3.5 years (Treatment Period 3) and a 12-week from last study treatment (8-week after EOT) Safety Follow-Up Period. In total 294 participants randomised to one of the following 3 treatment arms in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg or placebo. Patients in the secukinumab arms also received a 26-week prednisone taper regimen, then 26-week placebo to prednisone, whereas patients in the placebo arm received a 52-week prednisone taper regimen.

The study did not meet its primary endpoint. The proportion of participants who achieved sustained remission at Week 52 was numerically, but not statistically significantly, greater for the secukinumab 300 mg arm (25.6%) vs the placebo arm (16.9%) (marginal difference: 8.7, 95% CI: -1.3 to 18.8, p=0.0895). When excluding CRP component of primary endpoint definition, the proportion of participants who achieved sustained clinical remission at Week 52 was similar for the secukinumab 300 mg arm (36.4%) vs the placebo arm (33.0%) (p=0.5788). The cumulative corticosteroid dose was lower in the secukinumab 300mg arm vs. placebo mainly due to the planned shorter corticosteroid taper; but no difference in total corticosteroid use was seen in the 150mg secukinumab arm vs. placebo arm, regardless of the shorter protocol-derived corticosteroid taper in the secukinumab arm.

## 2.4.3. Discussion on clinical efficacy

## Design, methods and conduct of clinical study

The MAH has conducted a double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group, multicentre Phase 3 trial C22301 to evaluate efficacy and safety of secukinumab administered subcutaneously versus placebo, in combination with a glucocorticoid taper regimen, in patients with polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR). The study was ongoing at the time of this report with an extension period. However, an interim CSR with data up to the end of the placebo-controlled treatment period (up to 52 weeks) was submitted.

The main elements including the population, placebo-control, the primary and key secondary endpoints were endorsed by the CHMP in a scientific advice procedure. However, the CHMP stated that \"the proposed fast tapering scheme will not contextualise the effect of secukinumab, i.e. it will not inform of the steroid-sparing potential of secukinumab when compared to placebo on top of a sub-optimal scheme. Thus, steroid tapering over 52 weeks would be considered clinically more relevant.\" This advice was not followed (see later).

## Population

Patients with PMR at least 50 years of age were eligible, if they had been treated for at least 8 consecutive weeks with prednisone (≥ 10 mg/day, or equivalent dose of another GC) at any time prior to screening, had relapsed within 12 weeks prior to study baseline, were treated as per local treatment recommendations following the latest PMR relapse and must have been on prednisone of at least 7.5 mg/day. Hence, the patients were not necessarily such for whom a prednisone dose of 15 mg/day or 10 mg/day would be prescribed with the aim of tapering over 24 weeks. Nevertheless, the study is considered acceptable for assessment of efficacy and safety of secukinumab compared to placebo in the proposed indication for use.

Of note, patients with evidence or history of giant cell arteritis (GCA) were excluded from the study. PMR and GCA are closely related. Features of PMR occur in approximately 42% of patients with GCA, and 3 to 46% of patients with PMR have evidence of large-vessel inflammation on imaging, therefore, PMR and GCA are often regarded as a disease spectrum. Hence, in clinical practice, part of PMR patients would be expected to have concomitant GCA. Of note, the MAH conducted two studies in patients with GCA: the phase 2 study (CAIN457ADE11C) in 65 subjects and the phase 3 study (R12301) in 294 subjects. In the phase 2 study CAIN457ADE11C, 59.3% of patients in the secukinumab group vs. 8.0% in the placebo group were in sustained remission until Week 52. However, the phase 3 study R12301 did not show significant benefit by secukinumab compared to placebo. The protocol-defined duration of the glucocorticoid (GC) tapering schedule in Study R12301 was different from Study C22301. In study R12301, GC was tapered within 24 weeks in both secukinumab arms (150 mg and 300 mg) but continued in the placebo arm up to Week 52, which may have contributed to the negative result. In essence, study R12301 indicated lack of efficacy of a regimen including secukinumab for 52 weeks combined with 24 weeks GC tapering followed by placebo to a regimen of 52 week GC tapering (combined with placebo) in patients with GCA. The proposed therapeutic indication in the current application, treatment of polymyalgia rheumatica in adults who have had an inadequate response to glucocorticoids or who experience a relapse during glucocorticoid taper, does not exclude PMR subjects with GCA. Consequently, the MAH was requested to discuss and propose how to appropriately reflect in the SmPC that efficacy has not been demonstrated in patients with GCA. The MAH added to the section 5.1 of the SmPC information that patients with a prior or current GCA were excluded from the study, which is deemed adequate.

## Study treatments in study C22301

## Treatments

The compared treatments included three arms: the secukinumab 300 mg and 150 mg dosing regimens (weekly dosing at baseline, Weeks 1, 2, 3, and 4, followed by maintenance dosing every 4 weeks, starting at Week 4) compared to a similarly administer placebo. Furthermore, all study arms included an open-label prednisone taper regimen starting from 10 mg or 15 mg, dose selected by the investigator based on pre-baseline GC doses/tapering and PMR disease activity. The GC dose was tapered down biweekly, with a maximum duration of GC treatment of 24 or 20 weeks depending on whether initial dose was 15 or 10 mg/day.

Notably, no dose-finding studies have been conducted to determine the appropriate dose of secukinumab for use in patients with PMR. The dosage of secukinumab in the phase 3 program is, according to the MAH, based on the phase 2 study CAIN457ADE11C, with a total of 65 participants, of whom 27 patients were randomised to receive secukinumab 300mg SC weekly from week 0 to 4 and then every 4 weeks from week 4 to week 48 and 25 to received corresponding placebo injection SC with co-administered prednisolone to all patients in a tapered regimen from their baseline dose (25 mg to 60 mg) to 1 mg at Week 26 (last dose of prednisolone, 0 mg from Week 27 onwards). Since the phase 2 study included newly diagnosed subjects with GCA, the starting dose of prednisolone tapering was higher than in the prednisone regimen in the currently assessed study C22301 which included PMR subjects who had relapse while attempting to taper prednisone at a dose that is ≥5 mg/day, also taking in account that prednisolone and prednisone have similar therapeutic potency. Consequently, the posology in study C22301 was based on a post-hoc analysis from a small number of patients, with addition of the 150 mg secukinumab arm on recommendation from the CHMP.

In standard treatment of PMR, in real-world clinical practice, patients do not follow a pre-specified prednisone tapering schedule; rather, the dose is reduced if and when the patient's clinical condition appears to allow it. Response to GC treatment is typically very quick, with improvement in symptoms within days from initiation of treatment. However, rapid tapering of treatment is associated with a risk of relapse, therefore, once a patient is in remission, the dose of GCs is tapered gradually, with the goal being 10 mg of prednisone equivalent 4 to 8 weeks after the start of treatment and discontinuation as a minimum only after 1 year, provided relapse does not occur. In the event of relapse, GC must be reinitiated with the starting dose, hence, the overall GC exposure is increased. The C22301 study design addressed the question of whether secukinumab - relative to placebo - increased the likelihood of successful management of PMR flares in situations when a fast GC tapering (of ≤6 months) is preferable or when GC alone has not been sufficiently efficient for managing PMR.

The MAH justified tapering of the prednisone within 20 or 24 weeks (in the 10 mg and 15 mg startdose regimens), instead of the slower regimen recommended by treatment guidelines, by avoiding prolonged GC toxicity when GC-sparing therapy is available. The MAH discussed that comparing secukinumab 300 mg and 150 mg SC with the same 20/24-week prednisone taper in the placebo arm allowed for meaningful assessment of secukinumab efficacy in PMR while providing an optimal balance between symptom control and cumulative GC exposure in an elderly patient population. The tapering schedule was approved by the FDA during End-of-Phase 2 Meeting with the MAH on 7 June 2022. Contrary to FDA, as mentioned earlier, the CHMP strongly recommended a GC tapering schedule of 52 weeks during scientific advice, which would also have been in line with international treatment recommendations (see also below).

## Methods of assessment of efficacy

## Sustained remission at Week 52

The applicant refers to the predefined primary endpoint as sustained remission at Week 52. Sustained remission was defined as remission at Week 12 sustained up to Week 52 without recurrence of signs or symptoms of PMR or new diagnosis of GCA requiring escape or rescue treatment. The PMR signs and symptoms (bilateral girdle and shoulder pain, and constitutional and other symptoms) were monitored and recorded weekly to support prednisone tapering until Week 24. From Week 24 to 76, PMR assessment was conducted every 4 weeks. Eventually, the determinant of primary efficacy assessment was whether the PMR signs and symptoms, in investigator's judgment, trigger a change to management of PMR or newly diagnosed GCA and subsequently a deviation from the planned GC tapering regimen.

The protocol defined two distinct ways of managing the situations where PMR signs and symptoms trigger a change in PMR management: if it was chosen to only escalate GC dose while continuing the study treatment, this was referred to as escape treatment. If study treatment was discontinued and other treatment - possibly including increased GC dose or immunomodulating agents otherwise prohibited by the protocol - as per physician's discretion, were chosen, this was referred to as rescue treatment. Putting aside the study-specific nomenclature, both actions imply a failure to comply with the prednisone taper regimen as a result of a failure to reach or sustain adequate disease control ('remission'). As such, the primary endpoint is simple and clinically relevant but virtually complex given the lengthy study-specific nomenclature and definitions.

## Complete sustained remission at Week 52

Inflammatory markers such as CRP and ESR play an important role for the initial diagnosis of PMR and need to be elevated as per ACR/EULAR provisional classification criteria to establish the diagnosis. However, their value for monitoring the course of disease and detection of relapses in PMR is according to the MAH less established. Therefore, the MAH did not include ESR and CRP to the definition of the primary endpoint. However, the secondary endpoint of complete sustained remission at Week 52 included sustained remission (as defined for the primary endpoint) and no elevation of ESR to ≥ 40 mm/hr at ≥ 2 consecutive scheduled visits from Week 12 to Week 52 and normal hsCRP (&lt; 10 mg/L) without elevation to ≥ 10 mg/L at ≥ 2 consecutive scheduled visits from Week 12 to Week 52.

## Handling of intercurrent events for sustained remission endpoints

The predefined primary estimands for sustained remission and complete sustained remission at Week 52 considered intercurrent events of treatment discontinuation and use of prohibited medication before Week 52 as treatment failure. Supplementary analyses that consider sustained remission and complete sustained remission at Week 52 irrespective of whether secukinumab/placebo was discontinued or prohibited treatment used (referred to as treatment policy strategy for those intercurrent events, and endorsed by the CHMP in the prior scientific advice as the primary estimand, in accordance with the MAH's plan at that time) were also provided.

## Glucocorticoid use

As per CHMP's request, evaluation GC sparing effect was determined based on analyses of total cumulative GC dose through Week 52 under a treatment policy strategy, summing up GC taper, escape and rescue treatments and the use of prohibited medications. For missing data due to study dropout, or other reasons, multiple imputation under a copy-increment-reference assumption was implemented using 4-week analysis intervals for cumulative GC dose. Despite the concerns whether this analysis fully accounted for dropout (leading to partially observed and complete missing analysis intervals), the impact on the statistical comparison is likely to be minor and not to favour secukinumab arms.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## Time to first use of escape or rescue treatment

As noted earlier, the primary endpoint was essentially determined by whether, based on PMR signs and symptoms or newly diagnosed CGA, a deviation to the preplanned GC tapering was required. Hence, the analysis of time to first use of escape or rescue treatment offers complementary information on the time-course of these failures of GC tapering. Here, use of prohibited medications is considered as an event also, should it happen before entering escape or rescue treatment.

In the analysis of time to first use of escape or rescue treatment, data were considered for this analysis irrespective of whether study treatment was continued or not. This is acceptable.

## FACIT-Fatigue and HAQ-DI

The PROs were primarily analysed using a composite strategy, whereby failure to comply with the planned GC tapering was assumed to imply that participant's fatigue and functionality, respectively, at Week 52 would be as poor as the worst respective scores amongst all participants at baseline. Under this strategy, the result is driven by whether or not GC tapering regimen could be followed and, thus, ceases to measure intended HRQoL. Since signs and symptoms of PMR and the associated GC use are well covered by the endpoints discussed above, any results of FACIT-Fatigue and HAQ-DI under the composite strategy can be considered as complementary only. This said, supplementary analyses based on data (predominantly) as observed, were also provided for these PRO endpoints and are included in this assessment report.

## Statistical methods

The statistical methods involving estimation of marginal probabilities of response (as predicted from logistic regression model) and ANCOVA were, in general, fit for purpose. Also, the statistical methods for time to first use of escape or rescue treatment, i.e., Cox proportional hazards and logrank test, appear appropriate provided that potentially informative censoring is limited.

As discussed above, methodological questions were related to the data imputation rules triggered by predefined intercurrent events and protocol deviations. The predefined estimands and analysis adopted a so-called composite strategy according to which the endpoint data after the intercurrent event were to be censored and replaced with single-imputed imaginary data based on a predefined algorithms as defined for a respective endpoint. The MAH clarified that results selected for this assessment report were principally based on supplementary analyses using actual data rather than those imputed based on composite strategies. This is acknowledged. Formally, type I error is adequately controlled to the level of 5% (2-sided) using a hierarchical (fixed-sequence) testing strategy across multiple secondary endpoints and two dose levels of secukinumab. This is acceptable.

## Efficacy results and additional analyses

In the secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg and placebo arms of the study, respectively, 104/127 (81.9%), 99/127 (78.0) and 86/127 (67.7) subjects completed the 52-week treatment period. The most common reason for treatment discontinuation in all treatment arms was unsatisfactory therapeutic effect (secukinumab 300 mg: 6.3%; secukinumab 150 mg: 10.2%; placebo group: 19.7%). Hence, treatment discontinuation due to unsatisfactory therapeutic effect was more than 3-fold higher in the placebo arm than in the secukinumab 300 mg arm, and almost 2-fold higher than in the secukinumab 150 mg arm.

## Sustained remission at Week 52

Study C22301 met its primary objective, demonstrating a statistically significant increase in the probability of meeting sustained remission at Week 52 with the 300 mg dose of secukinumab vs. placebo: in the secukinumab 300 mg group, 52/126 (41.3%) subjects achieved sustained remission vs. 26/127 (20.5%) in the placebo group, with an estimated mean of 41.2% vs. 20.4% in the secukinumab 300mg vs. placebo group and marginal difference of 20.8 (95% CI [10.0, 31.7], p=0.0002; SmPC section 5.1).

Also, the secondary objective of sustained remission at Week 52 for secukinumab 150 mg vs. placebo was met: the proportion of participants achieving sustained remission at Week 52 was significantly higher (adj. p=0.0003) for the secukinumab 150 mg than for the placebo group (40.6% vs. 20.4%), with a clinically meaningful difference (20.2%).

The observed difference between the secukinumab arms vs. placebo arm of the study was due to a greater number of subjects achieving remission at Week 12 and no recurrence of signs or symptoms, attributable to PMR, that would have required escape or rescue from Week 12 to Week 52. There were no new diagnoses of GCA that required escape or rescue treatment among study subjects between Weeks 12 and 52. There was only one new case of GCA during the study, prior to Week 12 in the placebo group.

The estimated rates of primary endpoint 41.3% and 40.5%, in secukinumab 300 mg and 150 mg, respectively, vs. 20.5 % in placebo, demonstrate that concomitant use of secukinumab, at either dose, improves the likelihood of successful PMR management by glucocorticoid tapering regimen of 28/24 weeks.

The proportion of subjects who discontinued from study treatment was 22/127 (17.3%), 27/127 (21.3%) and 41/127 (32.3%) in the secukinumab 300 mg, 150 mg and placebo arms of the study, respectively.

An analysis based on a supplementary estimand (where treatment discontinuation and use of prohibited medication were handled using a treatment policy strategy) produced results consistent with the primary analysis: the difference between secukinumab 300 mg and placebo was 19.9% (p=0.0004) and the difference between secukinumab 150 mg and placebo was 22.6%, (p&lt;0.0001). Hence, the handling of these ICEs has a small concrete impact in the results, therefore, the methodological question about whether study treatment discontinuation should in itself be considered a non-response was not pursued further.

The primary analysis findings regarding sustained remission at Week 52 were further assessed for both 300 mg and 150 mg secukinumab dose using a tipping point analysis, which did not identify any tipping point.

Only a minority of subjects in any treatment group achieved complete sustained remission: 35/126 (27.8%), 31/126 (24.6%) and 6/127 (4.7%) in the secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg and placebo groups, respectively (SmPC section 5.1). However, the differences between the secukinumab 300 mg and placebo group as well as between the secukinumab 150 mg and placebo group were highly statistically significant and in favour of secukinumab.

As described above, there was no meaningful difference in achieving the primary endpoint or key secondary endpoints between the secukinumab arms of the study C22301. In the absence of demonstrated additional efficacy benefit of the 300 mg dose over the 150 mg dose in the primary endpoint or key secondary endpoints in study C22301, the MAH was requested to justify the approval of the 300 mg dose by identifying the patient population for whom a 300 mg dose would be preferred over 150 mg. Furthermore, the MAH was requested to propose criteria for treatment duration and discontinuation based on obtained data and recommend how long the treatment should be continued when there is response, when the treatment could be discontinued in case of no clinical benefit, and when/if escalation of dose from 150 mg to 300 mg could be considered. In response the MAH provided the SmPC section 4.2 wording of recommendations for the initial and maintenance dosing, together with advice when to increase the maintenance dose: \"The recommended dose is 150 mg by subcutaneous injection with initial dosing at weeks 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4, followed by monthly maintenance dosing, in combination with a tapering course of glucocorticoids, after which secukinumab can be continued as monotherapy. Each 150 mg dose is given as one subcutaneous injection of 150 mg. Based on clinical response, the maintenance dose can be increased to 300 mg. Each 300 mg dose is given as one subcutaneous injection of 300 mg or as two subcutaneous injections of 150 mg. Data are available in patients that were treated for up to 1 year. Therefore, treatment beyond 52 weeks should be guided by disease activity, physician discretion and patient choice.\"

The MAH submitted post-hoc analyses of the PMR subpopulation in study R12301 conducted in GCA subjects (i.e., subjects with GCA + PMR) demonstrating numerically better results for the secukinumab 300 mg + 26-week prednisone taper (n=57) vs. placebo + 52-week prednisone taper (n=48). No benefit in PMR+GCA patients was seen for the secukinumab 150 mg + 26-week prednisone taper (n=39) vs. comparator group. Results of study R12301 did not indicate a safety risk of secukinumab for patients with GCA that would differ from the known safety of secukinumab; and the observed safety in secukinumab-treated patients with GCA was no different from the comparator arm of 52week prednisone taper either. The MAH proposed addition to Section 5.1 of the SmPC to specify that the Phase 3 study enrolled adult patients with PMR excluding those with evidence or history of GCA, which is deemed adequate. In absence of a specific risk related to use of secukinumab in patients with PMR and GCA that would warrant precautions, and as no claim for efficacy in GCA is sought, it can be agreed that no addition in Section 4.4 is needed. Furthermore, the relationship between PMR and GCA remains a matter of scientific debate, with some considering them part of a disease continuum and others viewing them as distinct clinical entities.

Interestingly, analyses for sustained remission at week 52 by the stratification factor of baseline prednisone starting dose (10 mg/day vs. 15 mg/day) demonstrated a larger difference between secukinumab vs. placebo in subjects starting the prednisone taper regimen from the larger 15 mg dose vs. 10 mg dose. The MAH was requested to discuss the efficacy in both prednisone regimen subgroups based on characteristics of subjects in each subgroup to investigate if data implicate that a recommendation should be given in the SmPC for using a larger dose or longer duration of prednisone as adjunct to secukinumab when treating PMR. The MAH submitted post-hoc analyses on the non-randomised groups using different prednisone tapering courses. Based on the results it is not possible to draw conclusions regarding optimal dosage of GCs combined with secukinumab. The group starting with 10 mg dose vs. 15 mg dose had paradoxically better results on efficacy. At baseline, the hsCRP levels were lower in the 10 mg prednisone taper group, hence, it is possible that the obtained results reflected lower baseline activity of PMR in the 10

mg group, even though the groups were similar in other characteristics. The MAH did not include information on GC dose used by study subjects immediately prior to initiation of the study. ## Cumulative glucocorticoid dose

The estimated mean adjusted annual cumulative glucocorticoid dose through Week 52 was 1501.2 mg, 1473.1 mg and 1875.5 mg in the secukinumab 150 mg, 300 mg and placebo groups, respectively, indicating statistically significant GC sparing by 374.24 mg or 402.21 mg over a year, when secukinumab 150 mg or 300 mg, respectively, was used instead of placebo. Based on the observed data (not accounting for dropouts), the overall median total cumulative GC dose in the study groups was 1155.5 mg in each secukinumab group and 1613 mg in the placebo group. This difference was explained by the higher proportion of participants needing escape or rescue treatment in the placebo group. Escape treatment was used by 61/126 (48.4%), 58/126 (46.0%) and 90/127 (70.9%) patients, and rescue treatment was used by 6/127 (4.8%), 10/127 (7.9%) and 17/127 (13.4%) patients in the secukinumab 300 mg, 150 mg and placebo groups, respectively.

Use of other rescue treatments than GS was uncommon. In total, 31/379 (8.2%) subjects used such rescue medications, of whom 17/127 (13.4%) in the placebo group. The medications used for rescue included adalimumab, golimumab, hydroxychloroquine, leflunomide, methotrexate, sarilumab, secukinumab and tocilizumab, of which most common were methotrexate (n=10) and tocilizumab (n=12). The infrequent use of these medications is not considered to relevantly affect the results of the study.

## Time to first use of escape treatment or rescue treatment

The Kaplan-Meier curves in SmPC section 5.1 reflect the course of GC tapering failure: roughly during the first 8-10 weeks, on relatively high GC doses, few patients failed to taper GC as planned, followed by a period of higher failure rate until, beyond 24 months, roughly 60% of participants in the secukinumab arms and roughly 35% of participants in the placebo arm remained in the follow-up without GC, other rescue treatment or prohibited medications. No information about the reasons of censoring was included in the CSR, but the number of censored participants was low and raised no concerns. Formally, the hazard ratios (95% CI) and logrank tests [0.5 (0.4, 0.7), p&lt;0.0001 and 0.5 (0.4, 0.7), p&lt;0.0001] for secukinumab 300 mg and secukinumab 150 mg, respectively, vs. placebo demonstrated the effect of secukinumab.

The median time to first use of escape treatment or rescue treatment through Week 52 was 157 days for placebo vs. 337 days for secukinumab 300 mg and 282 days for secukinumab 150 mg. Regarding the virtual difference in the estimated medians between the secukinumab doses, the stepwise nature of the Kaplan-Meier curves and nearly identical confidence intervals should be noted.

The analysis discussed above maintains the data handling rule, specified in the SAP, according to which participants whose treatment assignment was unblinded before Week 52 were considered as events. Upon closer inspection of the data, all the unblinding cases were preceded by escape or rescue medication (events) or, in one case, by death (censored) and, hence, this controversial data handling rule did not impact the result.

## 2.4.4. Conclusions on the clinical efficacy

Results of the phase 3 trial C22301 demonstrated significantly better efficacy of secukinumab compared to placebo in the treatment of relapsed polymyalgia rheumatica, when combined with a prednisone tapering regimen up to 20 or 24 weeks of treatment. A larger proportion of subjects achieved sustained remission of PMR at Week 52. Consequently, the cumulative glucocorticoid dose was smaller with secukinumab compared to placebo.

The application for secukinumab in the treatment of polymyalgia rheumatica is approvable from the efficacy perspective.

## 2.5. Clinical safety

## Introduction

## Known safety profile

The most frequently reported adverse reactions for Cosentyx are upper respiratory tract infections (17.1%) (most frequently nasopharyngitis, rhinitis).

Over 20 000 patients have been treated with secukinumab in blinded and open label clinical studies in various indications (plaque psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, axial spondyloarthritis, hidradenitis suppurativa [HS] and other autoimmune conditions), representing 34 908 patient years of exposure. Of these, over 14 000 patients were exposed to secukinumab for at least one year. The safety profile of secukinumab is consistent across all indications.

## Scope of variation

With the intention to add the indication of polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR), key safety data are provided from a randomised, parallel-group, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase III study (CAIN457C22301, also known as REPLENISH, hereafter referred to as Study C22301), in which adult patients with PMR who had recently relapsed during glucocorticoids (GC) taper were treated with secukinumab 300 mg or secukinumab 150 mg or placebo administered s.c. for 52 weeks, each in combination with prednisone tapered in a protocol-defined regimen over 20 or 24 weeks.

Additionally, supportive evidence of safety is provided from a separate Phase III study for giant cell arteritis (GCA) (Study CAIN457R12301, also known as GCAptAIN, hereafter referred to as Study R12301), a condition with similar demographics, similar comorbidities and long-term GC use.

Safety data from Studies C22301 and R12301 were not pooled due to differences in study design and durations of glucocorticoid exposure between the placebo arms of the 2 studies (20 or 24 weeks in Study C22301 vs. 52 weeks in Study R12301).

A detailed overview of Study C22301 is provided in section 2.4 of this AR. Study R12301 is briefly described in Table 1 and Table 26.

The terms 'Cosentyx', 'AIN457' and 'secukinumab' are used interchangeably in this document.

Secukinumab 150 mg arm; PT Meningitis cryptococcal: A patient with pre-existing condition of benign meningioma (diagnosed in 2021) had multiple medical conditions including ongoing skin ulcers (started about a month prior to the screening visit). ## Adverse drug reactions in the target indication

ADR candidates consisted of pre-qualified ADRs, and AEs identified through a numerical screening rule, i.e., all PTs for which a risk difference between secukinumab 300 mg or secukinumab 150 mg and placebo was &gt; 2% during the 52-week placebo-controlled period of Study C22301.

Events identified as ADR candidates were medically assessed for clinical meaningfulness and causal association with secukinumab based on confounding factors.

Additionally, in order to update the current list of ADRs for the secukinumab label, pooling was performed using the safety data from the placebo-controlled period of secukinumab pivotal studies in multiple approved indications (PsO, PsA, AS, nr-axSpA, and HS) and the target indication for this submission (PMR).

Upon a thorough medical review, 'urinary tract infections' was identified as a new ADR in PMR patients.

Newly identified ADR 'urinary tract infections (CMQ)' for PMR indication was reported with a frequency of 'very common': 13.5% (17 patients) for secukinumab 300 mg, 14.3% (18 patients) for secukinumab 150 mg, and 7.1% (9 patients) for placebo.

With the addition of safety data from PMR Study C22301 to the previous safety data pool used in the approved SmPC, the frequency categories have changed for the following ADRs in the safety pool due to the increased sample size: Rhinorrhoea - changed from 'common' to 'uncommon', Urticaria - change from 'uncommon' to 'common'.

## Immunogenicity

In Study C22301, at baseline, 2 patients each in the secukinumab 300 mg arm and 150 mg arm tested ADA-positive. These patients were thereafter ADA-negative.

The overall incidence of treatment-emergent ADAs was 1.2% (3 of 245 patients: 2 patients in the secukinumab 300 mg arm and 1 patient in the secukinumab 150 mg arm). Of these patients, one possible immunogenicity-related AE (PT dermatitis) occurred in 1 patient in the secukinumab 300 mg arm. The AE was non-serious with onset date on Day 107, about 3 weeks after the first treatmentemergent ADA at Week 12; the event was mild and was considered as related to study treatment by the Investigator. It did not lead to discontinuation or interruption of study treatment and was ongoing at the time of the last available report. There were no patients with neutralising ADAs.

## 2.5.1. Discussion on clinical safety

With this variation application the MAH has introduced a new indication, polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR). Key safety data were provided from a Phase 3 study (Study C22301), in which adult patients with PMR who had recently relapsed during glucocorticoids (GC) taper were treated with secukinumab 300 mg or secukinumab 150 mg or placebo administered s.c. for 52 weeks, each in combination with prednisone tapered in a protocol-defined regimen over 24 weeks.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

Additionally, supportive evidence of safety was provided from a separate Phase 3 study for giant cell arteritis (GCA) (Study R12301), a condition with similar demographics, similar comorbidities and longterm GC use.

In patients with PMR, treatment with secukinumab (300 mg or 150 mg) in combination with a 24-week prednisone taper demonstrated a safety profile generally consistent with the established safety profile of secukinumab across approved indications.

In this study, PMR patients experienced higher rates of AEs and infections compared to those observed in previously approved indications. However, higher rates were also observed among placebo-treated participants. Infections were observed in 61.1% and 57.9% of PMR patients treated with secukinumab 150 mg and 300 mg, respectively, compared with 53.5% of patients treated with placebo (SmPC section 4.8).

The elevated incidence of AEs, especially regarding infections, is consistent with the demographic profile of PMR patients, who are mainly older adults undergoing long-term glucocorticoid therapy.

Based on data from Study C22301, 'Urinary tract infection' was identified as a new ADR in patients with PMR, with a frequency of 11.1% with secukinumab compared to 5.5% with placebo. This is appropriately reflected in the ADR table in SmPC section 4.8 with a frequency 'very common'.

Also previously recognised ADRs such as nasopharyngitis, nausea, herpes zoster and several other infections were more frequently observed with secukinumab than with placebo, as expected. However, no change in frequency for these ADRs was considered needed in the SmPC.

The frequencies of 'back pain', 'myalgia' and 'abdominal pain' were also higher in the secukinumab groups compared to placebo but these are not previously recognised as ADRs. After a thorough evaluation of causality, applying the Bradford Hill criteria and patient-level clinical review, these events are not considered causally related to the study drug.

A lower proportion of patients experienced AEs leading to discontinuation of study treatment in both secukinumab arms (3.2% for each secukinumab arm and 'Any secukinumab' group) compared with the placebo arm (6.3%).

In the two clinical studies provided for safety analysis, 7 deaths occurred, 2 of which were with placebo. Narratives were provided and showed that of the 5 deaths with secukinumab, 4 were related to infectious diseases. An elevated risk for infections is known. As the subjects were elderly with several comorbidities, the deaths are not considered unexpected. All the fatal events were assessed as not related by the applicant. This is acknowledged.

Among the safety topics of interest, infections and hypersensitivity events occurred at a higher rate in both secukinumab arms compared with the placebo arm, as expected. However, incidence of serious infections was comparable between secukinumab-treated patients and placebo-treated patients, and no serious hypersensitivity events occurred. MACE was reported in one secukinumab-treated patient (150 mg arm) with various comorbidities. Neutropenia was reported in one secukinumab-treated patient (150 mg arm) with no concurrent infection (section 4.8 of the SmPC). Eight malignancies were reported across the two secukinumab treatment arms and 2 with placebo; all were solid tumours which usually have a long latency period and are therefore unlikely to be related to study treatment as time to onset for malignant or unspecified tumours was between 9 and 390 days. No events of IBD or hepatitis B reactivation were reported.

Across all subgroups, the AE profile was generally consistent with that of the overall population. None of the subgroups treated with secukinumab showed an increased risk of total AEs. Although some numerical variations in specific event types were observed within subgroups, these differences were not considered clinically meaningful.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

No dose-dependent trends were observed between secukinumab 300 mg and 150 mg for overall AEs, SAEs, AEs leading to treatment discontinuation, most frequently reported SOCs and also in various safety topics of interest. This was also true for patients in the highest age group (&gt;75 years) and in patients weighing &lt;70kg, indicating that there is no identified risk associated with a dose increase to 300 mg in any subpopulation. The observed differences were small and inconsistent across groups, suggesting random numerical variation rather than a true dose effect.

Looking specifically at infections, in patients aged 50-&lt;65 years, both doses were associated with lower infection rates than placebo. In patients aged ≥75 years, infection rates were higher with both AIN457 doses compared with placebo, with a slightly lower rate of infections with the higher dose, i.e. no consistent separation between 150 mg and 300 mg was found.

In contrast, in patients aged ≥75 years, the higher frequency of infections with Any AIN457 compared with placebo is unlikely to be attributable solely to placebo imbalance, given the magnitude of the difference, which also translated into a difference in serious infections.

To conclude, the rate of infections and serious infections increased with age. The difference to placebo also increased with age but no dose-interaction was observed.

## 3. Benefit-Risk Balance

## 3.1. Therapeutic Context

## 3.1.1. Disease or condition

Polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) is a chronic inflammatory disease that affects persons older than 50 years of age. It is the second most common inflammatory rheumatic disease in older persons after rheumatoid arthritis. Approximately 75% of patients with PMR are women, and the lifetime risk of PMR is 2.4% among women and 1.7% among men. PMR does not appear to affect life expectancy but may profoundly affect quality of life.

PMR causes pain and stiffness typically located at the shoulders, neck, and pelvis. Shoulder pain may radiate distally toward the elbows, and pelvic-girdle pain often extends to the groin and lateral aspects of the hips and frequently radiates to the posterior thighs and knees. The symptoms are typically worse in the morning and after physical inactivity. Peripheral-joint involvement is present in approximately one in four of patients, presenting as nonerosive arthritis or tenosynovitis, which typically affects the knees or wrists. Systemic symptoms such as low-grade fever, profound fatigue, and weight loss may be present in approximately 40% of patients. Diffuse swelling of the distal extremities, mainly the hands but occasionally also the feet, occurs in approximately 12% of patients. The ESR, CRP level, or both are elevated in almost all patients; normal values for these acute-phase reactants render the diagnosis unlikely.

There is no specific test for PMR. The diagnosis is clinical, so substantial uncertainty remains in daily practice. Differential diagnoses to be considered include inflammatory arthritis, a noninflammatory musculoskeletal disorder, vasculitis, infection, and cancer.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

PMR and giant-cell arteritis (GCA) are closely related and usually regarded as a disease spectrum. Features of PMR occur in approximately 42% of patients with GCA, and 3 to 46% of patients with PMR have evidence of large-vessel inflammation on imaging. The relevance of large-vessel vasculitis in clinically isolated polymyalgia rheumatica is as yet uncertain, and patients are usually treated as having polymyalgia rheumatica. GCA, if untreated, progresses to involve the aorta and its collateral branches, leading to various complications. Late diagnosis and treatment of GCA can have serious consequences, including irreversible loss of visual function.

## 3.1.2. Available therapies and unmet medical need

Glucocorticoids (GC) are the cornerstone of treatment for PMR. Response is usually very rapid and the patient experiences alleviation of symptoms within days of onset of GC treatment.

Although GCs can markedly decrease the often severe and disabling pain and stiffness, GC-related side effects are a major long-term burden. More than 40% of patients have at least one relapse. Rapid dose reduction is associated with a higher risk of relapse than gradual tapering. Relapses are usually treated with an incremental increase in the prednisone dose. Therefore, each relapse prolongs the duration of GC treatment. A meta-analysis showed that 51% of patients are still taking glucocorticoids (commonly prednisone at a dose of 2.5 to 5 mg per day) 2 years after initiating treatment, and 25% receive glucocorticoids for 5 years or longer.

In the EU, the indications for use of Kevzara (sarilumab) was extended in 2024 to the treatment of PMR in adult patients who have had an inadequate response to corticosteroids or who experience a relapse during corticosteroid taper. The ACR and EULAR recommendations for managing PMR from year 2015 represent the current standard of treatment. These guidelines suggest oral GCs as first-line treatment and consideration of methotrexate (MTX) for selected patients (even though in the EU, MTX is not authorised for this therapeutic indication).

## 3.1.3. Main clinical studies

The MAH is conducting one clinical trial in PMR subjects. The study is ongoing with the extension period; however Week 52 data (end of treatment period) are presented in the CSR submitted with the application. The study C22301 is a randomised, parallel-group, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre Phase 3 trial to evaluate efficacy and safety of secukinumab administered SC versus placebo, in combination with a GC taper regimen, in patients with relapsed PMR. The subjects were required to be at least 50 years of age, have a diagnosis of PMR and a history of being treated for at least 8 consecutive weeks with prednisone (≥ 10 mg/day, or equivalent dose of another GC) at any time prior to screening. Participants must have had at least one episode of PMR relapse while attempting to taper prednisone at a dose that is ≥ 5 mg/day. The subjects were required to be on prednisone of at least 7.5 mg/day (or equivalent) and not exceeding 25 mg/day at screening and during the screening period. Patients with evidence or history of GCA as indicated by typical (cranial) symptoms were excluded.

The study subjects (N=379) were randomised in a 1:1:1 ratio to 3 arms (secukinumab 300 mg s.c., secukinumab 150 mg s.c., or placebo s.c.), each in conjunction with an open-label GC taper regimen. The GC taper could start from 15 mg/day and continue to end of Week 24 or start from 10 mg/day and continue to end of Week 20 from baseline. Investigators chose the GC regimen based on the GC dose used by the patient prior to baseline and the activity of the disease.

The primary analysis was conducted after all participants completed 52 weeks of treatment or discontinued earlier. All 379 participants in the full analysis data set were assessed for primary and secondary endpoints. The primary endpoint of the study was sustained remission at Week 52, defined as a participant meeting all of the following: achieved remission at Week 12 that was sustained from Week 12 to Week 52; no recurrence of signs or symptoms, attributable to PMR, that requires escape treatment or rescue treatment; no new diagnosis of GCA, that requires escape treatment or rescue treatment. Remission at Week 12 was defined as a participant meeting all of the following at Week 12: no use of escape treatment or rescue treatment prior to Week 12; no signs or symptoms attributable to PMR, that requires escape treatment or use of rescue treatment, at Week 12; and no new diagnosis of GCA, that requires escape treatment or rescue treatment, at Week 12.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## 3.2. Favourable effects

The study met its primary objective, demonstrating the superiority of secukinumab 300 mg plus a prednisone taper regimen with a duration of 20 or 24 weeks over placebo plus the same prednisone taper. The proportion of participants achieving sustained remission at Week 52 was 41.3% in the secukinumab 300 mg (p=0.0002 vs. placebo), 40.5% in the secukinumab 150 mg (p=0.0003 vs. placebo) and 20.5% in the placebo group.

At Week 12, during the ongoing prednisone taper, a majority of patients had achieved remission; 88.9%, 85.7% and 78.0% in the secukinumab 300mg, secukinumab 150 mg and placebo groups, respectively.

The difference was also demonstrated between secukinumab and placebo in the proportion of participants achieving complete sustained remission, which included, in addition to sustained remission requirements, also normalisation of CRP (without elevation to ≥10 mg/L) and no elevation of ESR ≥40 mm/hr for ≥2 consecutive visits from Week 12 to Week 52. Both secukinumab 300 mg and 150 mg demonstrated higher response rates compared to placebo across individual components of sustained/complete sustained remission. Additional analyses including tipping point analysis, and supplementary estimand analysis results were consistent, demonstrating the robustness of the primary estimand analysis result.

A higher proportion of participants from the placebo arm (75.6%) required escape and/or rescue treatment compared to either of the secukinumab arms (50.8% and 51.6% for the 300mg and 150 mg arms, respectively). There were 61/126, 58/126 and 90/127 participants who started escape treatment in the secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg and placebo groups, respectively. Respective numbers of participants starting rescue treatment were 6/126, 10/126 and 17/127 in the secukinumab 300 mg, secukinumab 150 mg and placebo groups. Due to the difference in number of subjects starting escape or rescue medication for PMR, the cumulative dose of GC was lower in both secukinumab groups compared to the placebo group. Nevertheless, among subjects who needed escape or rescue treatment, the daily GC dose was in the same order of magnitude.

Subgroup analyses based on prednisone starting dose (10 mg/day vs. 15 mg/day) demonstrated numerically higher efficacy benefit of both secukinumab doses over placebo in both subgroups. Among participants who began the prespecified prednisone regimen at 15 mg/day, the results suggested a potentially greater benefit in achieving sustained remission with the 300 mg dose than with the 150 mg dose. Moreover, a larger difference in sustained remission at week 52 was observed between either secukinumab arm compared with the placebo arm in subjects starting the prednisone taper regimen from the larger 15 mg dose vs. 10 mg dose. Within the placebo group as well as within the secukinumab 150 mg group, the remission rate was higher in subjects assigned by the investigator to the prednisone tapering course started with 10 mg/day prednisone than in subjects assigned to the prednisone tapering course started with 15 mg/day (see below).

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## 3.3. Uncertainties and limitations about favourable effects

Although PMR and GCA are considered part of a disease spectrum, patients with current or previous GCA were excluded from the pivotal phase 3 PMR study, and efficacy was not demonstrated in the separate phase 3 GCA study. Consequently, the MAH was requested to appropriately reflect in the SmPC that efficacy in GCA has not been established. Post-hoc analyses of Study R12301 in patients with concomitant GCA and PMR showed a numerical benefit for secukinumab 300 mg, but not 150 mg, with no new safety concerns identified. It was therefore agreed to state in Section 5.1 that patients with current or previous GCA were excluded from the pivotal PMR study. In the absence of an identified specific safety risk, and as no efficacy claim in GCA is sought, inclusion of a warning in Section 4.4 is not considered warranted.

The MAH proposed in the SmPC the s.c. dose of 150 mg with initial administration at weeks 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4, followed by monthly maintenance, in combination with a tapering course of glucocorticoids. Additionally, the MAH claimed that for some patients, a s.c. dose of 300 mg may be beneficial. The CHMP requested the MAH to justify the approvability of the 300 mg dose given no meaningful difference in achieving the primary endpoint or key secondary endpoints between the secukinumab doses in the study C22301 was observed. Furthermore, the MAH was requested to provide criteria for treatment duration and discontinuation. The responses based on subgroup analyses showed that some patients appeared to gain benefit from the larger 300 mg dose vs. 150 dose of secukinumab, e.g. those aged 75 years and above. Furthermore, in the follow-up after cessation of treatment, the treatment effect of secukinumab decreased numerically more slowly over time in the 300 mg group compared with the 150 mg group. Of note, there was no difference in safety between the 300 mg and 150 mg dose. Consequently, dosing recommendations for the 300 mg dose to be used as up-titration based on clinical response have been included in the SmPC section 4.2. This amendment is regarded acceptable, although it was not specifically evaluated in the PMR clinical studies. Notably, the wording is consistent with the posology recommendations included in the SmPC for several other approved adult indications.

As mentioned above, within the placebo group as well as within the secukinumab 150 mg group, the remission rate was paradoxically higher in subjects assigned by the investigator to the prednisone tapering course starting with 10 mg/day prednisone than in subjects assigned to the prednisone tapering course starting with 15 mg/day. The MAH considered this result to be driven by baseline disease activity (baseline prednisone dose being surrogate of disease severity at baseline). Baseline hsCRP ≥10 mg/L was more frequent in the 15 mg than in the 10 mg prednisone taper group (30.4% vs 16.1%). Other assessed baseline characteristics, including PMR signs and symptoms, prior relapses and ESR, showed no consistent between-group differences. The investigator's selection of the taper regimen was informed, among other factors, by the baseline GC dose; however, pre-baseline GC doses were not reported in the original CSR or the post hoc analyses. The available parameters may not capture all factors which informed treatment selection, including symptom severity. Given the nonrandomised allocation to the prednisone taper regimens, the post hoc analyses do not support determination of an optimal GC dose in combination with secukinumab. Additional information on prebaseline GC exposure would have been unlikely to change this conclusion and was therefore not requested. The approved indication reflects adequately the recommended clinical use of the product leaving room for the discretion of the treating physician.

The MAH submitted also the 6-month treatment-free follow-up data showing that a significant proportion of secukinumab treated patients who were in remission at Week 52 relapsed after treatment discontinuation. Therefore, continuation of secukinumab beyond 1 year might be advisable; however, there are currently no available data to recommend discontinuation after any certain duration of treatment or to up-titrate at any particular timepoint. It is deemed appropriate that duration of treatment and potential up-titration remain in the remit of the treating physician as indicated in the SmPC section 4.2. The MAH clarified that long-term data of secukinumab treatment in PMR are expected from the ongoing open-label extension study CAIN457C22301E1 of study C22301. Subjects who completed the treatment period up to Week 52 of study C22301 and who experience a relapse during the treatment-free follow-up period are eligible to enter the extension study where available.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The scheduled tapering of the prednisone within 6 months differs from the guideline-based slower tapering of GC over a year. The chosen shorter GC tapering may favour the secukinumab groups over the placebo group. On the other hand, this setting provides a comparison to placebo after 24 weeks without concomitant GCs other than escape and rescue treatment providing reassurance on the efficacy of secukinumab.

There were methodological issues in the determination of cumulative annual GC dose. The results included in this AR, and proposed for the SmPC by the MAH, are based on supplementary analysis focusing on actual GC use (rather data imputed as per 'composite strategy'). Although some uncertainly remains around the way missing data due to participant dropout were addressed, the statistical results of the supplementary analysis are judged to be sufficiently robust.

## 3.4. Unfavourable effects

## 3.7. Benefit-risk assessment and discussion

## 3.7.1. Importance of favourable and unfavourable effects

Efficacy of secukinumab with either the dose of 300 mg SC administered weekly from study baseline until Week 4, and every 4 weeks until Week 48 or with the dose of 150 mg SC administered with the same schedule was superior to placebo, when combined with a prednisone taper regimen starting from 15 mg (duration of taper 24 weeks) or 10 mg (duration of taper 20 weeks). Less than half of subjects in either secukinumab group achieved sustained remission, hence, the efficacy is only moderate in this treatment-resistant group of patients with polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) who have not responded to GC treatment or have already prior to the study experienced at least one relapse during GC treatment. However, since only one fifth of subjects in the placebo group achieved sustained remission, there is a place in the treatment of PMR for secukinumab as a second-line option for PMR patients corresponding to the applied indication for use, adults who have had an inadequate response to glucocorticoids or who experience a relapse during glucocorticoid taper.

The MAH has also conducted two studies in patients with giant cell arteritis (GCA). The small phase 2 study yielded promising results, however, the phase 3 study did not meet the primary endpoint, therefore, secukinumab was not deemed superior to placebo in the treatment of GCA in combination with a prednisone taper regimen. The phase 3 study in PMR excluded patients with GCA, and only one patient in the placebo group had newly onset GCA during the study. Therefore, data on efficacy on GCA was not obtained by the study in PMR patients. Consequently, the MAH was requested to discuss and propose how to appropriately reflect in the SmPC that efficacy has not been demonstrated in patients with GCA. The MAH submitted post-hoc analyses from the GCA study regarding the PMR subpopulation (i.e., subjects with GCA + PMR). Efficacy results were numerically better in the GCA+PMR subgroup with the 300 mg secukinumab vs. placebo, and similar with the 150 mg vs. placebo, however, numbers in subjects in the groups were relatively small. Results of study R12301 did not indicate a safety risk of secukinumab for patients with GCA that would differ from the known

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

safety of secukinumab; and the observed safety in secukinumab-treated patients with GCA was no different from the comparator arm of 52-week prednisone taper either. The MAH proposed addition to Section 5.1 of the SmPC to specify that the Phase 3 study enrolled adult patients with PMR excluding those with evidence or history of GCA, which is acceptable. In absence of a specific risk related to use of secukinumab in patients with PMR + GCA that would warrant precautions, and as no claim for efficacy in GCA is sought, it can be agreed that no addition in Section 4.4 is warranted.

As Study C22301 did not demonstrate an additional efficacy benefit of secukinumab 300 mg over 150 mg for the primary or key secondary endpoints, the MAH was requested to justify the approvability of the 300 mg dose and to define the patient population in whom this dose would be preferred over 150 mg. The MAH was also requested to discuss whether the available data support criteria for treatment duration and discontinuation. In their response, the MAH submitted 6-month treatment-free follow-up data showing that the treatment effect decreased more slowly in subjects who had received the 300 mg dose vs. the 150 mg dose during the treatment period of the study. Therefore, continuation of secukinumab beyond 1 year might be advisable; however, it should be guided by disease activity, physician discretion and patient choice. The MAH also submitted subgroup analyses, albeit in small groups, based on which some patients appeared to gain benefit from the larger 300 mg dose vs. 150 dose of secukinumab, e.g., patients aged 75 years and above. No definitive recommendations on dose or timing of potential up-titration could be proposed emphasising further the need for considering the clinical response as described in the SmPC section 4.2. Although treatment beyond 52 weeks or up titration were not specifically evaluated in the PMR clinical studies, the wording is consistent with the posology recommendations included in the SmPC for several other approved adult indications. Of note, there was no difference in safety between the 300 mg and 150 mg dose.

Reduction in glucocorticoid (GC) toxicity is an important aspect in the treatment of PMR. Since the treatment typically continues at least for one year or even several years, patients develop multipleorgan adverse events from GCs. If GC treatment is tapered down too fast, this triggers relapses warranting a larger GC dose to be tapered down again, leading to increase in the burden from GC toxicity. In the current phase 3 study in PMR, a smaller proportion of subjects administered secukinumab needed escape or rescue treatment with GC, therefore, the total cumulative GC dose was lower in the secukinumab groups vs. placebo. However, in the patients who started escape or rescue treatment, the daily dose of GC was similar regardless of study group, as could be expected in the target population.

The safety profile of Cosentyx in the PMR population is generally consistent with the known safety profile of secukinumab across the various approved indications although incidences were somewhat higher in both secukinumab and placebo treated patients. The elevated incidence of AEs, especially regarding infections, is consistent with the demographic profile of PMR patients, who are mainly older adults undergoing long-term glucocorticoid therapy.

Urinary tract infection was identified as a new ADR. The increased susceptibility to infections is a known effect of secukinumab. Considering the age and gender distribution of the PMR population, it is not unexpected that this susceptibility is seen also as an increased risk of UTI. Most cases were nonserious and mild to moderate in severity.

The rate of infections and serious infections increased with age. The difference to placebo also increased with age but no dose-interaction was observed.

Overall, no dose-dependent trends were observed in terms of safety in PMR.

In summary, the efficacy of secukinumab in this indication has been demonstrated albeit it appears to be moderate. Treatment was associated with a reduction in glucocorticoid exposure of approximately

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

400 mg prednisone equivalent, which may be considered clinically meaningful in a chronic disease setting where minimisation of corticosteroid burden is an important treatment goal. Furthermore, the efficacy of secukinumab is also supported by the observation that, following treatment discontinuation, patients previously treated with secukinumab remained in remission for longer than those who had received placebo. The safety profiles of secukinumab and glucocorticoids also differ, which may be a factor to be taken in account when choosing medication for individual patients with different concomitant medications and medical history.

## 3.7.2. Balance of benefits and risks

Efficacy of secukinumab has been demonstrated in adult patients with polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR), who have had an inadequate response to glucocorticoids or who experience a relapse during glucocorticoid taper but who do not have/have a history of giant-cell arteritis (GCA). This efficacy was achieved with an acceptable safety profile, as Cosentyx was generally well tolerated. Therefore, the benefit-to-risk balance (B/R) is positive.

## 3.7.3. Additional considerations on the benefit-risk balance

None

## 3.8. Conclusions

The overall B/R balance of Cosentyx in the treatment of polymyalgia rheumatica in adults who have had an inadequate response to glucocorticoids or who experience a relapse during glucocorticoid taper is positive.
