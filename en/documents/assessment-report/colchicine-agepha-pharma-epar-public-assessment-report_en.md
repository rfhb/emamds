## 2. Scientific discussion

## 2.1. Introduction

The applicant submitted current dossier to meet the unmet needs of the patient with coronary artery disease (CAD) by providing a treatment option for secondary prevention of atherothrombotic events. The significant human and economic costs of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) have led the WHO to highlight the prevention and control of CVDs as an important pillar of a global action plan for prevention and control of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). The applicant proposed to register colchicine 0.5 mg tablets, for the following therapeutic indication: 'To reduce the risk of myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, coronary revascularization, and cardiovascular death in adult patients with established atherosclerotic disease or with multiple risk factors for cardiovascular disease'.

Colchicine is an anti-inflammatory medicinal product that is authorised with the following indications:

- treatment of acute gout in adults,
- prophylaxis of a gout attack in adults during initiation of urate-lowering therapy,

- in Familial Mediterranean Fever for prophylaxis of attacks and prevention of amyloidosis in adults and paediatric patients.

Recently, colchicine has been rediscovered for its potential use in the prevention of secondary cardiovascular events due to its anti-inflammatory properties. The key role of inflammation in the pathogenesis of CAD, has led to the exploration of anti-inflammatory agents for the prevention of secondary cardiovascular (CV) events.

Although the exact mechanism of action of colchicine in CV prevention is not well understood, there were number of explanations provided that are listed below.

The primary mechanism of action of colchicine is tubulin disruption, leading to subsequent downregulation of multiple inflammatory pathways and modulation of innate immunity.

Multiple pathways are responsible for inflammation in acute coronary syndromes (ACS). Monocytes/macrophages are key effector cells in atherosclerosis-associated inflammation, leading to both plaque progression and instability. Colchicine disrupts cytoskeletal functions through inhibition of β -tubulin polymerization into microtubules and consequently prevents the activation, degranulation and migration of neutrophils. Evidence suggests that colchicine may also interfere with the intracellular assembly of the inflammasome complex present in neutrophils and monocytes that mediates activation of interleukin-1 β ( Zhang 2022).

It has recently been shown that the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptors, pyrin domain-containing (NLRP3) inflammasome - a cytosolic protein complex that serves as sensor of either pathogen invasion or cellular stress promotes caspase-1 activation, and therefore the production of active interleukin IL-1b and IL-18, key mediators in atherosclerotic plaque development, progression, and destabilization. Furthermore, this inflammatory milieu leads to increased levels of IL-6 and Creactive protein (CRP), which could impact, it was postulated, with the incidence of future CV events in patients with atherosclerosis (Martinez 2015).

Colchicine inhibits the proinflammatory response to cholesterol crystals (that trigger the inflammatory response) by reducing macrophage expression of IL-1 β and the release of various cytokines, including tumour necrosis factor (TNF). It is reducing endothelial expression of selectins and inhibiting the actions of circulating leukocytes, including suppressing their ability to release lytic enzymes (e.g., matrix metalloproteinase and superoxide). In addition, it is postulated that colchicine impairs platelet leukocyte interactions that promote atherothrombosis, and it can promote resolution and healing by stimulating the expression of IL-10 and TGF-b and by limiting the growth of SMCs, fibroblasts, and osteophytes, which if unchecked lead to vascular thickening, deformity, and calcification (Nidorff et al 2019).

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The ongoing inflammation is thought to be responsible for residual CV risk. Unresolved inflammation was associated with monocyte influx into atherosclerotic plaques that results in impaired efferocytosis, necrotic core development, and a thin fibrous cap. Atherosclerotic plaque rich in lipids may harbour neutrophils whose function is inhibited by colchicine. Furthermore, proinflammatory cytokines that activate leukocytes and monocytes, which were associated with the advancement of atherosclerosis, are suppressed by colchicine (Webb 2020).

By targeting tubulin in neutrophils, colchicine also impairs their mobility, adhesion, and activation, further reducing their response to inflammatory signals. These effects of colchicine likely explain its ability to prevent the neutrophil-mediated inflammatory response to cholesterol crystals that was observed in earlier in-vivo studies (Nidorf et al 2014).

Following the assessment, the CHMP approved the following indication: 'Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma is indicated for secondary prevention of atherothrombotic events in adult patients with coronary disease stable for at least 6 months.'

## 2.3.2. Active substance

## 2.3.2.1. General Information

The chemical name of Colchicine is (S)-N-(5,6,7,9-tetrahydro-1,2,3,10-tetramethoxy-9oxobenzo[a]heptalen-7-yl)acetamide corresponding to the molecular formula C22H25NO6. It has a relative molecular mass of 399.4 g/mol and the following structure:

Figure 1: Active substance structure

<!-- image -->

The chemical structure of colchicine was elucidated by a combination of UV spectrophotometry, IR spectroscopy, 1H-NMR spectroscopy, 13C-NMR spectroscopy, and Mass spectroscopy.

Colchicine is a yellowish-white crystalline powder, slightly hygroscopic, very soluble in water, freely soluble in alcohol, practically insoluble in cyclohexane.

Colchicine contains only one asymmetric carbon atom (C-7). Natural colchicine (l-form) possesses the absolute configuration S for biogenetic reasons. However, due to the presence of a 'biaryl' system (rings A-C), two conformers [aR,7S (I) and aS,7S (II)] are possible for natural colchicine. H-NMR analysis indicates that natural colchicine is present in solution and in the crystalline state mainly as conformer I.

Colchicine does not exhibit polymorphism. Moreover, as the AS is dissolved in water during the wet granulation process the manufacturer does not consider those parameters relevant; this is acceptable.

As there is a monograph of colchicine in the European Pharmacopoeia, the manufacturer of the active substance has been granted a Certificate of Suitability of the European Pharmacopoeia (CEP) for Colchicine, which has been provided within the current Marketing Authorisation Application.

## 2.3.3. Finished medicinal product

## 2.3.3.1. Description of the product and Pharmaceutical development

The finished product (FP) is presented as are white to slightly yellow, round, biconvex tablets, debossed with 'L1' on one side and plain on the other side, (ca. 6.0 mm diameter and ca. 3.1 mm thickness.

The unit composition of the finished product has been provided. The finished product (FP) contains no overage or overfill.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The FP has been developed to be a generic equivalent to the EU reference medicinal product Colchicine Tiofarma 0.5 mg, tablets. Consequently, the objective was to prepare an immediate release tablets being essentially similar to the reference medicinal product. The proposed test and reference medicinal products are conventional immediate-release tablets and are interchangeable with each other.

AGEPHA Pharma currently holds national marketing authorisation of Colchicine tablets for other indications, and this product has been selected as a prototype formulation for the development of the proposed Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma 0.5 mg tablet.

The AS is very soluble in water, freely soluble in alcohol. The aqueous solubility of colchicine is independent of pH. Colchicine is available as crystalline powder, which does not exhibit polymorphism.

All excipients are well known pharmaceutical ingredients, and their quality is compliant with Ph. Eur. standards. There are no novel excipients used in the finished product formulation. The list of excipients is included in section 6.1 of the SmPC. Characteristics of the AS and function of the excipients in the final formulation were discussed and described.

AGEPHA's approved Colchicine tablets 0.372 mg was used as a prototype formulation for the development of Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma 0.5 mg tablets, and the same composition has been applied except for the increased quantity of colchicine. Colchicine tablets, 0.372 mg are manufactured by AGEPHA since 1951 and the product has a shelf life of 36 months. According to the presented position the change of drug strength from 0.372 to 0.5 mg should not impact the dissolution profile. Although, the compatibility is supported by the stability studies of the FP, the applicant also provided a compatibility study between the AS and the excipients, conducted at 40 °C / 75% RH and 25 °C / 65% RH under open and closed conditions for a period of four weeks. Based on the results submitted, the excipients tested are considered compatible with the colchicine AS.

In the proposed FP composition and the product used in LoDoCo2 study contain common excipients are lactose monohydrate and magnesium stearate, which constitute the majority of the tablet weight in all the products. Other excipients present are microcrystalline cellulose, gelatine, povidone, starch (maize or potato), sodium starch glycolate, magnesium stearate and talc. ## 2.4.2. Pharmacology

Considering that colchicine is a well-known substance with an extensive history of clinical use, the applicant has not conducted any pharmacological studies. It is agreed that no new studies are required. Various in vitro and in vivo data on the pharmacological properties of colchicine are summarised.

Colchicine is an alkaloid with antimitotic activity that binds to the β subunits of tubulin and prevents the polymerisation of α and β tubulin and microtubule formation. This leads to inhibition of many essential cellular functions, including intracellular vesicle transport and secretion of mediators such as chemokines and cytokines. Thus, colchicine has direct anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting key inflammatory signalling networks, preventing both local chemoattraction of inflammatory cells and systemic inflammation. At the cellular level, colchicine could also exert antiarrhythmic effects.

In vivo, colchicine inhibits apoptosis and exerts indirect antifibrotic effects. It was shown that colchicine, as a pleiotropic anti-inflammatory molecule, may exert cardioprotective effects under acute ischemia. It also inhibits excessive tubulin polymerisation in the infarcted area. It stabilises atherosclerotic plaque by reducing inflammatory activity and plaque burden, without altering macrophage infiltration or plaque typology. Colchicine was shown to reduce inflammatory response in several urate and carrageenan-induced rat paw inflammation models. The exact mechanism of in which colchicine is effective in the treatment of gout is not completely established.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

In animals, it is found to alter neuromuscular function, intensifies gastrointestinal activity by neurogenic stimulation, increases sensitivity to central depressants, heightens response to sympathomimetic compounds, depresses the respiratory centre, constricts blood vessels, causes hypertension by central vasomotor stimulation, and lowers body temperature.

The safety pharmacology of colchicine is well documented in animal studies. At therapeutic doses, colchicine is generally well tolerated. However, at higher exposures, systemic toxicities have been observed in multiple organ systems. Neurological effects include muscle weakness, myopathy, and gait abnormalities. Although colchicine does not significantly penetrate the central nervous system, it accumulates in the adrenal and pituitary glands and has been shown to cause dose-dependent neurotoxicity in rodents. Cardiovascular toxicity at supratherapeutic doses included increased myocardial stiffness and reduced contractility. Renal effects involved altered renin secretion and reduced urine output. Gastrointestinal effects, which are consistent with human clinical experience, included increased intestinal permeability, reduced electrolyte absorption, and apoptosis of intestinal crypt cells. Endocrine and metabolic effects observed included increased insulin secretion, hyperglycaemia, elevated triglycerides, and prolonged corticosteroid responses, likely via ACTH stimulation. Hematopoietic effects included reduced thrombin-induced platelet aggregation and alterations in ATP secretion. Bone toxicity studies reported inhibition of osteoblast proliferation and formation of ectopic calcified tissue in the bone marrow cavity.

The Applicant provided an overview of published data on cardiovascular, neurological, gastrointestinal, renal, metabolic and endocrine, hematopoietic and bone effects of colchicine.

## 2.4.3. Pharmacokinetics

Considering that colchicine is a well-known substance with an extensive history of clinical use for the treatment of gout, the provided information on PK is sufficient.

No dedicated non-clinical pharmacokinetic studies were conducted by the applicant with the proposed product, Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma 0.5 mg tablets. The applicant refers to previously published literature and regulatory findings related to Colchicine Tiofarma 0.5 mg tablets (EU reference product), Colcrys 0.6 mg tablets (US FDA-approved), and Colgout tablets (used in the LoDoCo and LoDoCo 2 clinical studies). Comparative in vitro multimedia dissolution studies have been conducted using these formulations, which all share a similar composition and excipient profile. Furthermore, the applicant conducted in vivo bioavailability and bioequivalence bridging studies between Colgout 500 μ g tablets and the proposed Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma formulation. All products considered are immediaterelease conventional tablets and have been presented as interchangeable to establish pharmacokinetic comparability.

The absorption of oral colchicine was studied in rats, and Cmax was observed to be reached rapidly. Due to sustained levels in plasma, colchicine had long t½ of 9.11 and 8.11 h by oral and intravenous routes, respectively. The absolute oral bioavailability of colchicine in rats was 11.2%, which was comparably less when compared with humans (less than 50%).

Colchicine is distributed primarily into bile, liver, spleen, and kidney. Moderate levels are seen in lungs, heart, intestine, and stomach. Only very small amounts of colchicine are distributed in the brain. The determination of LD50 demonstrated that colchicine was 8 times more toxic in the younger animals.

Colchicine is metabolized in liver microsomes. The extent varies across species; in mouse and rat liver, about 11 to 12% is metabolised, but in hamster, about 40% of colchicine is converted into metabolites. In human liver microsomes, the formation of 3-demethylcolchicine (3-DMC) and 2demethylcolchicine (2-DMC) due to demethylation of colchicine is mediated mainly through CYP3A4. 2DMC does not inhibit inflammation, but 3-DMC is as active as Colchicine. O-glucuronidated metabolites, O - sulfation conjugates, and glutathione conjugates derived from demethylated colchicine were detected in rat bile. Liver is the major route of colchicine elimination. Liver injury impaired the clearance of colchicine in rats.

Colchicine is excreted primarily into faeces (70%) and, to a lesser extent, into the urine (14%). In rats, net secretion appears to be mediated by one or more transporters including P-glycoprotein (Pgp), while in dogs, P-gp does not appear to be involved. Demethylated metabolites, and other unidentified polar metabolites, are also excreted in the bile. One of the findings suggests that extrahepatic bile duct obstruction and chemically induced intrahepatic cholestasis significantly impair the systemic clearance of colchicine. With co-administration of Cyclosporine in rats, the renal clearance of colchicine was reduced.

Drug interactions: Colchicine is the classical substrate of the P-gp transporter; thus, severe drug interactions may occur when colchicine is used with drugs that modulate the activity of the P-gp (verapamil, cyclosporin A). In rats, colchicine has been shown to block the recruitment of ABC transporters to the bile duct canicular membrane. Colchicine administration increases mdr gene expression and P-gp protein (the encoded product of mdr) levels in the liver of rats and mice in vivo. Severe drug interactions are possible using colchicine with the drugs acting on cytochrome P450 (cimetidine). Also, colchicine has been known to cause drug-drug interactions with some macrolides, however, azithromycin did not interfere with the binding of colchicine. This risk is reflected in the SmPC.

## 2.4.4. Toxicology

The toxicity of colchicine is well-understood due to decades of clinical experience. The Applicant provided published literature data on the non-clinical toxicity profile of colchicine. This is considered sufficient.

Toxicity appears to be a function of dosage. Most prominent adverse effects involve gastrointestinal symptoms. Adverse events other than gastrointestinal toxicity are rare (&lt;15%) even with long-term therapy. Colchicine can cause serious and sometimes fatal adverse reactions, such as myelosuppression, leukopenia, pancytopenia, agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia, neuromuscular toxicity, and rhabdomyolysis.

According to evidence from published literature, when taken at therapeutic dosages and at doses modified for individuals with renal and/or hepatic impairment, orally given colchicine is typically well tolerated. Inappropriate dose in individuals with renal or hepatic insufficiency, concurrent medication interactions, or purposeful or accidental overdosing are the main causes of the majority of serious adverse events (AEs) that do occur. Death results from rapidly progressive multi-organ failure and sepsis.

Colchicine shows acute toxicity across species, with LD50 values ranging from 0.1 mg/kg in rabbits to 6.1 mg/kg in rats, and lethal symptoms in rats can be delayed up to 24 hours post-exposure. High single doses cause severe organ damage, including liver, kidney, muscle, and cardiotoxicity, as well as elevated ALT and AST levels in mice. Female and newborn rats are more susceptible to acute toxicity due to reduced drug clearance, often developing profound muscle weakness and dying within 2-4 days. Mice and rats experienced significant weight loss and muscle degeneration following a single intraperitoneal dose.

With repeated oral administration (e.g., 500 µg/100 g daily), guinea pigs developed diarrhea, degeneration of intestinal epithelial cells, and loss of microvilli after 5-6 days. Rabbits, although tolerant of a large acute dose, showed testicular atrophy and arrested spermatogenesis with no other systemic toxicity. Overall, repeated exposure leads to cumulative tissue damage, particularly in reproductive and gastrointestinal systems, even when acute symptoms may appear mild initially.

The applicant has been requested to provide non-clinical safety information to support the chronic oral administration  of  colchicine.  The  applicant  includes  the  reference  to  the  study  ' Evaluation  of  the pharmacokinetics, chylomicron inhibition, and toxicity of colchicine in rats given low doses' , conducted in  male  Sprague-Dawley  rats  (Hamdah  M.  Al  Nebaihi  et  al.,  2024).  According  to  the  study  design, colchicine has been administered at oral doses of 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mg/kg, an intraperitoneal dose of 0.25 mg/kg, and an intravenous dose of 0.1 mg/kg. No evidence of repeated administration of colchicine has  been  identified.  Moreover,  oral  colchicine  doses  ranging  from  0.1  to  0.5  mg/kg  exhibited approximately equal normalized systemic exposures (AUC0-4h values of 24.5, 13.2, and 15.0 ng.h/mL corresponding to oral 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mg/kg doses, respectively). Thus, the relevance of the safety concerns related to the acute oral dosing of colchicine (namely 'Hepatotoxicity in the rat' and 'lymphatic blocking  activity  in  preclinical  species')  was  not  considered  adequate  to  establish  the  chronic  safety profile.  Within  the  scope  of  the  outstanding  requested  safety  data,  the  applicant  provided  a  set  of additional  literature-based  information,  in  particular  concerning  the  Veterinary  Chronic  Use  in  Dogs (Shar-Pei), and the Repeat-Dose Oral Studies, up to 12 weeks duration, in rodents (see: response points 2 and 3, respectively), which was considered adequate to support the chronic oral administration of colchicine. Moreover, the safety profile in the intended target population has been addressed from a clinical perspective, which supersedes the need of additional non-clinical data.

Colchicine was not mutagenic in the bacteria reverse mutagenicity assay (AMES test) and negative for mutagenicity in the bacterial reverse mutation assay. In several studies, colchicine was positive in mutagenic assays. It is known to be an aneugenic substance which causes chromosome aberrations both in vitro and in vivo. In a chromosomal aberration assay in cultured human white blood cells, colchicine treatment resulted in the formation of micronuclei. However, these micronuclei were formed by mitotic nondisjunction without structural DNA changes, and therefore, colchicine is not considered clastogenic. Positive clastogenic results were reported for in the in-vitro mammalian cell micronucleus assay (0.1 μ g/mL), and in vivo micronucleus in mice (0.35 to 5 mg/kg), hamsters (3 mg/kg), and rats (2.5 to 5 μ g/kg). In general, positive chromosomal aberration assays were obtained with higher doses than utilized in studies that had negative results.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

No evidence of carcinogenic potential was observed in animals. Given long clinical experience in broad age range of patients, colchicine has been considered safe concerning the carcinogenicity aspects. Two-year studies were conducted in mice and rats to assess the carcinogenic potential of colchicine. No evidence of colchicine-related tumorigenicity was observed in mice or rats at colchicine oral doses up to 3 mg/kg/day and 2 mg/kg/day, respectively (approximately 6 and 8 times, respectively, the maximum recommended human dose of 2.4 mg on a mg/m2 basis).

Colchicine has detrimental effects on reproduction and development due to its inhibition of microtubule formation and cell division. This adversely affects germ cell development by meiosis and subsequent fertility in males and females, as well as interfering with mitosis and subsequent early embryonic development and implantation, and organogenesis. The effects are species and dose-dependent, with the timing of exposure also critical for the effects on embryonic development.

In adult male rats, Leydig cells develop a rapid rise in testosterone synthesis when exposed to colchicine. In male mice, an increase in sperm abnormalities was observed in all levels of treatment. Several dosage-related effects on oogenesis, some irreversible, were produced in females exposed to colchicine. Pregnant mouse was found to be more sensitive to colchicine than non-pregnant mice following i.v. administration. Colchicine interferes with sperm penetration, the second meiotic division and normal cleavage in the mouse, but no effect was observed in the rabbit with similar dose levels.

However, based on its spindle effect, non-clinical evidence indicates toxicity to fertility and reproduction as reflected in the product information of medicinal products containing colchicine approved for gout prevention, treatment of acute gouty flares, and familial Mediterranean fever. Nevertheless, colchicine is mentioned amongst the Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs (DMARDs) compatible with pregnancy and breastfeeding, as reported in the literature (Ruegg et al., 2025).

Based on scientific literature, there is a clear link between the mechanism of action of colchicine and the embryotoxicity, teratogenicity, impaired spermatogenesis and adverse perinatal outcomes observed in animals. However, even though the theoretical risk is mechanistically plausible, it is unlikely under therapeutic doses in humans. The data from Ruegg et al. (2025) review show that along with the data from other scientific literature, the human data indicate no increased risk of spontaneous abortion, congenital malformations, or major adverse pregnancy outcomes with colchicine exposure at therapeutic doses. Overall, available pregnancy data are reassuring, but remain limited, and a residual theoretical risk cannot be excluded. Colchicine is also generally compatible with lactation, and very low concentrations appear in breast milk with no reported harm in breastfed infants.

Overall, available reports do not indicate an increased risk of congenital anomalies, do not show a consistent pattern of embryo-fetal toxicity, and do not suggest harm in breastfed infants, but these findings must be interpreted with caution, as post-marketing data are inherently limited by small case numbers, under-reporting &amp; lack of reliable information. In line with SWP/NcWP recommendations for agents with potential effects on gametogenesis (EMA/CHMP/SWP/74077/2020 rev. 1*), a precautionary contraception period is advised.

Non-clinical studies demonstrated that colchicine is embryotoxic and teratogenic. Aneuploidy during germ cell meiosis can result in spontaneous abortion and congenital abnormalities. Other toxic effects observed in the mouse were changes in the live birth and viability index, cytological changes.

Colchicine administered to pregnant rats on Days 18, 19, and 20 of gestation causes an overall reduction in brain size, a reduction of neocortical cells, and a reduction in the width of the corpus callosum.

Colchicine disrupts lactation by interfering with intracellular transport, leading to the accumulation of secretory vesicles and altered fat droplet positioning in mammary gland cells. It inhibits lactose production by elevating cAMP levels due to hormonal changes, as seen in guinea pig studies. In larger animals like Holstein heifers and goats, colchicine reduces milk biosynthesis and causes a reversible decrease in milk flow during lactation.

No safety concerns related to the potential genotoxic of photodegradant products, beta and gamma-lumicolchicine were identified.

## 2.4.1. Discussion on non-clinical aspects

Pharmacodynamic, pharmacokinetic and toxicological properties of colchicine are well known. As colchicine is a widely used, well-known active substance, the applicant has not provided additional studies, and further studies are not required. Overview based on literature review is, thus, appropriate.

Colchicine is considered a well-known active substance with an established safety profile. During the procedure, the applicant provided a set of additional literature-based data, in particular concerning the veterinary chronic use in dogs (Shar-Pei), and the repeat-dose oral studies, up to 12 weeks duration, in rodents, which was considered adequate to support the chronic oral administration of colchicine. Moreover, the safety profile in the intended target population has been addressed from a clinical perspective, which supersedes the need for additional non-clinical data.

This risk of drug-drug interactions that may occur when colchicine is used with medicinal products that modulate the activity of the multidrug resistance efflux transporter P-glycoprotein or cytochrome P450 3A4 is sufficiently reflected in the SmPC and is in line with the SmPC of other colchicine containing medicinal products.

The effects on fertility, embryotoxicity and teratogenicity and recommendations for the duration of contraception during/after colchicine treatment are addressed in Section 4.6 of the SmPC of Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma. Due to its spindle effect (interference with the mitotic spindle during cell division), non-clinical evidence indicates toxicity to fertility and reproduction as reflected in the product information of medicinal products containing colchicine approved for gout prevention, treatment of acute gouty flares, and familial Mediterranean fever. Nevertheless, colchicine is mentioned amongst the Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs (DMARDs) compatible with pregnancy and breastfeeding, as reported in the literature (Ruegg et al., 2025). In line with the EMA SWP/NcWP recommendations on the duration of contraception following the end of treatment with a genotoxic drug, the SmPC Sections 4.6 and 5.3 of Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma include information regarding the reproductive toxicity.

The non-clinical aspects of the Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma product information are in line with the product information of other colchicine medicinal products in the EU. The impurity profile has been discussed and was considered acceptable. This is in accordance with the relevant guideline and additional non-clinical studies were not considered necessary.

## Absorption

Colchicine is rapidly absorbed after oral administration from the gastrointestinal tract. The bioavailability of colchicine is about 45% In healthy adults, the mean Cmax of 2.5 ng/mL (range 1.1 to 4.4 ng/mL) was achieved in one to two hours (range 0.5 to 3 hours) after a single dose administered under fasting conditions. Administration of colchicine with food does not affect the colchicine absorption rate but decreases the extent of colchicine by approximately 15%.

## Distribution

The mean apparent volume of distribution in young and healthy patients is about 5-8 L/kg. It is known that colchicine crosses the placenta and distributes into the breast milk. Colchicine has been found to distribute to various tissues, mainly into the bile, liver, and kidney tissues. Smaller amounts have been detected in the heart, lungs, intestinal tissue, and stomach. The plasma protein binding for colchicine is low to moderate, at 39 ± 5%, and it is mainly bound to albumin regardless of concentration.

## Biotransformation

Colchicine is metabolized intestinally and hepatically by CYP3A4/P-gp, which catalyses demethylation of colchicine to inactive metabolites. Plasma levels of these metabolites are less than 5% of parent drug.

## Elimination

In a pharmacokinetic study of healthy research subjects who received 1 mg of oral colchicine, about 40% to 65% of the dose was recovered in the urine in the form of an unchanged drug. Colchicine undergoes enterohepatic recirculation and biliary excretion. After several doses of 0.6 mg twice daily, the average elimination half-life of colchicine ranges from 26.6 to 31.2 hours. Another study reported the elimination half-life ranging to be 20 to 40 hours. In a multiple-dose study of colchicine administration at a dose of 1 mg per day, steady-state concentrations were achieved by day 8 following administration.

## Pharmacokinetics in the patient population

No PK data were obtained directly in the target population. However, the PK profile of colchicine is well established in the literature and demonstrates predictable absorption, distribution, and clearance characteristics across adult populations. When combined with the proposed low dose (0.5 mg once daily), these data support the expectation that PK behaviour will not differ significantly nor pose clinically relevant concerns in the intended therapeutic setting. Furthermore, indirect evidence from long-term clinical trials and published PK studies in elderly subjects and patients with common comorbidities reinforces that systemic exposure at this dose remains within the established safety range. Therefore, while the absence of population-specific PK data is acknowledged, the overall risk is considered low and does not warrant additional investigation for this indication.

## Renal impairment

In published open-label study conducted in 8 healthy subjects with normal renal function, 8 subjects each with mild (eGFR 60-89 mL/min/1.73 m 2 ), moderate (eGFR 30-59 mL/min/1. 73 m 2 ), or (severe eGFR 15-29 mL/min/1. 73 m 2 ) renal impairment, and 8 subjects with end-stage renal disease (ESRD - eGFR &lt;15 mL/min/1.73 m 2 ), who received 0.6 mg colchicine prior to receiving, and again following, haemodialysis, the exposure of colchicine was similar for subjects with normal renal function, mild impairment, or ESRD prior to and during haemodialysis (24.7 - 31.7 ng.h/mL) but was up to two fold higher in subjects with moderate or severe renal impairment (48.9 and 48.0 ng.h/mL, respectively).

## Geriatric patients

An open label, non-randomized study was conducted in 20 young subjects with normal renal function and 18 elderly subjects with normal or mild renal impairment with the objective to compare the relative bioavailability of colchicine to determine whether dosing adjustments are required in elderly patients. There were no statistically significant (P &lt; 0.05) differences in mean colchicine pharmacokinetic parameters between young and elderly subjects. The  pharmacokinetics  of  colchicine  appear  broadly  consistent  between  males  and  females  when differences in body size are taken into account. Observed variability in exposure is primarily attributable to weight and lean body mass rather than intrinsic sex-related metabolic differences.

Regarding ethnic differences, colchicine metabolism is predominantly mediated by CYP3A4 and transport via P-glycoprotein, with plasma levels of metabolites remaining low. Current evidence does not indicate that ethnicity is a major contributor to variability in systemic exposure.

Colchicine is a substrate for the efflux transporter P-glycoprotein. Of the cytochrome P450 enzymes tested, CYP3A4 is the primary enzyme involved in the metabolism of colchicine. If administering colchicine with drugs that inhibit P-glycoprotein, most of which also inhibit CYP3A4 increased concentrations of colchicine are likely.

## 2.5.2.4. Pharmacodynamics

The applicant did not present new pharmacodynamics studies to justify the new therapeutic indication for low-dose colchicine (0.5 mg).

The major pharmacological action of colchicine is its ability to bind to tubulin dimers. Colchicine binds in an equimolar and poorly reversible manner to tubulin, forming a tubulin-colchicine complex in cells. It prevents the polymerisation of microtubules by binding their protein subunits and preventing conglomeration. By disrupting the cytoskeleton, it inhibits many signalling pathways and cellular events such as chemotaxis and phagocytosis, which explains most of the anti-inflammatory properties of this molecule. Colchicine has also marked effects on leukocyte function, preventing diapedesis, mobilization, lysosomal degranulation, and chemotaxis. Colchicine modulates the action of the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to a decrease in the production of cytokines involved in the progression of the inflammatory response, such as interleukin (IL)-1 β and IL-18.

Colchicine disrupts cytoskeletal functions through inhibition of β -tubulin polymerization into microtubules and consequently prevents the activation, degranulation and migration of neutrophils. Evidence suggests that colchicine may also interfere with the intracellular assembly of the inflammasome complex present in neutrophils and monocytes that mediates activation of interleukin1β. Based upon the paradigm of atherosclerosis as an inflammatory process, colchicine has recently been investigated as a possible therapy for the prevention and/or treatment of stable CAD and ACS. Hypothetically, colchicine's effects on macrophages, adhesion molecules and neutrophils, as well as its ability to reduce cytokine and matrix metalloproteinase production, could all play roles in reducing adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

The studies presented are the result of scientific publications. These studies, developed in animal models and in humans (clinical trials), reveal the mechanism of action by which colchicine can be used for the therapeutic indication mentioned above. The submitted studies reveal that colchicine reduces a set of mediators involved in the chronic inflammatory response and associated with cardiovascular disease (formation of atherosclerotic plaque).

## Pharmacodynamic data in clinical studies submitted as part of the submitted literature supporting the application.

EKSTROM trial concluded in 2025, demonstrated that anti-inflammatory treatment with low-dose colchicine, 0.5 mg improved several measures of plaque volume changes over a period of 12 months in patients with stable CAD. The randomized, double-blind, placebo controlled EKSTROM trial evaluated the effects of low-dose colchicine, 0.5 mg on atherosclerotic plaque. A total of 84 participants were enrolled, of which 72 completed the trial, with participants randomized to receive either colchicine 0.5 mg/day or placebo (n=36 per treatment group) as add-on to their standard of care. All participants underwent coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), at baseline and at 12 months. The results demonstrate low-dose colchicine, 0.5 mg significantly reduced the total plaque volume compared to placebo at 12 months (change in percent atheroma volume [PAV] was -1.1% between treatment groups; P=0.015). Low-dose colchicine treatment significantly reduced dense calcified plaque volume (-0.9% PAV between treatment groups; P=0.009), another indicator of cardiovascular risk. Similarly, low-dose colchicine treatment demonstrated trends toward regression of non-calcified plaque, fibrous and fibro-fatty plaque. Future analyses are needed in a larger population of patients to confirm these findings [Verghese et al., 2024].

In a randomized study conducted, 83 adult patients (age &gt;21 years) were divided into 3 groups: ACS (Acute Coronary Syndrome) (n= 40), stable CAD, (n=33) and control group (n=10) to assess the local cardiac production of inflammatory cytokines in patients with ACS, stable CAD and in controls; and to determine whether acute administration of colchicine inhibits their production. Patients included in the ACS and stable CAD groups were randomized in a 1:1 fashion to receive either colchicine (1 mg, followed by 0.5 mg 1 hour later) or no colchicine therapy, 6 to 24 hours prior to coronary angiography. Increased intracardiac production of the inflammasome specific cytokines IL-1 β, IL-18 and downstream IL-6 in patients presenting with an ACS was demonstrated. Furthermore, acute colchicine administration is associated with a significant reduction in the transcoronary production of these cytokines in patients with ACS. These data suggest a possible therapeutic role for colchicine in acutely suppressing atherosclerosis-associated inflammation [Martínez, 2015].

The justification of the colchicine dose to be used (0.5 mg/day) was based on two published clinical trials (LoDoCo and LoDoCo2) and supported by seven additional published studies.

LoDoCo clinical trial (Study 1) - LoDoCo clinical trial was a prospective, randomized, observer-blinded endpoint design. 532 patients with stable coronary disease receiving aspirin and/or clopidogrel (93%) and statins (95%) were randomly assigned colchicine 0.5 mg/day or no colchicine and followed for a median of 3 years.

LoDoCo2 clinical trial (Study 2) - LoDoCo2 trial was a much larger double-blind, placebo-controlled multicentre trial that included 5522 patients (2762 were assigned to the Colchicine group and 2760 to the placebo group) recruited in Australia and the Netherlands. The median duration of follow-up was 28.6 months. The mean age was 66 years; the majority of the patients had a history of remote MI or coronary stenting. Patients were not selected based upon any clinical or laboratory markers of risk such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein. All had proven tolerant to a 30-day trial of colchicine 0.5 mg daily. A significantly lower occurrence of cardiovascular events was observed in patients with chronic coronary disease receiving low-dose colchicine compared with those receiving placebo. Overall, among patients with chronic coronary disease, 0.5 mg of colchicine once daily resulted in a 31% lower relative risk of cardiovascular death, spontaneous MI, ischemic stroke, or ischemia-driven coronary revascularization (the primary endpoint) than placebo, with a hazard ratio of 0.69.

## 2.5.5.2. LoDoCo clinical trial (Study 1) (Nidorf et al 2013)

LoDoCo (Low Dose Colchicine) was the first trial to demonstrate the proof of concept that an antiinflammatory drug could prevent cardiovascular events in patients with CAD.

LoDoCo clinical trial was a prospective, randomized, observer-blinded endpoint design. 532 patients with stable coronary disease receiving aspirin and/or clopidogrel (93%) and statins (95%) were randomly assigned colchicine 0.5 mg/day or no colchicine and followed for a median of 3 years. The primary outcome was the composite incidence of ACS, out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, or noncardioembolic ischemic stroke. The primary analysis was by intention-to-treat.

The primary outcome occurred in 15 of 282 patients (5.3%) who received colchicine and 40 of 250 patients (16.0%) assigned no colchicine (hazard ratio: 0.33; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.18 to 0.59; p &lt; 0.001; number needed to treat: 11). In a pre-specified secondary on-treatment analysis that excluded 32 patients (11%) assigned to colchicine who withdrew within 30 days due to intestinal intolerance and a further 7 patients (2%) who did not start treatment, the primary outcome occurred in 4.5% versus 16.0% (hazard ratio: 0.29; 95% CI: 0.15 to 0.56; p &lt; 0.001). Colchicine 0.5 mg/day administered in addition to statins and other standard secondary prevention therapies appeared effective for the prevention of cardiovascular events in patients with stable coronary disease.

The effect of colchicine on the primary outcome was evident early, and the benefits of colchicine continued to accrue throughout the follow-up period. Results of the primary analysis were consistent in all subgroups examined. There was no evidence of differential treatment effects based on any of the clinical or therapeutic variables.

## 2.5.5.3. LoDoCo clinical trial (study 2) (Samuel 2021; Nidorf et al 2020)

LoDoCo2 trial was a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled multicentre trial that included 5522 patients (2762 were assigned to the colchicine group and 2760 to the placebo group) recruited in Australia and the Netherlands. The primary end point was a composite of cardiovascular death, spontaneous (nonprocedural) MI, ischemic stroke, or ischemia-driven coronary revascularization. The key secondary end point was a composite of cardiovascular death, spontaneous MI, or ischemic stroke. The median duration of follow-up was 28.6 months.

The mean age was 66 years; the majority had a history of remote MI or coronary stenting. Patients were not selected based upon any clinical or laboratory markers of risk such as high-sensitivity Creactive protein. All had proven tolerant to a 30-day trial of colchicine 0.5 mg daily.

A primary end-point event occurred in 187 patients (6.8%) in the colchicine group and in 264 patients (9.6%) in the placebo group (incidence, 2.5 vs. 3.6 events per 100 person-years; hazard ratio, 0.69; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.57 to 0.83; P&lt;0.001). A key secondary end-point event occurred in 115 patients (4.2%) in the colchicine group and in 157 patients (5.7%) in the placebo group (incidence, 1.5 vs. 2.1 events per 100 person-years; hazard ratio, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.57 to 0.92; P=0.007).

A significantly lower occurrence of cardiovascular events was observed in patients with chronic coronary disease receiving low-dose colchicine compared with those receiving placebo. Overall, among patients with chronic coronary disease, 0.5 mg of colchicine once daily resulted in a 31% lower relative risk of cardiovascular death, spontaneous MI, ischemic stroke, or ischemia-driven coronary revascularization (the primary endpoint) than placebo, with a hazard ratio of 0.69. The benefit was mostly driven by significant reductions in the risk of MI and ischemia-driven revascularization, suggesting that colchicine was affecting the progression of atherosclerosis. The benefits were seen soon after initiating therapy, continued to accrue over time, and were achieved in patients already receiving effective secondary prevention therapies.

As in the LoDoCo trial, involving patients with chronic coronary disease, it was found that the risk of acute cardiovascular events was lower among those who received 0.5 mg of colchicine once daily than among those who did not receive colchicine (Nidorf et al 2020).

The LoDoCo2 trial provides evidence to support repurposing of colchicine for routine secondary prevention in patients with chronic coronary diseases. Gout occurred in 38 patients (1.4%) in the colchicine group and in 95 patients (3.4%) in the placebo group (cumulative incidence ratio, 0.40; 95% CI, 0.28 to 0.58). Neutropenia and myotoxic effects were uncommon in both trial groups. Among the patients from the Netherlands, myalgia was reported in 384 (21.2%) in the colchicine group and 334 (18.5%) in the placebo group (cumulative incidence ratio, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.01 to 1.31). Dysesthesia was reported in 143 patients (7.9%) in the colchicine group and in 150 patients (8.3%) in the placebo group (cumulative incidence ratio, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.76 to 1.18).

LoDoCo and LoDoCo2 trial results provided evidence that colchicine can reduce the risk of cardiovascular events and therefore  support repurposing of colchicine for secondary prevention in patients with chronic coronary diseases.

LoDoCo study and LoDoCo2 study included 532 and 5522 patients respectively with chronic, stable CAD, stable for 6 months. In these studies patients received colchicine 0.5 mg once daily or placebo along with standard therapy and the patients were followed for a median period of 3 years and 28.6 months respectively.

Both studies had common inclusion criteria. There was no prominent difference in the colchicine and placebo group in number of subjects that discontinued the therapy, in LoDoCo2 trial (289 in colchicine vs 291 in placebo) (Nidorf et al 2020). ## 2.5.6. Discussion on clinical efficacy

The drug substance colchicine is an alkaloid originally derived from the plant Colchicum autumnale (autumn crocus). Colchicine has been used for decades primarily for treatment of acute gout in adults, prophylaxis of a gout attack in adults during initiation of urate-lowering therapy, in Familial Mediterranean Fever for prophylaxis of attacks and prevention of amyloidosis in adults and paediatric patients.

Recently, colchicine has been rediscovered for its potential use in the prevention of secondary CV events due to its anti-inflammatory properties. The key role of inflammation in the pathogenesis of CAD, has led to the exploration of anti-inflammatory agents for the secondary prevention of CV events. The mechanism of action of colchicine in the prevention of major CV events is not understood. Colchicine is considered to act against the inflammatory response by possibly inhibiting the migration of granulocytes to the inflamed area and by interaction with the microtubules, which together could contribute to the prevention of CV events although the mechanism of action remains not fully understood.

It has been demonstrated that colchicine can rapidly and reliably reduce hs-CRP, have favourable effects on the morphology of coronary atherosclerotic plaque, reduce the risk for in-stent stenosis, and reduce the risk for cardiovascular events without tolerability concerns.

The application concerns a hybrid medicinal product as defined in Article 10(3) of Directive 2001/83/EC and refers to a EU reference medicinal product for which a Marketing Authorisation has been granted pursuant to Article 10a of Directive 2001/83/EC (Colchicine Tiofarma 0.5 mg tablets). The hybrid nature of this application is in the changes to the therapeutic indication.

The applicant's claim was that the current application could respond to the unmet needs of the patient with CAD and provide a treatment for the CV prevention indication.

The applicant proposed the formulation of the already well-known drug substance colchicine (0.5 mg) for the following therapeutic indication: 'To reduce the risk of myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, coronary revascularization, and cardiovascular death in adult patients with established atherosclerotic disease or with multiple risk factors for cardiovascular disease'.

The effect of colchicine 0.5 mg/day for CV events prevention is being explored in several large-scale, rigorous trials, including LoDoCo2 (Low Dose Colchicine2) in patients with stable CAD (Nidorf 2019)

The LoDoCo and LoDoCo2 trials revealed that long term use of low dose colchicine was effective in secondary prevention in patients with CAD when used along with standard therapy like statins, antiplatelet drugs like aspirin, betablockers and other interventional methods (Nidorf 2020, 2013).

Meta-analyses of the CVD prevention trials, (n = 11,594) show a 32% reduction in the composite end point of cardiovascular mortality, MI, ischemic stroke and urgent revascularization (pooled HR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.51-0.81). No statistically significant differences in adverse events between colchicine and placebo patients were detected (Samuel 2021). Efficacy of colchicine in CV diseases has been also demonstrated in clinical observational studies (Slobodnick 2015; Katsanos 2020; Defteros 2013).

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

Additionally recent studies confirm that the use of colchicine in patients with stable CAD could have a potential role in stabilizing coronary plaque and reducing atherosclerosis progression. The EKSTROM trial is a randomized, double-blind, placebo- controlled study investigating the effects of 0.5 mg/day colchicine on atherosclerotic plaque progression in 84 participants with stable CAD. Conducted over 12 months, the trial used coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) to measure changes in total plaque volume, inflammatory markers, pericoronary/epicardial adipose tissue, and vascular function. The study demonstrated a 1.1% reduction in total plaque volume progression (P=0.015) and significant slowing of dense calcified plaque progression (P=0.009). Trends toward regression of noncalcified, fibrous, and fibro-fatty plaques were also observed (Verghese 2024).

The applicant provided detailed summaries of all relevant published clinical studies regarding efficacy and safety of colchicine in secondary CV prevention.

The applicant is relying on efficacy and safety of an EU reference product (Colchicine Tiofarma 0.5 mg tablets), and published clinical studies LoDoCo, LoDoCo2 along with other scientific literature. The applicant has performed bridging studies between products used in the clinical trials LoDoCo &amp; LoDoCo2 and Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma. The EU reference product, Colchicine Tiofarma, has the following therapeutic indication approved:

- in adults for the treatment of acute gout and prophylaxis of gout attack;
- in adults and paediatric patients for the prophylaxis of attacks and the prevention of amyloidosis in familial Mediterranean fever.

Clinical practice guidelines for the use of low dose colchicine (0.5 mg) in CV prevention have been published by the American Heart Association, American College of Cardiology, American College of Clinical Pharmacy, Asian Pacific Society of Cardiology, National Lipid Association, Preventive Cardiovascular Nurses Association. They underline that inflammation is a key component in the development of atherosclerosis. Colchicine, with its anti-inflammatory properties may have a role in CV prevention. The recommendations concerning the use of colchicine in CV prevention are also discussed in the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines: low-dose colchicine (0.5 mg o.d.) may be considered in secondary prevention of CVD, particularly if other risk factors are insufficiently controlled or if recurrent CVD events occur under optimal therapy (Class of recommendation IIB, Level of evidence A [multiple clinical trials or meta-analysis]) (ESC Guidelines on Cardiovascular Prevention, 2021). In Latin America the following clinical practice recommendation has been suggested: after MI and in patients with stable CAD low dose colchicine may be used for the prevention of CVD. In Canada the following clinical practice recommendation has been established: low dose colchicine may be considered in reduction of atherothrombotic events in adults with existing coronary heart disease in addition to standard therapies.

The primary data supporting the efficacy and safety of colchicine for reducing the risk of CV disease is based on the following two submitted randomized controlled studies, along with other supporting published literatures: (1) LoDoCo clinical study, (2) LoDoCo2 clinical study.

## (1) LoDoCo Study

The LoDoCo (Low-Dose Colchicine), is a prospective, randomized, observer-blinded endpoint (PROBE) trial, which was conducted in 532 patients to determine whether Colchicine 0.5 mg/day can reduce the risk of CV events in patients with clinically stable coronary disease. The control group did not receive colchicine.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

Study population consisted of 532 patients with stable coronary disease receiving aspirin and/or clopidogrel (93%) and statins (95%) were randomly assigned to colchicine 0.5 mg/day or no colchicine and followed for a median of 3 years.

Colchicine 0.5 mg/day administered in addition to statins and other standard secondary prevention therapies appeared effective for the prevention of CV events in patients with stable CAD.

The inclusion criteria were as follows (meeting each of the following criteria):

- 1) angiographically proven coronary disease;
- 2) age 35 to 85 years;
- 3) clinically stable for at least 6 months;
- 4) no major competing comorbidities or contraindication to colchicine therapy;

Patients with a history of bypass surgery were only eligible if they had undergone bypass surgery more than 10 years before, had angiographic evidence of graft failure, or had undergone stenting since their bypass surgery.

The primary efficacy outcome was the composite of ACS, fatal or nonfatal out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, or non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke. Secondary outcomes were individual components of the primary outcome and the components of ACS unrelated to stent disease.

The primary outcome occurred in 15 of 282 patients (5.3%) who received colchicine and 40 of 250 patients (16.0%) assigned no colchicine (hazard ratio: 0.33; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.18 to 0.59; p &lt; 0.001; number needed to treat: 11). In a pre-specified secondary on-treatment analysis that excluded 32 patients (11%) assigned to colchicine who withdrew within 30 days due to intestinal intolerance and a further 7 patients (2%) who did not start treatment, the primary outcome occurred in 4.5% versus 16.0% [hazard ratio: 0.29 (risk reduction of 70%); 95% CI: 0.15 to 0.56; p &lt; 0.001]. The results of this study allowed the following conclusions: colchicine 0.5 mg/day administered in addition to statins and other standard secondary prevention therapies appeared effective for the prevention of cardiovascular events in patients with stable coronary disease.

## (2) LodoCo 2 study

The LoDoCo2 was a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial with 5522 patients. The results obtained are consistent with those obtained in the first LoDoCo trial. Registered colchicine products (Colchicine Tiofarma 0.5 mg tablets approved in the EU and Colgout 500 microgram tablets approved in Australia) were used in the LoDoCo2 trial.

LoDoCo2 trial was a much larger double-blind, placebo-controlled multicenter trial that included 5522 patients (2762 were assigned to the Colchicine group and 2760 to the placebo group) recruited in Australia and the Netherlands. The median duration of follow-up was 28.6 months.

The following inclusion criteria of LoDoCo2 were similar to those in the LoDoCo study:

- coronary disease with angiography (or MI with PCI),
- clinically stable condition, for at least six months.

The full list of the LoDoCo 2 study inclusion criteria:

1. Age &gt;35 and &lt;82 years
2. Proven CAD; as evidenced by coronary angiography, CT coronary angiography or a Coronary Artery Calcium Score (Agatston score &gt;400). Individuals with a history of bypass surgery are only eligible if they have undergone coronary artery bypass surgery more than 10 years

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

before, or have angiographic evidence of graft failure or have undergone percutaneous intervention since their bypass surgery

3. Clinically stable for at least six months
4. No significant co-morbidity that might affect the follow - up
5. No contraindication for colchicine use.

Primary objective of LoDoCo 2 was to determine in patients with established stable CAD whether low dose colchicine (0.5 mg once daily) compared with placebo will reduce the composite of CV death, out of hospital cardiac arrest, ACS (non-fatal MI or unstable angina) and non-cardio-embolic ischemic stroke.

Secondary objectives of LoDoCo 2 were:

## 1) assessment the effect of colchicine on other CV endpoints

including new onset atrial fibrillation, and

2) assessment of the safety of long-term low dose colchicine in patients with CV disease who are taking statin therapy.

The primary end point in LoDoCo2 study was a composite of cardiovascular death, spontaneous (nonprocedural) MI, ischemic stroke, or ischemia-driven coronary revascularization. The key secondary end point was a composite of CV death, spontaneous MI, or ischemic stroke. A primary end-point event occurred in 187 patients (6.8%) in the colchicine group and in 264 patients (9.6%) in the placebo group (incidence, 2.5 vs. 3.6 events per 100 person-years; hazard ratio, 0.69; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.57 to 0.83; P&lt;0.001).

A key secondary end-point event occurred in 115 patients (4.2%) in the colchicine group and in 157 patients (5.7%) in the placebo group (incidence, 1.5 vs. 2.1 events per 100 person-years; hazard ratio, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.57 to 0.92; P=0.007). The incidence rates of spontaneous MI or ischemiadriven coronary revascularization (composite end point), CV death or spontaneous MI (composite end point), ischemia-driven coronary revascularization, and spontaneous MI were also significantly lower with colchicine than with placebo.

A significantly lower occurrence of cardiovascular events was observed in patients with chronic coronary disease receiving low-dose colchicine compared with those receiving placebo. Overall, among patients with chronic coronary disease, 0.5 mg of colchicine once daily resulted in a 31% lower relative risk of CV death, spontaneous MI, ischemic stroke, or ischemia-driven coronary revascularization (the primary endpoint) than placebo, with a hazard ratio of 0.69. The benefit was mostly driven by significant reductions in the risk of MI and ischemia-driven revascularization, suggesting that colchicine was affecting the progression of atherosclerosis. The benefits were seen soon after initiating therapy, continued to accrue over time, and were achieved in patients already receiving effective secondary prevention therapies.

With respect to the initially proposed therapeutic indication, it should be noted that 'coronary revascularization' refers specifically to procedures performed in the context of an acute MI, whereas 'ischemia-driven coronary revascularization' refers to revascularization performed due to proven myocardial ischemia, for example in cases of stable angina associated with an abnormal stress test. In addition, the term 'stroke' in the primary endpoint encompasses both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes.

Concerning the LoDoCo 2 study, the patient population was broadly representative of individuals with stable CAD, with the majority (57%) having experienced their last ACS event more than 24 months prior to enrolment.

Most patients were male (85%), with a mean age of 66 years, and had a medical history notable for hypertension (50%) and diabetes (19%).

The majority of patients were receiving standard medical therapy, including antithrombotic treatment (either single antiplatelet therapy or dual antiplatelet therapy), lipid-lowering agents, reninangiotensin system inhibitors, and beta-blockers. The population is representative of patient with multiple risk factors for cardiovascular disease using lipid-lowering therapies, blood pressure control, glucose control and antithrombotic therapies.

With the D120 response sequence, the Applicant addressed the issue of metformin use and whether metformin use could influence the anti-inflammatory effects of colchicine, with relevance to patients with metabolic syndrome. Metformin use was permitted as part of routine care in the LoDoCo and LoDoCo2 studies, remained stable during treatment, and was balanced between groups by randomisation. Subgroup analyses from LoDoCo2 indicate that neither diabetes status nor metformin use modified the treatment effect. The distinct mechanisms of action of metformin and colchicine, together with pharmacokinetic considerations, do not suggest a clinically relevant interaction. Overall, the information provided by the applicant was considered sufficient, and no specific SmPC restrictions or warnings were justified. The clinical efficacy of low-dose colchicine was shown to be consistent across relevant patient subgroups and background cardiovascular therapies, supporting its use as addon treatment to standard secondary prevention without the need for subgroup-specific restrictions.

Regarding the primary outcome of LoDoCo, this small study had open-label (PROBE) design, with methodological limitations. The randomised patients were with stable coronary disease for over 6 months. The reduction in the primary outcome was largely driven by the reduction in the number of patients presenting with ACS unrelated to stent disease. The difference is not significant concerning the out of hospital cardiac arrest and the non-cardioembolic stroke.

Concerning the primary and secondary outcome of LoDoCo 2, this study appears to be more interpretable than the smaller LoDoCo study, particularly in relation to the definition of the endpoints. The primary endpoint of LoDoCo2 is defined as 'the incidence of the first occurrence of the composite endpoint of cardiovascular death, MI, ischemic stroke, or ischemia-driven coronary revascularization.

In LoDoCo 2, like in the LoDoCo study, the randomised patients were with stable CAD for over 6 months. However, the study was overall well conducted, randomized, double blind, placebo-controlled and of larger scale than LoDoCo. The design of the LoDoCo 2 study was considered acceptable. .

The study concluded that colchicine administered for a median of 28.6 months reduced a composite primary outcome of CV death, spontaneous MI, ischemic stroke and ischemia- driven coronary revascularization by 31%. Also, LoDoCo2 allowed to conclude to a 28% reduction of MACE (CV deaths, MI, stroke).

Regarding the result concerning the ischemic stroke, the difference doesn't appear clinically significant between the colchicine and the placebo groups. The applicant was therefore requested to provide additional information regarding the efficacy of colchicine in ischemic stroke as well as stroke in general (including both ischemic and haemorrhagic forms), given that prevention of stroke is part of the proposed indication. The applicant has satisfactorily addressed the question by contextualising the LoDoCo2 findings within the broader clinical evidence base. While the LoDoCo2 study alone does not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in stroke, the totality of evidence supports a favourable effect of colchicine on the risk of ischaemic stroke without an increased risk of haemorrhagic stroke.

Other studies discussed during the procedure in the context of colchicine use in the CV prevention were as follows:

The objectives of the study (Ref: Solomon, 2016), was to test the association between colchicine and CV risk and all-cause mortality (ACM) in gout. Despite many similarities between the two groups, key differences suggest that colchicine may have been more frequently prescribed to patients who were more compliant with medication use, as reflected by higher rates of statin, antihypertensive, allopurinol, NSAID, and steroid use. A significant decrease in MI was observed in the Colchicine group versus the No-Colchicine group. The results below only suggest a potential benefit of colchicine in STEMI, but the result were not significant and the authors concluded that the study was not powered to assess clinical endpoints.

With the D120 response sequence, the Applicant provided an adequate explanation for the numerical difference in treatment effect observed between patients receiving and not receiving β -blockers in the LoDoCo study. As β -blocker use was not randomised, subgroup comparisons are observational and subject to confounding, with β -blocker users likely representing a higher-risk population. The small subgroup sizes and overlapping confidence intervals do not indicate a statistically significant treatment-by-subgroup interaction, and the forest plot suggests benefit in both subgroups. Consistent with findings from LoDoCo2, together with the absence of a plausible mechanistic interaction, the conclusion was reached that the observed difference is likely due to chance. Overall, the efficacy of lowdose colchicine is considered independent of β -blocker use, and no further clarification was considered required.

With the D120 response sequence, the Applicant addressed the numerical increase observed in the insulin-dependent diabetes subgroup and provided analyses indicating that this finding is not clinically meaningful. On this basis, no specific restrictions or warnings in the SmPC were considered necessary.

Furthermore, it is noted that the LoDoCo and LoDoCo 2 studies involved patients with CAD and constitute the principal clinical evidence supporting the proposed indication, and that colchicine was administered concomitantly with other cardiovascular therapies in these trials.

The Applicant has adequately justified the applicability of the LoDoCo and LoDoCo2 findings to the European clinical setting. Although LoDoCo was conducted in Australia, the patient population, background therapy and study design are considered representative of contemporary EU practice.

With regard to the mechanism of action, the applicant acknowledged the absence of hsCRP measurements in LoDoCo2 study and provided external evidence from randomised trials and metaanalyses demonstrating reductions in hsCRP and other inflammatory biomarkers with low-dose colchicine. Coronary plaque imaging data from sub-studies and external trials further support a plaquestabilising, anti-inflammatory effect. While these mechanistic and imaging data are supportive rather than confirmatory, they, together with the extensive literature on this subject, were considered sufficient to reinforce the biological plausibility of the observed clinical benefits.

Overall, both LoDoCo and LoDoCo 2 trials showed that long term use of colchicine 0.5 mg daily was effective in secondary prevention of a composite outcome of cardiovascular events, in patients with stable CAD for at least 6 months, when used along with standard therapies like statins, antiplatelet drugs like aspirin, betablockers and other interventional methods.

Both, LoDoCo and LoDoCo2 trials included a low number of female patients, who accounted for only 23% and 15.3% of the trial population, respectively. The enrolment of women in these trials was not proportional to the prevalence of cardiovascular disease among women in the general population, which indicates an inadequate inclusion of women in these trials. This is relevant, given that there have been reported pathophysiologic differences between men and women, and worse outcomes among women with cardiovascular disease.

In LoDoCo 2 that included only patients from Australia and the Netherlands, a prespecified subgroup analysis, showed a greater treatment effect among patients in Australia than in the Netherlands. The result for primary outcome was homogenous among subgroups except for a significant interaction between geographical regions: Australia vs  Netherlands (hazard ratios of 0.51 95%CI (0.39-0.67), approx. 1900 patients and 0.92 95%CI (0.71-1.20), respectively, approx. 3600 patients). According to subgroup analysis, there was a numerical increase in risk of primary outcome in patients with stage 3a renal failure. A warning regarding renal function monitoring is included in section 4.4 of the SmPC. Patients with any degree of renal impairment should be monitored closely for adverse reactions of colchicine. The applicant presented possible explanations for this geographical difference. Namely, the applicant analysed the differences in baseline characteristics in the Dutch subgroup vs Australian patients: a higher incidence of smoking (15.7% vs. 4.2%), a higher incidence of stage 3a chronic kidney disease (7.9% vs. 1.1%), a higher incidence of prior ACS (86% vs 80%) or a greater use of anticoagulants (14.3% vs. 8.1%) (Table S4 Supplementary Appendix NEJM.org) and discussed if they may have had any effect on outcome.  If so, such variables could influence the decision to administer colchicine for reducing CV events in patients with stable coronary disease. The apparent regional difference observed in LoDoCo2 (Australia versus the Netherlands) were discussed in the context of different follow-up duration and limited statistical robustness in subgroups, with sensitivity and landmark analyses showing convergence of treatment effects when follow-up was aligned. In the Netherlands cohort, patients were slightly younger and included a higher proportion of females and current smokers, a higher proportion of patients with stage 3a renal dysfunction compared to Australian cohort. The follow-up time of the Australian cohort was substantially longer compared with the follow-up time of the Dutch cohort, but that in itself does not explain the differences found in treatment effect. The difference in effect was not explained by any of the differences in colchicine products or clinical characteristics in the two regions. This issue was considered a chance finding; however, these explanations do not remove the remaining uncertainty regarding the external validity and robustness of the effect estimate across populations.

The findings of the neutral effect found in the Dutch subpopulation align with the neutral results from the recent CLEAR-SYNERGY study and questioned the robustness of the results from LoDoCo2 study in the context of the total population across the CV risk continuum of the secondary prevention setting. The applicant has included initially a limited number of studies in the application which lacked a broad perspective on the effect of colchicine in the secondary prevention of CV events. During the procedure, the applicant provided a broader perspective on colchicine use in secondary prevention of CV events by discussing two recent meta-analyses (Samuel et al, 2025 and d'Entremont et al, 2025) and several individual studies included in these meta-analyses . In particular, the results of CLEAR SYNERGY study were discussed.

The initially applied indication stated that 'Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma is indicated to reduce the risk of MI, stroke, coronary revascularization, and CV death in adult patients with established atherosclerotic disease or with multiple risk factors for cardiovascular disease.' Considering that the main data supporting efficacy were the clinical trials LoDoCo and LoDoCo 2, that included patients with stable CAD for at least 6 months, the applicant agreed to change the applied indication to reflect the inclusion criteria of the pivotal studies. Also, 'established atherosclerotic disease' could be an ambiguous term as patients with acute coronary events could be included in this definition. Limited data was initially submitted regarding the use of colchicine in patients with acute phase of CAD disease, namely there was no reference to the 'Colchicine and Spironolactone in Patients with Myocardial Infarction/SYNERGY Stent (CLEAR SYNERGY) (OASIS 9) trial (Jolly SS, NEJM 2024).

The CLEAR SYNERGY study was a multicentre RCT in which patients who had a MI were randomised as soon as possible after their MI to receive colchicine 0,5 mg or placebo.  The primary efficacy outcome was a composite of death from CV causes, recurrent MI, stroke, or unplanned ischemia-driven coronary revascularization, evaluated in a time-to-event analysis. A total of 7062 patients at 104 centers in 14 countries underwent randomization. A primary-outcome event occurred in 322 of 3528 patients (9.1%) in the colchicine group and 327 of 3534 patients (9.3%) in the placebo group over a median follow-up period of 3 years (hazard ratio, 0.99; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.85 to 1.16; P = 0.93). The investigators concluded that among patients who had MI, treatment with colchicine, when started soon after MI and continued for a median of 3 years, did not reduce the incidence of the composite primary outcome (death from CV causes, recurrent MI, stroke, or unplanned ischemiadriven coronary revascularization). Even if the patients included in this trial had experienced a very recent MI, the median duration of follow up was 3 years which indicated that this population could be relevant in the context of the proposed target population for colchicine. Therefore, the proposed antiinflammatory effects of colchicine on plaque biology, would also be expected to have been translated into a clinical benefit in the CLEAR-SYNERGY study population.

A more comprehensive discussion concerning these aspects was provided, comparing populations, concomitant treatments, time to events, distribution of events, or other potential explanations for the conflicting results. The apparent discrepancy between positive results of LoDoCo2 study and neutral results of CLEAR-SYNERGY study could be explained by differences in clinical setting, timing of intervention, and limitations affecting the conduct, enrolment patterns and interpretability of CLEARSYNERGY. CLEAR-SYNERGY enrolled patients in the acute phase following MI, with the majority presenting with STEMI. This represents a different biological and clinical context compared with stable CAD population in LoDoCo 2 study. The CLEAR study was partly performed during the COVID-19 pandemic which might have affected the results. Also, the biological rationale for colchicine treatment for prevention of CV events was further discussed and although the mechanism of action is not fully understood, the rationale was accepted given the totality of evidence available to date with the use of colchicine in patient with stable CAD.

Following the oral explanation and written responses, the agreed therapeutic indication is: 'Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma is indicated for secondary prevention of atherothrombotic events in adult patients with coronary disease stable for at least 6 months.' This restricted wording reflects the directly relevant LoDoCo2 population studied, and it is considered acceptable.

LoDoCo2 remains the pivotal and most directly relevant trial for the indication in stable coronary disease for at least 6 months. CLEAR-SYNERGY remains a relevant neutral study and introduces residual uncertainty; however, differences in clinical setting and timing, and plausible but not definitively demonstrated pandemic-related, adherence and operational factors, mean its findings do not invalidate the positive and directly applicable LoDoCo2 results. A balanced justification concerning the differences between both studies was accepted for the claimed treatment effect in the proposed target population, including comparison of populations, background therapies, time to events and other plausible explanations for the divergent findings across disease stages.

## 2.5.8. Clinical safety

The Applicant did not conduct any safety studies for Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma 0.5 mg tablets, but relies on LoDoCo and LoDoCo2 clinical trials, the available reports for the EU reference medicinal product - Colchicine Tiofarma 0.5 mg, tablets, the authorised in the US medicinal product Colcrys 0.6mg as well as other published literature. The Applicant states that the safety of long-term colchicine therapy in gout and FMF is well documented. No specific primary safety concerns have been identified for colchicine, as this medicinal product has been available for the treatment of gout for over 70 years and its safety profile has been well documented in patients with this disease when used at doses up to 6 mg in the course of treatment. The Applicant further argues that, since colchicine is proposed to be administered at the low dose of 0.5 mg once daily, patient exposure is limited and the probability of adverse events would be lower than with the established maximum daily doses of 1.2mg (gout) and 2.8mg (FMF).

LoDoCo (Nidorf 2013) was an observer-blinded clinical trial conducted in Australia, which 532 clinically stable patients with coronary disease proven by angiography, were randomised to receive either colchicine 0.5 mg/day or no colchicine, with a median follow-up of 3 years. Neither the submitted dossier nor Nidorf 2013 describe how safety data were collected or reported in this study. The only reported safety outcomes are related to patient deaths and side effects resulting in withdrawals. No other adverse events are described. The trial protocol was not included in the dossier. It is not clear if the trial followed GCP. The sponsor stated  that serious adverse events reported in LoDoCo were minimal. No serious adverse events were reported. It is extremely unlikely that no serious adverse events occurred to the 532 high-CV-risk participants during the median follow-up of 3 years, except for the reported deaths. It therefore appears that Serios Adverse Events (SAE) were either not collected or not reported. This was initially not recognised by the applicant.

LoDoCo2 (Nidorf 2020) was a double-blind placebo-controlled clinical trial which randomised a total of 5522 clinically stable patients with coronary disease proven by angiography, to either colchicine 0.5 mg/day or no colchicine, with a median follow-up of 28 months, conducted in Australia and the Netherlands. According to the LoDoCo2 protocol, patients were followed via a clinical visit or telephone call every six months. However, no predefined safety assessment procedures were specified: renal and haematology assessments were not included in the protocol, on the basis that these were considered as part of standard clinical management. Other safety assessments were also lacking.

Additionally, the protocol specified mandatory reporting only for subset of events, namely 'Non serious adverse events of special leading to discontinuation of Investigational Product' and 'Non serious AEs of special interest, in light of colchicine safety, like deterioration of renal function'. The protocol does not indicate whether all serious adverse events irrespective of nature were systematically collected and reported to the sponsor. Nor is it clear which events were considered of special interest. Particularly, the incidence of renal function deterioration, which is used as an example of AE of special interest, is not described in the research article. Notably, the data presented reports only a limited, pre-specified subset of outcomes/events and does not provide a complete tabulation of SAEs (e.g., overall SAE rates and SOC/PT breakdown). Considering the sample size and duration of the trial, it is not credible that all SAEs of any type/ or nature were fully captured in the submitted reporting.

As the collection of safety data in LoDoCo2 study was not complete, according to the study protocol, it hampers the assessment of the risks with colchicine treatment in the target population. In addition, LoDoCo2 included a 30-day open-label run in period before randomisation, during which subjects with repeated intolerance to colchicine, e.g. gastro-intestinal intolerance were excluded. Notwithstanding, the safety profile of colchicine in the treatment of subjects with gout and/or autoimmune diseases such as Familiar Mediterranean Fever and Behcets disease is very well established. The applicant was asked to discuss whether the known safety profile in the approved indications may be extrapolated to a population with chronic cardiovascular disease. Furthermore, the applicant was requested to provide a comprehensive safety analysis, including clear description of the of AE/SAE collection and reporting procedures used in LoDoCo and LoDoCo2, and justify the selective reporting criteria used in these trials.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The applicant's response confirmed lack of access to primary trial data and reiterates that low-dose colchicine has a long history of use in approved indications and refers to published cardiovascular trials, but does not adequately address the core concerns raised regarding incomplete safety data collection, selective reporting, and the generalisability of the safety profile to a chronic CV population. The applicant has not initially provided evidence to support the claim that the safety profile of low-dose colchicine in the CV setting is similar to that observed in gout and FMF populations. Importantly, the applicant stated that SAEs meeting ICH-GCP criteria were subject to standard reporting practices but does not address results on serious adverse events for LoDoCo trials (overall SAE rates, SOC/PT tabulation). Overall, the safety database remained incomplete and was in-depth discussed during the procedure.

The safety dataset derived from LoDoCo2 presents methodological limitations, including the 30-day open-label run-in period excluding colchicine-intolerant patients and the limited comprehensiveness of safety data collection.

The pooled safety analysis included cardiovascular trials with populations sufficiently similar to the proposed indication; among trials with adequate overall SAE reporting, no excess in serious adverse events was identified with colchicine 0.5 mg daily in the long-term setting.

The summary of SAE data from approved indications (pericarditis, gout, FMF and Behçet's disease), although mostly based on episodic treatment settings, is consistent with the absence of colchicinerelated serious toxicity at therapeutic doses.

Residual uncertainties, including non-cardiovascular mortality, long-term safety beyond 28 months, safety in patients aged ≥ 75 years and drug-drug-interaction-related toxicity, were addressed during the procedure. A recommendation was added to section 4.2 of the SmPC that colchicine can be safely used for a period of 28 months. Safety of colchicine will be further monitored via routine pharmacovigilance and PSUR assessments. This approach was considered adequate.

Despite the trials providing a considerable follow-up period, the applicant does not offer any long-term safety data. Given that colchicine is intended for long-term use, the applicant was requested to present and discuss a comprehensive safety analysis on long-term risks.

With the D120 response, the applicant provided a descriptive overview of exposure duration and selected safety outcomes in main CV trials. This is relevant background but does not provide the requested comprehensive long-term safety analysis. ## 3. Benefit-risk balance

## 3.1. Therapeutic context

Colchicine is an anti-inflammatory agent used for decades, primarily to treat familial mediterranean fever (FMF) and gout, both chronic inflammatory diseases. The EU birth date for the active substance is 30/07/1947. The EU reference medicinal product, Colchicine Tiofarma 0.5 mg tablets have been approved in the EU since 1998 and there is broad experience with these tablets.

In recent years, several clinical studies have been published investigating the use of colchicine in the treatment of cardiovascular (CV) diseases. Although the mechanism of action of colchicine in the prevention of major CV events is not well understood, it is postulated that its action is attributed to tubulin disruption, leading to subsequent down-regulation of multiple inflammatory pathways and modulation of innate immunity.

Current application for Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma 0.5 mg tablets was submitted under article 10(3) - hybrid application for the following indication:

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

'Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma is indicated for secondary prevention of atherothrombotic events in adult patients with coronary disease stable for at least 6 months.' The EU reference medicinal product for this application is Colchicine Tiofarma 0.5 mg tablets.

This application is considered acceptable from quality perspective.

No new nonclinical studies have been provided within the current application but an adequate summary of the available literature was presented and considered sufficient.

From a clinical perspective, this application does not contain sponsor-conducted studies on the pharmacokinetics (except for two bioequivalence studies) and pharmacodynamics or the efficacy and safety. The Clinical Overview is based on information from published literature, in particular LoDoCo and LoDoCo2 studies, and this is considered sufficient. The majority of published studies used colchicine formulations available in Europe (Colchicine Tiofarma), Australia (Colgout) and the United States (Colcrys). The product administered in LoDoCo study was Colgut 500 microgram tablets while products administered in LoDoCo2 study were Colchicine Tiofarma 0.5 mg tablets and Colgut 500 microgram tablets.

## 3.2. Main studies

To support the application and bridge to one of the products used in the LoDoCo and LoDoCo2 studies, the applicant submitted two bioequivalence studies. The bioequivalence studies were conducted under fasting (294-20) and fed (295-20) conditions as two open label, balanced, randomized two-treatment, two-sequence two-period, cross-over, single-dose, oral bioequivalence studies in healthy, adult, human subjects. The study design was considered adequate to evaluate the bioequivalence of Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma 0.5 mg tablets and Colgout 500 microgram tablets and was in line with the respective EMA bioequivalence guidelines (CPMP/EWP/QWP/1401/98 Rev. 1/ Corr **). The choice of dose, sampling points, overall sampling time as well as wash-out period were adequate. The analytical method was validated. Pharmacokinetic and statistical methods applied were appropriate.

The test formulation of Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma 0.5 mg tablets met the protocol-defined criteria for bioequivalence when compared with the Colgout 500 mcg tablets. The point estimates and their 90% confidence intervals for the parameters AUC0-t, AUC0∞, and Cmax were all contained within the protocol-defined acceptance range of 80.00 to 125.00%. Bioequivalence of the Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma 0.5 mg tablets and Colgout 500 microgram tablets was demonstrated.

As the efficacy of colchicine in the proposed indication was supported by results from published clinical studies with the use of Colchicine Tiofarma, Colgout and Colcrys, the applicant compared the composition of the Colchicine Tiofarma tablets (administered in LoDoCo 2), Colgout tablets (administered in LoDoCo, LoDoCo 2) and Colcrys tablets with the proposed product, Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma. All tablets have a similar composition and contain similar excipients. Furthermore, all are immediate release conventional tablets and claimed to be interchangeable. Comparative dissolution data was provided to justify bridging the reference products with the to be marketed medicinal product, Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma 0.5 mg tablets. All above listed products, including Colchicine AGEPHA, showed dissolution similarity as all dissolved more than 85% within 15 minutes in water and three media with different pH (i.e. pH 1.2, 4.5 and 6.8).

The pivotal data to support efficacy comes from two investigator-driven studies conducted in Australia (LoDoCo) and in Australia and the Netherlands (LoDoCo2). The LoDoCo study (Nidorf, et al. JACC vol. 61,4 (2013): 404-410. doi:10.1016/j.jacc.2012.10.027) may be considered as 'proof of concept' with LoDoCo2 study (N Engl J Med 2020; 383:1838-1847, DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa2021372) as a main study supporting the agreed indication.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

LoDoCo study was completed more than 10 years ago with the limitations of a small sample size and the single blind design, and therefore it was considered as supportive in the current application.

The LoDoCo2 study was randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentred and included a total of 5522 patients aged 35 to 82 years with angiographically or CT-confirmed CAD who were stable for at least 6 months with 84% of patients having a prior ACS event and 89% having prior revascularisation. Total of 2 762 patients were assigned to the colchicine group and 2 760 to the placebo group who underwent median follow-up of 28.6 months. An open-label run-in period already excluded a substantial proportion of patients (15.4%) due to intolerance or patient preference.

The primary efficacy endpoint was the time from randomization to the first occurrence of a composite of CV death, spontaneous MI, ischemic stroke, or ischemia driven coronary revascularization (4-point MACE). Secondary endpoints included a composite of CV, spontaneous MI, or ischemic stroke.

Colchicine significantly reduced the risk of the primary composite endpoint (4-point MACE) compared with placebo (n=187 [6.8] vs n=264 [9.6%], HR 0.69; 95% CI 0.57-0.83; p&lt;0.001). Colchicine also significantly lowered the incidence of several key secondary outcomes, including 3-point MACE endpoint (HR 0.72 [95%CI 0.57-0.92; p = 007]), ischemia-driven coronary revascularization (HR 0.67), MI (HR 0.70), ischaemic stroke (0.66). No statistically significant differences were observed for all-cause mortality, venous thromboembolism, atrial fibrillation, or new-onset diabetes.

Comprehensive explanation of how concomitant preventive therapies included in the LoDoCo2 protocol may have influenced the study outcome were provided. LoDoCo2 was designed to evaluate the additional benefit of colchicine on top of standard secondary CV prevention therapy, and all evidencebased treatments, such as statins, antiplatelets, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, beta-blockers and others were allowed and widely used. These therapies were well balanced between treatment groups through randomisation, reducing the risk that background treatment differences could influence the study results. The anti-inflammatory mechanism of action of colchicine supports the conclusion that its effect is complementary to, rather than overlapping with, other CV preventive therapies. The treatment benefit of colchicine was consistent across major background therapies, with no evidence of clinically meaningful effect modification. The available evidence does not support restricting the indication to a biomarker-defined subpopulation.

The benefits were not replicated across all subgroups in the primary analysis. The result for primary outcome was homogenous among subgroups except for a significant interaction between geographical regions: Australia vs Netherlands (hazard ratios of 0.51 95%CI (0.39-0.67), and 0.92 95%CI (0.711.20), respectively). Possible explanations for this geographical difference were analysed: a higher incidence of smoking (15.7% vs. 4.2%), a higher incidence of stage 3a chronic kidney disease (7.9% vs. 1.1%), a higher incidence of prior acute coronary syndrome ([ACS], 86% vs 80%) or a greater use of anticoagulants (14.3% vs. 8.1%). This was also discussed in the context of differences in follow-up duration (substantially longer in Australian cohort) and limited statistical robustness of analysis in subgroups, with sensitivity analyses showing convergence of treatment effects when follow-up was aligned. The difference in effect was not explained by any of the differences in colchicine product or clinical characteristics in the two regions and the issue was considered a chance finding. The justification concerning the neutral effect found in the Dutch subpopulation of LoDoCo 2 was accepted considering the results from LoDoCo2 study in the entire population with clinically relevant effect, as well as acceptable magnitude of effect, in a population of chronic CAD patients stable for at least 6 months.

The positive results from the LODOCO 2 trial were discussed in the context of the conflicting results from the most recent study CLEAR-SYNERGY (N Engl J Med. 2025 Feb 13;392(7):633-642. doi: 10.1056/NEJMoa2405922) where colchicine 0.5 mg once daily was compared with placebo in patients in the acute phase after MI. In this study, among patients with acute MI, colchicine did not reduce the incidence of the composite primary outcome (CV death, recurrent MI, stroke, or unplanned ischemiadriven coronary revascularization) over a median treatment duration of 3 years as compared with placebo. A comprehensive discussion concerning these aspects was provided, comparing populations, concomitant treatments, time to events, distribution of events, or other potential explanations for the conflicting results. The apparent discrepancy between LoDoCo2 and CLEAR-SYNERGY can be explained by differences in clinical setting, timing of intervention, and limitations affecting the conduct, enrolment patterns of CLEAR-SYNERGY. The study enrolled patients in the acute phase following MI, with the majority presenting with ST-elevation MI. This represents a different biological and clinical context compared with stable CAD population in LoDoCo 2 study. The CLEAR study was partly performed during the COVID-19 pandemic which might have affected the results. Also, the biological rationale for colchicine treatment for prevention of CV events was further discussed and although the mechanism of action is not fully understood, the rationale was accepted given the totality of evidence available to date with colchicine in patient with stable CAD.

A justification for extrapolation of safety data from the approved indications to the proposed CV population was provided as well as a discussion concerning potential differences in baseline characteristics, comorbidities and concomitant medications that may impact safety. This was considered acceptable as well as discussion concerning impact of run-in period on the external validity of the safety data. The applicant provided a comprehensive safety analysis, that comprised of a comprehensive summary of SAE data from long-term colchicine use in all its approved indications, including overall SAE rates and SAE rates by Preferred Term, and a pooled safety analysis from studies of colchicine in CV populations, derived from a systematic literature review with a prespecified and justified search and selection strategy. The pooled safety analysis included CV trials with populations sufficiently similar to the proposed indication, and among those with adequate overall SAE reporting. No excess in SAEs was identified with colchicine 0.5 mg daily in long term settings. The summary of SAE data from the approved indications, across pericarditis, gout, FMF and Behçet's disease, while mostly in the setting of episodic treatments, was consistent with the absence of colchicine related serious toxicity at therapeutic doses. Regarding residual uncertainties, the applicant identified non-cardiovascular mortality, long-term safety beyond 28 months, safety in patients ≥ 75 years, and drug-drug interaction-related toxicity as residual uncertainties and committed to their evaluation through routine pharmacovigilance and PSUR-based review. This approach was considered adequate. The previously identified limitations in the completeness and transparency of safety data collection have been addressed sufficiently. The pooled cardiovascular safety analysis and the summary of long-term colchicine use in approved indications do not identify an excess in SAEs with colchicine 0.5 mg daily.

From a pharmacovigilance and risk management perspective, it was considered that the safety profile of colchicine is well characterised and that no new safety concerns specific to the proposed indication have been identified. The known risks, including gastrointestinal intolerance, myelosuppression, neuromuscular toxicity, drug-drug interactions and overdose-related toxicity, are adequately described in the Product Information and managed through routine risk minimisation measures. The CHMP, having considered the data submitted in the application and available on the chosen reference medicinal product, is of the opinion that no additional risk minimisation activities are required beyond those included in the product information.

## 3.2. Benefit-risk conclusions

Overall, the available efficacy, safety and quality data support a favourable benefit-risk balance for Colchicine AGEPHA Pharma 0.5 mg tablets for the secondary prevention of atherothrombotic events in adult patients with coronary disease stable for at least 6 months. The indication wording, the limitations of the available safety database, and the remaining uncertainties have been adequately reflected in the Product Information and Risk Management Plan.

Considering the totality of the clinical evidence, LoDoCo2 is the pivotal and most directly relevant trial for the proposed indication. This study demonstrated a clinically meaningful reduction in major cardiovascular events on top of contemporary standard of care. CLEAR-SYNERGY remains a relevant neutral study and introduces residual uncertainty, but the applicant has provided sufficient justification that its findings do not invalidate the positive results of LoDoCo2 in the restricted stable coronary disease population.

Having considered the data submitted in the application and available for the chosen reference medicinal product, no additional risk minimisation activities are required beyond those included in the product information. Routine pharmacovigilance is considered adequate to address residual uncertainties including non-cardiovascular mortality, long-term safety beyond 28 months, safety in patients ≥ 75 years and drug-drug-interaction-related toxicity.

A critical review of acute cardiovascular disease data has been provided and was considered acceptable for the stable coronary disease indication. The latest revised indication, for secondary prevention of atherothrombotic events in adult patients with coronary disease stable for at least 6 months, appropriately reflects the population studied in LoDoCo2 study.

The safety dataset derived from LoDoCo2 presented methodological limitations, including the 30-day open-label run-in period excluding colchicine-intolerant patients and the limited comprehensiveness of safety data collection. A strengthened justification for extrapolation of safety data from approved indications to the proposed CV population was provided as well as the pooled safety analysis from cardiovascular trials with populations sufficiently similar to the proposed indication, and among those with adequate overall SAE reporting. No excess in SAEs was identified with colchicine 0.5 mg daily in long term settings. The summary of SAE data from the approved indications, across pericarditis, gout, FMF and Behçet's disease, while mostly in the setting of episodic treatments, was consistent with the absence of colchicine related serious toxicity at therapeutic doses. The Applicant's pooled cardiovascular safety analysis and cross-indication SAE summary support the absence of a serious adverse event excess with colchicine 0.5 mg daily in the long-term setting. Reproductive toxicity has been reassessed based on updated human and post-marketing data.
