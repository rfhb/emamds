## 2.3. Clinical aspects

## 2.3.1. Introduction

The MAH submitted a final report(s) for the study:

· YKP3089C025 : A Randomized, Double-blind, Placebo-controlled, Multicentre Study to Evaluate the Efficacy and Safety of Cenobamate Adjunctive Therapy in Subjects with Primary Generalized TonicClonic Seizures

## 2.3.2. Clinical study

YKP3089C025: A Randomized, Double-blind, Placebo-controlled, Multicentre Study to Evaluate the Efficacy and Safety of Cenobamate Adjunctive Therapy in Subjects with Primary Generalized Tonic-Clonic Seizures

## Description

The study was a multicentre, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group, adjunctive therapy study in patients with PGTC seizures, followed by an opportunity to enrol in an open label extension (OLE) study, YKP3089C033.

This study incorporates study design and efficacy endpoints utilized for the studies of previously approved ASMs in PGTC seizures (such as lacosamide, levetiracetam, lamotrigine, topiramate, and perampanel) according to applicable regulatory guidance documents.

## Methods

## Bioanalytical method

The same method discussed in the initial application was used for the analytical determination of cenobamate in human plasma. The calibration curve comprised 8 levels of nonzero cenobamate standards, ranging from 0.0500 μg/mL to 40.0 μg/mL. Between 24 Sep 2019 and 29 May 2025, samples were received at Worldwide and stored at approximately -20 °C. A total of 544 samples were analyzed between 26-Sep-2019 and 05-Jun-2025. 2254 days passed between the date of first sample collection (4-Apr-2019) to the last sample analysis. The established long-term stability of 2729 days at -20 °C for Cenobamate in human K2-EDTA plasma covers the period of study sample storage. ISR was performed on 55 samples (&gt;10% of the total samples) with 100% complying with the acceptance criteria.

## Study participants

The enrolled population included patients with a clinical diagnosis of PGTC seizures (with or without other subtypes of generalized seizures) in the setting of IGE and had at least 5 PGTC seizures in 12 weeks during the Pre-Randomization Period.

## Treatments

Cenobamate 200 mg dose (or the adolescent equivalent) vs Placebo.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The dose that was selected for the study YKP3089C025 was based on population PK (popPK) analysis of the PK data obtained from the paediatric PK and safety study, YKP3089C039. PK data was collected in the study YKP3089C025 to summarize individual data, as well as attempt popPK and exposure-response analysis.

## Objective(s)

The primary objective was to demonstrate the efficacy of adjunctive cenobamate 200 mg dose (or the adolescent equivalent) compared with placebo on PGTC seizures in patients ≥ 12 years of age.

The secondary objectives were:

- To evaluate the safety and tolerability of cenobamate in patients with PGTC seizures
- To evaluate the seizure freedom rate for PGTC seizures during the Maintenance Phase and during the entire Double-blind Treatment Period.
- To evaluate the effect of cenobamate on other types of generalized seizures.
- To investigate the PK of cenobamate in patients with PGTC seizures.

## Outcomes/endpoints

The primary endpoint was chosen and it was adjusted to region per local regulatory expectations:

- USA and rest of world: median percent change from baseline in PGTC seizure frequency per 28day interval during the Double-blind Treatment Period.
- Europe, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand: 50% responder rate for PGTC seizures during the Maintenance Phase. A responder was defined as a patient who achieved at least a 50% reduction in PGTC seizure frequency per 28-day interval during the Maintenance Phase relative to baseline.

The secondary efficacy endpoints were:

- 100% responder rates for PGTC seizures during the Maintenance Phase relative to baseline.
- 75% responder rates for PGTC seizures during the Maintenance Phase relative to baseline.
- Percentage of patients who achieved a 50% reduction in all generalized seizure frequency per 28-day interval during the Maintenance Phase relative to baseline.
- The secondary safety endpoints included AEs and concomitant medication reporting, clinical laboratory results, vital signs, ECGs, physical examinations, neurologic examinations, and CSSRS results. which are summarized using descriptive statistics.

Additionally, there were PK focused endpoints:

- Summary of individual patients' plasma concentrations of cenobamate at their respective doses and visits.
- PopPK analysis performed on data available from this study alone or integrated with data from other cenobamate studies. A PopPK/PD E-R analysis may be conducted as a standalone approach for PGTC or in combination with POS data, if deemed appropriate.

## Results

## Participant flow

One hundred and sixty-eight (168) randomized patients who received at least 1 dose of cenobamate and had at least 1 post-baseline efficacy measurement, were included in the MITT and Safety Populations.

Out of 168 patients in the MITT Population, 161 (95.3%) had at least one dose of cenobamate during the Maintenance Phase and had at least one efficacy measurement in the Maintenance Phase, these were defined as MITT-M population. The number (%) of patients who completed the Double-blind Treatment Period was 154 (91.7%) overall; 79 (92.9%) in the cenobamate group and 75 (90.4%) in the placebo group. Of these, 147 patients (87.5%) were enrolled in the OLE.

89 (52.7%) patients in the Randomized Population who received at least one dose of cenobamate during the Double-Blind Treatment Period had at least one other type of generalized seizure at baseline and at least one efficacy measurement (for other types of generalized seizure) during the Double-blind Treatment Period (this constituted the MITT Population for other types of generalized seizures). 84 (49.7%) had at least one efficacy measurement (other types of generalized seizure) in the Maintenance Phase and constituted the MITT-M Population for Other Types of Generalized Seizure.

There was a total of 34 (20.1%) patients aged 12 to &lt; 18 years in the adolescent group, of whom 18 were randomised to cenobamate group and 16 were randomised to placebo.

## Recruitment

Overall, 140 patients were enrolled in Europe, of which, 29 were adolescents, sixteen (16) in cenobamate group and 13 in placebo group.

## Safety results

At least one TEAE was reported for 51 patients (60.0%) in the cenobamate group and 44 patients (53.0%) in the placebo group during the Double-blind Treatment Period. The majority of TEAES were mild to moderate and the frequency slightly higher in the cenobamate group in comparison to the placebo group. There were no subjects with TEAEs leading to treatment discontinuation in the cenobamate group and 1 (1.2%) in the placebo group. Serious Adverse events (SAEs) were reported for 5 patients (5.9%) in the cenobamate group, and 1 patient (1.2%) in the placebo group during the Double-blind Treatment.

The three most common side effects were CNS related. Somnolence was reported in 14 (16.5%) patients, fatigue in 9 (10.6%) patients, and dizziness in 7 (8.2%) patients in the cenobamate group, compared to 5 (6.0%), 0, and 4 (4.8%) patients in the placebo group, respectively.

A similar proportion of adolescents compared to the overall population, reported at least one TEAE during the double-blind period, 11 patients (61.1%) in the cenobamate group and 8 patients (50%) in the placebo group. All TEAES were mild to moderate. There were no subjects with TEAEs leading to treatment discontinuation in the cenobamate group and 1 (6.3 %) in the placebo group. Serious Adverse events (SAEs) were reported for 2 patients (11.1%) in the cenobamate group, and 0 in the placebo group.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The safety results in the adolescent population are in line with the safety results observed in the overall and adult population. There were no deaths during the study, no treatment-related SAEs in either group and no cases of

hypersensitivity reactions or DRESS syndrome

## PK results

A total of 34 adolescents aged 12 to &lt; 18 years (at the time of screening) were enrolled, which was approximately 20% of the total patient sample size. For adolescent patients, the dosing for oral suspension for the study was based on the patient body weight taken at Visit 4 and up to a dose of 3.0 mg/kg not exciding placebo on the maintenance phase. Sparse blood samples were collected during Visits 11 (Day 98) and 13 (Day 126) of the Maintenance Phase to determine plasma concentrations of cenobamate, with collections at pre-dose and a second PK sample any time before the end of the visit. All adolescent patients receiving cenobamate (n=17) had measurable cenobamate plasma concentrations for all analysed samples. At Visit 11, the median Css based on the pre-dose sample were 13.1 ug/mL and 12.1 ug/mL for the adolescent and adult patients, respectively. At Visit 11, the median Css based on the post-dose sample, as expected, were slightly higher - 16.1 ug/mL and 13.9 ug/mL for the adolescent and adult patients, respectively. At Visit 13, the median Css based on the pre-dose sample was 13.1 ug/mL and 11.6 ug/mL for the adolescent and adult patients, respectively. At Visit 13, the median Css based on the post-dose sample, again, as expected, were slightly higher - 15 ug/mL and 13.2 ug/mL for the adolescent and adult patients, respectively. The pre- and post-dose Css at Visits 11 and 13 were consistent with the long half-life of cenobamate, and exposures showed minimal fluctuations between pre- and post-dose Css values. The range of concentrations in the two groups is, basically, equal.

## 2.3.3. Discussion on clinical aspects

This was the first clinical study of cenobamate in patients with PGTC seizures.

Regarding the PK, the bioanalytical method validation was previously assessed and considered acceptable. The in study analysis was performed according to the protocol and its performance is considered acceptable. Based on the sparse cenobamate collection scheme, it was observed similar steady-state plasma concentrations in adult and adolescent patients with PGTC seizures, being thus reassuring. This data is to be included in a formal PopPK analysis not yet provided.

The study YKP3089C025 met its primary efficacy endpoints and demonstrated clinically relevant significant results for cenobamate in the overall population. There was a statistically higher proportion of responders (patients who achieved at least a 50% reduction in PGTC seizure frequency) and a significant higher reduction in median PGTC seizure frequency during the double-blind treatment period relative to baseline in patients treated with cenobamate 200 mg/day (or the adolescent dose equivalent). Additionally, treatment with cenobamate demonstrated a statistically significant difference

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

in higher response rates during the mantaiance phase, including seizure freedom (100% reduction in PGTC seizure frequency).

In the adolescent population, a trend for improvement in the cenobamate group was seen, although not reaching statistical significance which is hypothesized to be related to the small sample size of adolescents that were enrolled. The study was powered to evaluate the overall population and not the adolescent subgroup.

The safety results were aligned with the known safety profile of cenobamate having been generally well tolerated by patients 12 years of age and older, and with no new safety signals having been identified. The majority of TEAEs reported were mild to moderate in severity. The pattern and types of AEs reported are very similar to those previously observed in other RCTs as well as reported in the SmPC of cenobamate. Somnolence, fatigue and dizziness were the three more frequently reported adverse events. There were no deaths during the study, no treatment-related SAEs in either group and no cases of hypersensitivity reactions or DRESS syndrome.

The applicant believes that the results support the clinical potential of cenobamate for treating PGTC seizures in the pediatric population.

Cenobamate oral suspension is not a registered formulation in the European Union, no changes to the approved EU Product Information for Ontozry are proposed following the completion of this study. Angelini Pharma is submitting this study in accordance with Article 46 of the Paediatric Regulation.

## 3. CHMP's overall conclusion and recommendation

This was the first clinical study of cenobamate in patients with PGTC seizures. The MAH will not request an extension of indication to cover PGTC seizures in adults.

Cenobamate has no paediatric indication and the submitted study although pointing to an efficacy profile was not powered to assess it in adolescents and cannot be considered fully supportive of a paediatric indication.

Paediatric patients involved in the study were also treated with an oral formulation not approved in Europe.

Inclusion of data regarding paediatric use of cenobamate on the SmPC when no approved paediatric indication exists for this drug does not seem to be relevant. Additionally, the study was conducted with a formulation that is not approved/available in the EU market.

Finally, no new safety information was generated on top of the already existing one that could trigger interest in reporting it on the SmPC.

As such, it is agreed that no update to the SmPC is foreseen following availability of this study data.
