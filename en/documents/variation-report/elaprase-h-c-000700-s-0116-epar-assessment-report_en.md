## 2.2. Benefit-risk Balance

With the 17 th annual re-assessment, the MAH has submitted the final study report for the Hunter Outcome Survey (HOS), including data for the annual reporting period of 24 Jan 2023 through 23 Jan 2024, as well as 18 years of cumulative data.

The Hunter Outcome Survey was a MAH-sponsored, global, longitudinal, observational registry. It was established in 2005, with a minimum duration of 17 years (until September 2022). The purpose of the survey was to collect information on the natural history of Hunter syndrome and the long-term effectiveness and safety of enzyme replacement therapy with idursulfase (Elaprase) in real-world clinical practice. Specific obligations for Elaprase are addressed through the data collected in the HOS.

The 16th HOS Annual Report was submitted in March 2023 to address the outstanding SOBs (SO1 and SO5); the report provided cumulative data since the initiation of the HOS in 2005 and data for the previous annual reporting period of 24 Jan 2022 through 23 Jan 2023 for patients with MPS II. The MAH informed about the pursuit to close the HOS registry, with last patient's visit at 16 Feb 2023.

Patient enrolment in the HOS continuously increased over time. In total, 1332 patients were enrolled. These numbers are lower than reported in the last annual reassessment (n=1443 patients in the registry). This is due to the removal of duplicate patient records, exclusion from the final dataset because of missing signatures, forms or demographic data.

Over recent years the MAH has made several efforts to increase data quality and quantity in the HOS, including improved functionalities in the new database implemented in 2014 and more frequent training of clinical research associates and site staff. Despite this, no noteworthy improvements in data completeness have been seen. The MAH has developed a site-performance dashboard to evaluate data completeness at each of the sites participating in the HOS, gain insights into the challenges of collecting some data variables and encourage sites to enter the data needed in the HOS registry.

Within the annual HOS report, the MAH has evaluated and discussed the long-term clinical endpoints as required, including pulmonary and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, urinary GAG excretion patterns, antibody levels and correlation to other therapeutic parameters and immunogenicity after reexposure to idursulfase. Although there are limited number of patients with both data available at baseline and after 5-10 years of treatment, no robust conclusions can be drawn. Nevertheless, the assessment of these long-term clinical endpoints continues to be generally consistent with observations seen in previous annual assessments; there seems to be a trend for improvement under continued Elaprase treatment.

With closure of the HOS, the MAH concludes that SO1 and SO5 have been addressed sufficiently and to the extent possible. Considering the limitations (uncontrolled observational study, in which the amount of missing data is large and the number of patients with long-term follow-up data available is limited), data cannot be considered comprehensive. In line with the principles of a marketing authorisation under exceptional circumstances, it is not expected that the MAH will be able to generate comprehensive evidence in the future. SOB 1, the Hunter Outcome Survey, with the analysis of this final CSR, is considered fulfilled as this concerns the HOS registry. However, SOB 5 is considered not fulfilled. As long-term efficacy and safety is not confirmed and the marketing authorization under exceptional circumstances remains in place, monitoring of long-term efficacy and safety is required. Since the registry is closed, the MAH has revised the Annex II accordingly.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

In conclusion, during the period covered by this annual re-assessment, the new data that have emerged from the long-term clinical data continue to be generally consistent with observations seen in previous annual assessments. No new safety data have been identified.

The presented data does not impact the benefit-risk of Elaprase in the approved indication. The B/R remains favourable.

## CHMP comments:

In the current annual reassessment procedure, the MAH has submitted the final study report for the HOS registry study. Both outstanding SOs are linked to the HOS; SO1 the HOS analysis of the data and SO5 long term assessments of efficacy and safety. Therefore, both SOs will simultaneously be discussed in the assessment of the final CSR below.

## Hunter Outcome Survey (SOB 1) and Long-term treatment outcomes (SOB 5)

The Hunter Outcome Survey was a long-term, international, multicentre, observational, open-ended registry designed to document management and clinical outcome of patients with Hunter syndrome over time-based on real-world data. Data were collected to provide a spectrum of Hunter syndrome by age, treatment status, and disease status over time. Data could be collected prospectively or retrospectively (in the case of patients who are deceased).

All patient care and management were determined by the treating physician. All treatments and/or any changes considered necessary for patients' welfare were determined at the discretion of patients' physicians and were not provided or reimbursed as part of participation in the registry.

The HOS registry was open from 31 Oct 2005 to 16 Feb 2023 (the minimum duration of 17 years was met in September 2022) and was only to be discontinued upon agreement between the MAH and regulatory agencies. The MAH pursued closure of the HOS registry during the 16th annual reassessment. The last patient's last visit occurred on 16 Feb 2023. The final report is now being submitted with a request to evaluate whether the remaining SOs have been fulfilled.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## Study participants

The HOS was open to all patients with Hunter syndrome who were untreated or who were receiving or had received treatment with idursulfase, including patients who were alive at HOS enrolment (prospective patients) and patients who were deceased at HOS enrolment (historical patients).

Inclusion Criteria

- Diagnosis of Hunter syndrome (biochemically and/or genetically)
- Signed and dated written informed consent, as per either
- Prospective patients: signed and dated informed consent from the patient, for patients aged &lt;18 years (&lt;16 years in Scotland), parent and/or patient's LAR, and assent of the minor where applicable. Informed consent must be obtained from LARs for cognitively impaired patients.

OR

- Historical patients: signed and dated informed consent from the patient's LARs (where allowed by relevant individual country or site regulations/laws).

Exclusion Criteria

- Patients enrolled in an interventional clinical trial were not eligible. Patients could re-enrol once they completed or withdrew from the other clinical study.
- Patients receiving treatment for Hunter syndrome with an ERT product other than idursulfase were not eligible. Patient could enrol or re-enrol once they stopped treatment with another ERT.

## Treatments

The recommended dosage of idursulfase is 0.5 mg/kg weekly, administered via IV infusion.

## Objective(s)

The overall objective of HOS was to collect, analyse, and report the long-term safety and effectiveness data obtained through use of ERT with idursulfase and on the evolving nature of Hunter disease through biological and patient-, clinician-, or observer-reported outcomes.

The primary objective was to monitor the long-term safety and effectiveness of ERT with idursulfase in patients with Hunter syndrome.

The secondary objectives of the study were:

- To monitor the natural history of untreated patients with Hunter syndrome
- To assess the impact of Hunter syndrome on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) using the patient and parent-reported versions of the Hunter Syndrome-Functional Outcomes for Clinical Understanding Scale Questionnaire (HS-FOCUS)
- To monitor dosing regimens of idursulfase in patients with Hunter syndrome

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## Outcomes/endpoints

The primary endpoints to evaluate safety of ERT with idursulfase in patients with Hunter syndrome were:

- Occurrence of infusion-related reactions (IRRs) and other adverse events (AEs), including serious adverse events (SAEs)
- Immunogenicity as determined by time to first positive antibody response (antibody leveland isotype), antibody titer, isotype, and neutralizing antibodies (NAbs)

The primary endpoints to evaluate effectiveness of ERT with idursulfase in patients with Hunter syndrome were:

- Urinary GAG (uGAG) levels
- Growth parameters (height, weight and head circumference, and corresponding calculated Zscores)
- Distance walked in the 6-minute walk test (6MWT)
- Left ventricular mass index (LVMI; as calculated by echocardiography)
- Pulmonary function (as measured by forced expiratory volume in 1 second [FEV1] and forced vital capacity [FVC])
- Liver and spleen size (as estimated by palpation)
- The prevalence of cardiac- and pulmonary-related hospitalizations
- Deaths, including age at time of death and cause of death

The secondary endpoints were:

- Evaluation of signs and symptoms for the natural history of the disease
- Hepatosplenomegaly
- Central nervous system involvement
- Skeletal involvement
- Ear, nose, and throat signs and symptoms
- Pulmonary signs and symptoms
- Cardiac signs and symptoms
- Evaluation of dosing of idursulfase in patients with Hunter syndrome
- Prescribed dose levels
- Administered dose
- Total infusion time
- Missed infusions
- Reason for missed infusions
- Scoring in 5 domains in the patient- and parent-reported versions of the HS-FOCUS

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## Sample size

There was no predetermined sample size because this is an observational registry.

## Randomisation and blinding (masking)

There were no randomisation and blinding procedures.

## Statistical Methods

Postbaseline was defined as the closest measurement within a period ±3 months of the evaluation visit (eg, 1 year ±3 months after idursulfase, 2 years ±3 months after idursulfase). If there were 2 values in equal distance to the evaluation visit, the first value was used.

## CHMP comments:

The HOS included prospective and historical patients diagnosed with MPS II. The main objective of the HOS was to collect, analyse, and report the long-term safety and effectiveness data obtained through use of ERT with idursulfase.

Baseline is defined as the closest value to idursulfase start within a period of 12 months. This range of 12 months before or after is considered a relatively broad range. It is however understandable that in de clinical setting not all parameters are assessed during regular visits to the clinic. This broad range does enable greater data availability for a broader range of parameters.

Besides patients treated with idursulfase, also untreated MPS II patients were included into the registry who were deceased at HOS entry (n=143 in the Untreated Historical Patient Population (UHPP)) or alive at HOS entry (n=101 in the Untreated Patient Population (UPP)).

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The design of the study does not permit drawing firm conclusions on long-term efficacy and safety as this is an uncontrolled observational study. Cardiovascular- and pulmonary signs and symptoms and survival were also determined in the UHPP. However, a direct comparison between het UHPP and the TOP (treatment outcomes population) cannot be made as these are two different patient populations. Other clinical endpoints were not determined in the untreated patients.

In July 2012, the MAH implemented the \"HOS Data Completeness Project\" focused on sites known to have high numbers of patients using a contract research organization (CRO) to ensure that available data would be entered into the HOS database. The HOS Data Completeness Project was completed in January 2014. The principal objective of the project was to improve data collection and completeness in the short term and overall to optimize the value of HOS data. Sixteen sites participated in the project and medical records of 305 patients were reviewed, with available and relevant data entered.

The HOS Data Completeness Project resulted in an increase in quality of data entry for the projectspecific parameters. During the project, several limitations for obtaining a high level of data entry at sites became apparent: lack of existing data, limitations of local data entry staff, or simply that these assessments are not being performed at sites.

In May 2014, the HOS database was updated to increase user friendliness for the site staff and data entry vendors and also to enable better remote data querying, which in turn would also improve data completeness and quality. The new database, which was used by all sites, helped sites to identify the core data variables by underlining them and ensuring that they are placed at the top of all data entry forms. Several automatic edit checks were implemented, alerting staff to questionable entries at the time of data entry and thereby aiding the prompt correction of possible incorrect values. In 2014, database-specific \"worksheets\" were developed as a method to better facilitate sites' collection of HOS-specific data parameters in the main medical files and thus into the HOS database as well. More focused and frequent training and education on the importance of parameters to collect and report on in the new database were implemented for the CRAs, as well as for the local staff at HOS sites in 2014 to aid the data entry efforts. In 2017, in order to evaluate data completeness at each of the sites participating in the HOS, gain insights into the challenges of collecting some data variables, and encourage sites to enter the data needed in the HOS registry, the MAH developed a site-performance metrics dashboard. The dashboard calculated overall completeness for each site using the records for a selected subset of 13 core variables recorded at baseline and/or after baseline (Table 5). In the site performance dashboard, sites were ranked to indicate different tiers of site performance (80-100% completeness, 50-79%, and &lt;50%). The dashboard was used to generate a site performance report that was distributed to each site, so that each site could either focus on improving data completeness if necessary or give reasons for poor completeness for a particular variable. The first site performance report was distributed to the sites in December 2017. In 2019 and 2020, the MAH continued data completeness training. Data from patients who were untreated were also to be assessed for completeness, as part of an effort to match data from treated and untreated patients. However, because idursulfase has become the standard of care and most diagnosed patients are being started on treatment, the pool of untreated patients declined. Most of the data for untreated patients therefore had to be drawn from historical patients, and thus, the numbers of matched pairs were too low for this analysis to be performed. Many sites provided feedback to explain some of the reasons underlying low data completeness. In some countries, certain assessments may not be performed in routine practice or if not deemed clinically necessary. For example, tests such as the 6MWT and pulmonary function testing may not routinely be performed annually, or they are not performed in patients who are &lt;5 years of age or who have cognitive impairment. In some cases, patients may refuse assessments that are not covered by insurance. Assessment methods may also have differed. Additionally, it may not have been possible to perform some evaluations on patients who received home infusions. The sponsor followed up on this effort in 2021 with refresher training for the CRO, with a focus on genetic and cognitive impairment data. The MAH provided the CRO with a detailed tracker highlighting missing genetic and cognitive impairment data for each site to focus and improve continuing efforts. These measures resulted in increases compared to previous years in data completeness for active patients (those who had not yet completed an EXIT form): 463 active patients from 107 sites were included in the analysis. In July 2020, 55 sites had a completeness rated at 50-79%. By July 2021 this had increased to 58 sites. By Fall 2021, measures to increase data completeness by way of CRA and site trainings had been exhausted. While improvement in data completeness for active patients has been noted, it cannot be expected for historical patients and closed sites, for whom it has not been possible to retroactively obtain any further data. Although there was a slight improvement in data completeness after these measures were implemented, no noteworthy improvements in data completeness were observed. Changes to the electronic case report form (eCRF) were originally planned in an effort to simplify the database and improve the data entry process. However, updating the eCRF would not necessarily increase data completeness; it would only simplify the database while retaining the ability to meet post marketing commitments to the authorities. Moreover, with the closure of the HOS registry, it has been acknowledged by the EMA in the assessment report on the 15th annual reassessment that such updates would be ineffectual. Therefore, a decision was made not to proceed with the eCRF updates. As of the data cutoff for the 16th annual report, measures to increase data completeness by way of CRA and site trainings had been exhausted. With the registry closure, there were no ongoing efforts to improve data collection for the HOS registry.

## Urinary Glycosaminoglycans

In the pivotal Phase 2/3 efficacy study TKT024, decreases in uGAG values, a pharmacodynamic marker of disease, were the earliest signs of clinical improvement. Urinary GAG values fell within the reference range regardless of patient age, disease severity, and residual I2S activity; the greater the uGAG values at baseline, the greater the magnitude of decrease with idursulfase treatment. In the HOS data, uGAG values decreased at Year 1 and were sustained below baseline values at all time points. ## Assessment of long-term antibody levels, isotype and correlation to other therapeutic parameters

Blood samples for antibody determination are only taken if and when IRRs or hypersensitivity-type reactions occur during the idursulfase infusion.

Analysing samples for antibodies obtained only while an IRR is occurring and during an idursulfase infusion is likely to result in 2 issues:

- Ascertainment bias: if antibodies are only looked for during an IRR and not in the absence of an IRR, the data are likely to show that there is a relationship between IRRs and antibodies due to skewed collection
- Analysis errors: assays for anti-drug antibodies are best performed when there is no drug in the body.

For these reasons, in 2014 the HOS clinical protocol was amended to Version 5.0 to recommend that antibody samples be taken immediately before an idursulfase infusion (or as far removed as possible from a previous infusion) and at scheduled, predetermined times, eg, twice per year. Nonetheless, this recommendation could not be mandated due to the nature of the registry. Also, only a small number of sites routinely collected blood for immunogenicity analysis during a patient visit. Therefore, the antidrug antibody data available for analysis in HOS was scarce and analyses for persistent antidrug antibodies or by antidrug antibody or NAbs titres were not possible because immunogenicity data was not consistently entered into the HOS database or not collected at all (eg, titres).

It is well known from clinical practice that the 6MWT is dependent on effort, instruction, and clinic, so changes in distance walked should be interpreted with caution. The 6MWT is difficult to perform in children younger than 5 years of age and cognitively impaired patients with Hunter syndrome. In general, in patients with Hunter syndrome, growth and presumably, treatment with idursulfase seems to increase the distance walked. Any improvement in lung and cardiac function, or reduction in joint stiffness, may result in an increased walking distance. However, disease progression (even in the treated cohort) would have the opposite effect (ie, decrease in distance walked). It is not possible to distinguish between the effect of idursulfase and growth or to know to what extent the improvement over time is attributable to idursulfase versus other factors, due to the lack of a control group.

## Liver and spleen volumes

Only available liver and spleen size data assessed by palpation were summarized. Liver and spleen size assessments in patients with Hunter syndrome are problematic, especially in children. Quantitative imaging approaches such as magnetic resonance imaging may require sedation or anaesthesia and are therefore not performed frequently. Palpation of liver and spleen during clinical examination is difficult to perform in these patients due to hyperactivity and abnormal body habitus associated with skeletal abnormalities. Liver and spleen volume assessments (by imaging) were performed even less frequently in clinical practice than palpation; therefore, only sparse data are available on imaging in the HOS database.

## Joint range of movement

The joint range of motion results indicated shoulder flexion improvements at Year 1 through Year 3 and at Year 5 and shoulder abduction showed improvements at Year 2, Year 3, and Year 5. Shoulder extension showed improvements only at Year 3 and mild improvement at Year 2. External shoulder rotation showed improvements at Year 2 and Year 3, and internal shoulder rotation worsened at Year 2 through Year 5. In general, the patient numbers in the Year 4 and Year 5 assessment groups are limited, and it is, therefore, difficult to determine any clinical relevance. Additionally, because there are no longitudinal follow-up data of patients within the TOP and because there are no similar data on the contribution of progression of the disease on these parameters, a clinical determination is difficult to make based on these data.

## Growth velocity (z-scores) before and after ERT start

A major feature of patients with MPS II is a marked reduction in linear growth, and short stature is a prominent clinical feature of this condition. The effect of idursulfase on growth velocity as measured by the change in height was assessed in the paediatric HOS population.

Height z-scores are presented before and after initiating ERT in a subset of males in the TOP who started ERT between 8 and 15 years of age, inclusive. This age group was selected because in MPSII short stature becomes apparent after approximately 8 years of age; before this, height appears to be within the reference range. Therefore, it was assumed that patients would be old enough for short stature to be apparent but young enough that height could still potentially be affected by treatment. The slope before treatment was -0.040; the slope after treatment was -0.006; and the slope difference between before and after treatment was 0.034.

Results of the growth velocity analysis indicate a positive influence of ERT on the slope of height zscores in patients between the age of 8 and 15 years, inclusive. Although this effect is not clearly seen in the growth charts of individual patients, these plots show growth velocity rather than z-scores and, unlike the z-score analysis, they do not include all height measurements.

## Duration of Dosing

Per the prescribing information, the idursulfase infusion should be delivered over a period of 3 hours, which may be gradually reduced to 1 hour if no IRRs are observed.

Total infusion time was reported for 23,041 infusions. The mean (SD) infusion time was 174 (57.9) minutes, and the range was 0 to 795 minutes (P10=70 minutes, P90=240 minutes).

## Missed Infusions

Of the 1014 treated patients who were alive at HOS entry and received at least 1 dose of idursulfase, 420 (41.4%) patients have missed a total of 4698 infusions. Reasons recorded for missed infusions were categorized as follows: unknown (1484 infusions, 31.6%), illness (981 infusions, 20.9%), other reason (725 infusions, 15.4%), holiday/vacation (599 infusions, 12.8%), caregiver/family issue (597 infusions, 12.7%), idursulfase not available (186 infusions, 4.0%), and cannulation not possible (126 infusions, 2.7%). The reason for missed infusions was not recorded for 27 infusions.

## CHMP comments

As baseline in this study is defined as the start of idursulfase treatment, the exposure from start of ERT to last visit is most relevant. More patients have a longer exposure (≥10 years) in the idursulfase from start ERT to last visit calculation than in the idursulfase treatment from HOS entry to last visit calculation as apparently many patients have started idursulfase treatment before HOS entry.

Of the 1014 patients, 472 reported at least 1 administered dose of idursulfase. It is remarkable that the reported maximum administered dose was 3.90 mg/kg. That is almost 8 times higher than the recommended dose. Since the majority of patients received weekly dosing in the range 0.45 to &lt;0.55 mg/kg, which is conform the SmPC, this issue is not further pursued.

In total, 420 (41.4%) patients have missed one or more infusions, with the main reasons being unknown, illness, other reason, holiday or caregiver/family issues. It is noted that in 4.0% of the missed infusions, was due to availability of idursulfase treatment. While supply issues are highly undesirable, this issue is not further pursued.

## Publications Providing Data on Efficacy and Safety of Idursulfase

The MAH has provided an overview of the available literature to support the long-term efficacy and safety of idursulfase treatment.

The recent study by Kampmann demonstrated a treatment effect of ERT, showing a significant delay in the onset and worsening of valve disease (n= 81; CSR in CTD). The study by Broomfield et al. (2020), with its large cohort of pediatric MPS II patients (n=110) followed for a median of 10 years, found that treatment with idursulfase improved survival and respiratory outcomes in children with MPS II.

Additional studies have analyzed the effect of long-term (2-12 years) treatment with idursulfase on the somatic signs of MPS II, including uGAG levels, growth, organomegaly, hearing, heart function, lung function, and joint range of motion. ## CHMP comments:

The MAH has provided an overview of non-HOS based publications on idursulfase treatment in MPSII patients. These articles provide cumulative information about 988 MPS II patients of whom a part was treated with idursulfase. It is unclear how many unique patients were treated with idursulfase (e.g. the 94 patients from the Muenzer et al. 2011 publication is the open-label extension of the Elaprase pivotal trial). Many studies are retrospective (observational) studies or uncontrolled prospective studies which limits the conclusions that can be drawn regarding efficacy and safety of idursulfase treatment. Overall, the key results observed in these publications support the observations from the HOS study.

## Discussion

The study design, being an uncontrolled observational study, does not permit drawing firm conclusions on long-term efficacy. Furthermore, while a rather large number of patients is included in the registry, the amount of missing data is large and the number of patients with long-term follow-up data available is limited. The reasoning for this is acknowledged and understood, nonetheless this highlights the underlying assumption of the marketing authorization under exceptional circumstances; that it is unlikely that comprehensive data will be collected.

Regarding SOB 1, the Hunter Outcome Survey, with the analysis of this final CSR; the MAH has undertaken several data completeness initiatives (see section 'Initiatives to Improve Data Collection in the HOS'). It is agreed with the MAH that no additional measures could be implemented within the existing registry that could have led to an improvement in the analysable dataset. SOB 1 is considered fulfilled.

The MAH proposes that it has also fulfilled obligation SOB5 to the extent possible. However, SOB 5 is considered not fulfilled by the assessors. SOB5: \"To evaluate the following long term clinical endpoints primarily through the HOS. These data will be re-evaluated annually and outcomes are reported and discussed within the Annual Reassessments.

- Assessment of long term pulmonary morbidity (e.g., incidence of infections, pulmonary function status) and mortality

- Assessment of long term cardiovascular morbidity (e.g., incidence of events, and echocardiography data, where available) and mortality
- Assessment of long term urinary GAG excretion patterns
- Assessment of long term antibody levels, isotype and correlation to other therapeutic parameters\"

Provided data is in line with the previously observed effects of idursulfase treatment. Several clinical endpoints suggest an improvement upon ERT. Provided literature of non-HOS studies supports this. The cumulative safety data from the HOS registry submitted in this annual re-assessment procedure continues to be generally consistent with observations seen in previous annual assessments. No new safety data has been identified. Considering the limitations, data cannot be considered comprehensive. It is however, not expected that the MAH will be able to generate comprehensive evidence in the future.

As long-term efficacy and safety is not confirmed and the marketing authorization under exceptional circumstances remains in place, an open ended SOB needs to be in place ensuring monitoring of longterm efficacy and safety for the lifecycle of the product. Since the registry is closed and the set-up of a long-term randomized controlled study is considered unfeasible, the MAH has revised the PI as requested and now includes a more generic SOB for monitoring long-term efficacy and safety:
