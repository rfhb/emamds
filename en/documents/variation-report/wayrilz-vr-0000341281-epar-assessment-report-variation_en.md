## 2. Overall conclusion and impact on the benefit/risk balance

The Applicant has submitted paediatric study results for WAYRILZ (rilzabrutinib) in the scope of a Variation Type II (C.4), which were performed in compliance with a paediatric investigation plan, but no extension of indication to the paediatric indication is intended with this submission.

The phase 3 trial PRN1008-018 (also referred to as study 018) was a randomized, parallel-group, doubleblind, placebo-controlled, multicenter trial consisting of 24 weeks of blinded treatment (rilzabrutinib or placebo) followed by an OL period of 28 weeks. After completing the OL period, eligible participants may continue into the LTE period. Participants are to have a 4-week follow-up visit after their last dose of rilzabrutinib.

## Internal

The Applicant has included n=30 adolescent patients from 10 to &lt;18 years of age with primary ITP of duration &gt;6 months. The principal study design is adequate and aligned with the investigation of adult subjects in the same study 018 as submitted for MAA as basis for licensure in adult use granted on: 22th Dec 2025. Importantly, all data analyses were descriptive only, i.e. without formal hypothesis testing, which can be followed with respect to the rather limited sample size. The obtained data from paediatric patients were compared to the existing PopPK model and used for an E-R-analysis compared to adult patients. Paediatric patients were treated with the same dose as adult patients (i.e. 400mg BID), but it remains unclear whether this is indeed the optimal dosing range for paediatric patients considering the limited patient number (18 participants randomised to rilzabrutinib in the double-blind period, with a total number of 30 paediatric participants across all study periods) and descriptive nature of the trial. In addition, the performed modelling analyses cannot be considered robust and are not supportive of the Applicants claim of similar exposure and response with the same dose in paediatric and adult patients.

Numerically more patients have met the early response criterion after 12 weeks (55.6% vs. 25% for rilzabrutinib and placebo, respectively) as well as the durable platelet response definition within 24 treatment weeks (2 vs. 0 for rilzabrutinib and placebo, respectively) during rilzabrutinib treatment compared to placebo. Notably, the concluded difference to placebo does not provide a clear conclusion on beneficial treatment effects with rilzabrutinib in this adolescent population as the limited patient numbers result in low precision of results with large 95% CIs (difference to placebo 11.1% with 95%CI: -3.41, 25.63). Numerically higher rates were also observed across secondary endpoints for the study group treated with rilzabrutinib compared to placebo (e.g. number of weeks with response, time to first response). However, in contrast to data in adult subjects, the limited data in paediatric patients cannot fully confirm the established treatment effect in a paediatric population; at the same time, these data do not question the evidence established in the adult population.

Regarding safety characterisation, the overall paediatric safety database is clearly limited, and caution should be exercised when interpreting the available data.

The general safety profile was mostly characterised by GI events and an increased risk for infections, which seems to be largely in line with reports from the adult study population, and the newly provided data do not call into question the established safety profile of rilzabrutinib in adult patients.

The benefit-risk balance of WAYRILZ remains positive.

## 6. Clinical Pharmacology aspects

## 6.1. Methods - analysis of data submitted

Clinical pharmacology data were obtained from the paediatric population of Study PRN1008-018 and extrapolation study POH20069. The same dosing regimen (400 mg rilzabrutinib BID) was selected for evaluation in paediatric participants (10 to &lt;18 years of age) as the PK of rilzabrutinib was expected to be similar in paediatric (10 to &lt;18 years old) and adult participants.

In the blinded portion of the clinical trial PRN1008-018, PK samples for paediatric participants were taken Predose (within 1.5 hours predose) and postdose at the following timepoints: 0.5 hours (±5 mins), 2 hours (±5 mins) and 4 hours (±15 mins), 6 hours (±15 mins) at the Week 1 visit. At the Week 13 visit a sample was taken Predose only. At these visits, participants waited to take their morning dose until they complete the blood draw of pre-dose sample.

In the open-label portion of the clinical trial PRN1008-018, PK samples for paediatric participants were taken at the Week 25 visit taken Predose (within 1.5 hours predose) and postdose at the following timepoints: 0.5 hours (±5 minutes), 2 hours (±5 minutes) and 4 hours (±15 minutes), 6 hours (±15 minutes). At these visits, participants waited to take their morning dose until they complete the blood draw of pre- dose sample. At Week 53 and early termination visits, a random timepoint PK samples was collected and the time of sample and time of the last dose of rilzabrutinib was captured.

## PopPK Model - study POH20069

## Objectives

The main objectives of this analysis are the following:

- To apply a prior rilzabrutinib ITP Pop PK model to paediatric ITP patients aged 10 to 17 years and assess its ability to characterize rilzabrutinib PK in this population;
- To generate individual post-hoc rilzabrutinib PK exposures in paediatric ITP patients and evaluate the similarity of paediatric and adult PK exposures in support of PK extrapolation;
- To quantitatively extrapolate adult efficacy data to paediatric participants using a population PK/PD model of platelet response. (This part of study POH20069 is presented in the PK/PD section)

## Methodology

## Pop PK Analysis

The suitability of the previously developed population pharmacokinetic (Pop PK) model in mainly adult immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) patients (POH1156) for characterizing rilzabrutinib PK in paediatric ITP patients aged 10 to 17 years was evaluated. This model in POH1156 was developed using PK data from 545 subjects (255 healthy subjects, 273 adult ITP patients, and 17 paediatric ITP patients aged 10 to 17 years).

To support this evaluation, the POH1156 Pop PK model was applied to PK data from 30 paediatric ITP patients (including 17 patients already included for model development) from study EFC17093 using a maximum a posteriori (MAP) Bayesian approach, with all parameters fixed to the original values from POH1156. Model adequacy was assessed through visual predictive checks (VPC), goodness of fit plots, and predefined quality criteria.

Following this assessment, a sensitivity analysis was performed to further evaluate the robustness of POH1156 model, by updating the POH1156 model with a full dataset, which included the original POH1156 dataset (N = 545) plus an additional 13 paediatric ITP patients. In this full dataset, dose information for 6 out of 17 paediatric ITP patients in the original POH1156 dataset was corrected due to prior misassignment caused by blinded paediatric data at the time of original analysis. The objective of this sensitivity analysis was to confirm that inclusion of the additional paediatric patients and corrected dosing information had minimal impact on parameter estimates in ITP Pop PK model (POH1156).

## PK-PD Model

An initial population PK/PD analysis was conducted after database lock of the Study EFC17093 DB period for the adult ITP participants (POH1157) and summarized in Module 2.7.2 of the initial submission. This population PK/PD model served as the foundation for the extrapolation analysis for the platelet response in pediatric ITP population (10 years and older). The model was used to predict the response as defined by the primary efficacy endpoint of Study EFC17093, durable platelet response, counting the number of weekly platelet measurements during the last 12 weeks. This model was updated following inclusion of the pediatric data. The performance of the final updated Pop PK/PD model was evaluated using a suite of standard diagnostic tools to assess both structural and statistical adequacy. Together, these diagnostics were used to confirm that the model provides an adequate description of the central tendency and variability of platelet response over time and across the range of exposures and patient characteristics included in the analysis (POH20069). The validity of the proposed paediatric extrapolation relies on the observed exposure similarity between adults and pediatric participants receiving 400 mg BID.

A comparison of key parameter estimates between the initial and updated models showed that the parameter values remain highly consistent across model versions, demonstrating that integration of the full pediatric dataset did not introduce instability or structural changes to the model. Finally, simulationbased predictions of durable response were generated by forward-simulating platelet trajectories over the 24-week blinded period for each adult and paediatric participant. For paediatric participants, 500 simulations were performed using the paediatric dataset with allometrically scaled PK parameters and pediatric-specific covariates. For each simulation replicate, the durable response endpoint was recomputed using the same algorithm applied to observed data. This approach allowed quantification of uncertainty in response predictions, arising from both parameter variability and interindividual heterogeneity in PK and PD responses. It is important to recognize that this simulation-based approach used participants as described from the pediatric dataset of the EFC17093 DB period.

## 6.3. Discussion

Phase 3 trial PRN1008-018 enrolled 30 paediatric subjects from 10 to &lt;18 years of age with ITP, of whom 18 subjects received rilzabrutinib treatment and 12 received placebo during the double-blind phase. Blood draws for PK assessment were undertaken in Week 1 and 25 pre-dose, 0.5, 2, 4 and 6 hours post-dose, in Week 13 only pre-dose. Week 25 PK data were not pooled for subjects who received rilzabrutinib from the beginning and subjects who transferred from placebo to rilzabrutinib at Week 25 due to the different treatment duration.

The Applicant evaluated whether the previously developed model based mainly on adult data (545 subjects: 255 healthy subjects, 273 adult ITP patients, and 17 paediatric ITP patients aged 10 to 17 years) was suitable to describe the obtained paediatric data. In addition, as sensitivity analysis to conform the robustness of the model, the model was updated using the full data set (558 subjects: 545 subjects +13 paediatric patients) including corrected dose information for 6 out of 17 paediatric ITP patients in the original dataset. Although it can be agreed that the estimated parameter did not change much, the model does not describe the paediatric data well, in either version. While it is acknowledged that the overall trend is captured at least for later timepoints, both model versions overestimate the exposure for paediatric subjects. Furthermore, misspecifications for earlier timepoints are observed,

which is also the case for adult patients. The Applicant further estimated AUCSS, Cmax and Ctrough for paediatric and adult patients separately and claims that steady-state paediatric exposures were largely contained within, or overlapped, the adult therapeutic range, with no evidence of systematic under- or over-exposure. This cannot be agreed to. While the paediatric values might be contained in the adult range, they are overall lower and the 95% CI are not overlapping. In this respect, it has to be reiterated, that the paediatric data are overall overestimated. Consequently, the real exposure in paediatric patients might be even lower.

Similarly to the popPK model, the Applicant updated the previously developed PK/PD model used to predict the response as defined by the primary efficacy endpoint of Study EFC17093, durable platelet response, counting the number of weekly platelet measurements during the last 12 weeks. It can be agreed that the updated model does not differ much from the previous model. However, the model overpredicts the response especially in paediatric patients. While the model predicts a mean durable response rate of 27.0% (95% simulation interval: 21.8-32.3%) for adults, a rate of 23% (95% CI: 16%- 30%) was observed in the clinical study. For paediatric patients the predicted durable response rate was 20.8% (95% simulation interval: 5.6-36.2%), which is much higher compared to the observed durable response rate of 11% (95% CI: 1.4%-35%). This is most likely mainly a consequence of the overestimation of exposure in the popPK model. The Applicant claims that the differences do not reflect systematic model overprediction but is driven by the small paediatric sample. Although it is acknowledged that the sample size is limited, the statement cannot be agreed to since the overprediction can be clearly observed in both models and actually increased for the popPK model using the full dataset with a higher number of paediatric patients.

Overall, both presented models are not considered to describe the data well, especially for paediatric patients.

As the Applicant states the validity of the proposed paediatric extrapolation relies on the observed exposure similarity between adults and paediatric participants receiving 400 mg BID. The provided data did not establish such exposure similarity between adult and paediatric patients.

Therefore, the conclusion that the model outcomes show similar exposure and in consequence, similar effect to that observed in adults cannot be agreed.

## 7. Clinical Efficacy aspects

## 7.1. Methods

## 7.1.1. Study design

Study PRN1008-018 is a randomized, parallel-group, DB, placebo-controlled, multicenter trial consisting of 24 weeks of blinded treatment (rilzabrutinib or placebo) followed by an OL period of 28 weeks. After completing the OL period, eligible participants may continue into the LTE period. Participants are to have a 4-week follow-up visit after their last dose of rilzabrutinib. ## 7.1.2. Efficacy evaluations and parameters

## Primary endpoint (blinded treatment period)

As its primary endpoint, Study PRN1008-018 assessed durable platelet response. By Definition 1, durable platelet response was the proportion of participants able to achieve platelet counts at or above 50,000/μ L for ≥ two-thirds of at least 8 non-missing weekly scheduled platelet measurements during the last 12 weeks of the 24week blinded treatment period in the absence of rescue therapy, provided that at least 2 non-missing weekly scheduled platelet measurements are at or above 50,000/μL during the last 6 weeks of the 24-week blinded treatment period.

By Definition 2 applicable to the EU (EEA countries) and UK, durable platelet response was the proportion of participants able to achieve platelet counts ≥50,000/μL on at least 8 out of the last 12 weeks of the 24-week treatment in the absence of rescue therapy.

## Key secondary endpoints (blinded treatment period)

Key secondary efficacy endpoints in PRN1008-018 for the pediatric participants included the following endpoints:

· Number of weeks with platelet count ≥50,000/μL OR between ≥30,000/μL and &lt;50,000/μL and at least doubled from baseline over the 24-week blinded treatment period in the absence of rescue therapy

· Number of weeks with platelet counts ≥30,000/μL and at least doubled from baseline over the 24-week blinded treatment period in the absence of rescue therapy

· Time to first platelet count of ≥50,000/μL OR between ≥30,000/μL and &lt;50,000/μL and doubled from baseline

- Proportion of participants requiring rescue therapy during the 24-week blinded treatment period
- Applicable to countries within the EU (EEA countries) and UK: Change from baseline in ITP Bleeding Scale (IBLS) assessment at Week 25

## Randomisation

Participants of pediatric age (10 to 18 years of age) were planned to be randomised via Interactive Response Technology (IRT) in a 2:1 allocation ratio (rilzabrutininb vs placebo). Dynamic randomization algorithm (minimization) was planned to be implemented. The randomization was planned to be stratified by splenectomy status (yes/no) and by severity of thrombocytopenia (Inclusion Criteria 3 platelet counts &lt;15,000/μL or ≥15,000/μL). (Details of the randomisation were only included in Amendment 01, approved on 19-FEB-2021).

## Sample size considerations

The pediatric sample size of up to 30 participants (20 participants on rilzabrutinib and 10 participants on placebo) did not undergo a formal sample size calculation. It was determined based on clinical practice and was considered adequate to descriptively describe the safety and efficacy in pediatric participants. With a sample size of 20 pediatric participants on rilzabrutinib the maximum width of an exact 90% confidence interval (CI) on response rate would be 40%.

## Statistical methods

Efficacy endpoint analyses of pediatric population data were descriptive only without formal hypothesis testing.

## Primary endpoint analyses

The definition of primary efficacy endpoint durable platelet response can be found above. Participants who discontinued the treatment prior to Week 25 due to lack of efficacy or a related adverse event (AE), or received rescue medication after 8-week of DB treatment were considered as non-responders. ## 7.3. Discussion

In order to explore the efficacy of rilzabrutinib in paediatric patients, the Applicant has included n=30 adolescent patients from 10 to &lt;18 years of age with primary ITP of duration &gt;6 months in their randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study PRN1008-018 (also referred to as study 018). The principal study design is adequate and it is acknowledged that the study duration (24 weeks double-blind, followed by switch to rilzabrutinib for all patients for a 28 weeks open-label and subsequent long-term extension), primary objective/endpoint and other success criteria (e.g. response definition) are aligned with the investigation of adult subjects in the same study 018 as submitted for MAA as basis for licensure in adult use granted on: 22th Dec 2025. Importantly, all data analyses were descriptive only, i.e. without formal hypothesis testing, which can be followed with respect to the rather limited sample size. The limited sample size as such can be followed considering that the chronic form of ITP is less frequent in pediatric patients compared to adult patients.

Baseline demographics, characteristics as well as disease history and prior ITP medication seem mostly balanced with remaining uncertainties around imbalances presumably originating from the low sample size. Still, it should be highlighted that the median platelet count was clearly higher in the rilzabrutinib group (10,000/μL, range 2,000 to 30,000/μL) compared to the placebo group (8,000/μL, range 1,000 to 28,000/μL), which could have had an imbalanced impact on study success criteria (responder with platelet count ≥50000 platelets per mL). This aspect is not further followed on, considering the descriptive nature of the trial and the fact that no indication is sought for the pediatric population, but should be considered for interpretation of study results.

Numerically more patients have met the early response criterion after 12 weeks (55.6% vs. 25% for rilzabrutinib and placebo, respectively) as well as the durable platelet response definition within 24 treatment weeks (2 vs. 0 for rilzabrutinib and placebo, respectively) during rilzabrutinib treatment compared to placebo. Notably, the concluded difference to placebo does not provide a clear conclusion on beneficial treatment effects with rilzabrutinib in this adolescent population as the limited patient numbers result in low precision of results with large 95% CIs (difference to placebo 11.1% with 95%CI: -3.41, 25.63). Still, it is acknowledged that the platelet response of those 2 patients that have met the durable response definition during the DB period, a sustained response with a mean platelet count increase of 144.3 ([±12.7] × 10 9 /L) by week 25 and a consistent median of &gt;150000 platelets / µl was observed

## 8.3. Discussion

The paediatric data package comprises data from the double-blind and open-label periods of study PRN1008-018 at a cutoff date 05 Aug 2025. Study PRN1008-018 was a Phase 3 registrational study, which provided the pivotal data for marketing authorisation of Wayrilz in adult ITP patients. Treatment of paediatric ITP patients was investigated under the same study protocol in the separate paediatric cohort.

\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_\\_

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The safety population included all paediatric participants randomised or enrolled who received at least one dose of study drug.

The overall paediatric safety database is clearly limited, with 18 participants randomised to rilzabrutinib treatment compared to 12 placebo treated participants in the double-blind study period, and a total number of 30 rilzabrutinib treated paediatric patients across all study periods (double blind, open-label, extension). Paediatric patients received the same dosing regimen as adult patients, i.e. 400mg bid.

Given the low number of patients, caution is needed when interpreting the available paediatric safety data.

While overall no differences were reported for frequencies of TEAEs between rilzabrutinib and placebo treated patients (83.3% each), substantial increases compared to placebo were seen in the system organ class of GI disorders (44.4% vs 8.3%), which is largely in line with results from the adult cohort. Likewise, a higher frequency was also reported in the SOC of infections and infestations for rilzabrutinib treated patients compared to placebo (44.4% vs 25.0%), which was also higher than numbers reported from the adult cohort (33.1% vs 20.3%). In contrast to results from the adult cohort, lower reporting compared to placebo was noted in SOCs nervous system disorders (16.7% vs 25.0%) and respiratory, thoracic and mediastinal disorders (16.7% vs 33.3%).

Related TEAEs were significantly more common in the rilzabrutinib treatment arm (9/18, 50.0%) vs placebo (2/12, 16.7%), with proportions in line with reporting from adults. Overall, the most commonly reported related TEAEs were GI related, nausea (5/18, 27.8%) and vomiting (2/18, 11.1%). All other related TEAEs were reported from one participant each, and included pneumonia, headache, hypoaesthesia, abdominal pain, abdominal pain upper, constipation, diarrhoea, proteinuria, oedema, and aPTT prolonged.

In the double-blind period, 2 participants reported SAEs (epistaxis and ovarian cyst ruptured). Additional SAEs reported from the open-label and long-term extension periods include haematomas, epistaxis, and an event of grade 4 anaphylactic reaction. All SAEs were not considered related to rilzabrutinib.

There were no deaths, no AESIs (defined as ALT &gt;3x ULN, infections grade 4 or 5, uveitis, symptomatic overdose, pregnancy), and no TEAEs leading to permanent study intervention discontinuation reported across all study periods.

In laboratory findings, a substantially higher proportion of rilzabrutinib patients had low leukocytes (8/18, 44.4%) compared to placebo (3/12, 25.0%), which was also substantially higher than reported from the adult study population (8/132, 6.1% vs 0 with placebo). However, the reported events of low leukocytes in paediatric patients were mostly asymptomatic and not associated with AEs, and leukocyte levels returned to baseline without intervention. The finding therefore did not give rise to concern.

Taken together, while no robust conclusions on the safety profile in paediatric patients can be made from the limited sample of patients included in the paediatric cohort, in the limited data set the general safety profile was mostly characterised by GI events and increased risk for infections, and largely in line with reports from the adult study population.

## Internal

Mean (SD) change from baseline of white blood cell levels in all records (7 patients in rilzabrutinib and 2 patients in placebo arms), during the DB period was -1.39 (0.84) in rilzabrutinib vs -0.4 (0.25) in placebo arms, with median change of -1.18 and -0.4, respectively.

As per the records of the patients with low leukocytes in rilzabrutinib arm during the DB period, these laboratory findings are all asymptomatic and not associated with adverse events, except for the one patient described above. The leukocyte levels returned to baseline without any intervention, the patients continued the study with no IMP interruption. There is no change or imbalance observed in neutrophil and lymphocyte levels in both treatment arms. The change in leukocyte levels appears sporadic and not clinically significant fluctuations.

Therefore, the MAH considers that the limited available data, comprising incidental, sporadic, and transient laboratory findings, do not provide sufficient evidence to support the inclusion of decreased leucocyte counts as an adverse reaction in pediatric patients in section 4.8 of the SmPC.
