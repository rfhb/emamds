## 1. Introduction

On 28 January 2026, the MAH submitted one completed paediatric study for Constella (linaclotide), in accordance with Article 46 of Regulation (EC) No1901/2006, as amended.

A short critical expert overview has also been provided.

## 2. Scientific discussion

## 2.1. Information on the development program

The MAH stated that Study M21-572 \"Linaclotide versus Placebo in Paediatric Subjects, Ages 2 to 5 Years, with Functional Constipation (FC)\" is part of the EU PIP, Procedure No. EMEA-000927-PIP01-10M08 as Study 7 (Decision No. P/0207/2024), and that no product information update is proposed in this Article 46 submission. There were no new safety signals observed in paediatric FC subjects 2 to 5 years of age that change or result in a new benefit/risk evaluation. Consequently, no changes were made to the Constella product information (PI).

Linaclotide is a first-in-class, 14-amino acid peptide. It acts on the apical surface of intestinal epithelial cells in the intestinal lumen by stimulating guanylate cyclase subtype C (GC-C) receptors. GC-C is a key regulator of intestinal secretory function in mammals. Linaclotide acts locally in the intestine and has limited permeability, resulting in very low absolute oral bioavailability across all species tested (≤ 0.2%).

Linaclotide is approved in several regions of the world and for different indications:

- in the United States for the treatment of IBS-C (irritable bowel syndrome with constipation) in adults and paediatric patients aged 7 years and older, for the treatment of chronic idiopathic constipation (CIC) in adults, and for the treatment of FC in paediatric patients aged 6 years and older
- in Canada for the treatment of IBS-C and CIC in adults, and for the treatment of FC in paediatric patients aged 6 to 17 years
- in Japan, Australia, New Zealand, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, and Mexico for the treatment of IBS-C and CIC or chronic constipation in adults
- in the United Kingdom, China, and Switzerland for the treatment of IBS-C in adults.

In the EU, the product is currently authorised under the brand name Constella for the following indication:

\"Constella is indicated for the symptomatic treatment of moderate to severe irritable bowel syndrome with constipation (IBS-C) in adults\".

Functional constipation (FC) is a common paediatric disorder affecting children across all age groups. The submitted background information reports a worldwide prevalence ranging from 0.7 % to 29.6 %. Paediatric FC is characterised by symptoms such as infrequent bowel movements, defined as two or fewer bowel movements per week, hard or large-diameter stools, painful defecation, retentive posturing, and faecal incontinence. If untreated or insufficiently controlled, FC may be associated with clinically relevant consequences, including poor appetite, failure to thrive, encopresis, anal fissures, cystitis, impaired quality of life, school absenteeism, and increased healthcare utilisation.

The pathophysiology of paediatric FC is multifactorial. Contributing factors include genetic predisposition, insufficient fibre and fluid intake, immobility, and, particularly in younger children, stool-withholding behaviour. Withholding often develops after painful or frightening defecation and may result in a self-reinforcing cycle: voluntary stool retention increases water absorption from the faecal mass, leading to harder stools and further painful defecation. Longstanding faecal stasis may also contribute to impaired colonic function and dysmotility.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

Initial management is usually non-pharmacological and includes education, dietary advice with adequate fibre and fluid intake, and behavioural measures. In older children, toilet training, reward systems, and stool diaries may be added. Where faecal impaction is suspected, disimpaction may be attempted using rectal enemas or high-dose oral polyethylene glycol (PEG), followed by maintenance therapy and, after sustained symptom relief, gradual weaning. Longer treatment durations may be required in children with a longstanding history of untreated or inadequately treated constipation. The MAH cites submitted literature indicating that paediatric functional constipation may persist despite treatment in a referred specialist population, with approximately 40% of children referred to a paediatric gastroenterologist remaining symptomatic after 5 years and 20% after 10 years. Proposed explanations include dosing, adherence, and treatment mechanisms. This supports the clinical relevance of studying additional options in children aged 2 to 5, particularly treatments with a defined mechanism of action that may address intestinal secretory and motility-related components of the disease. The clinical study report therefore concludes that there is an unmet medical need for treatment of FC in paediatric patients in the EU.

## 2.2. Information on the pharmaceutical formulation used in the study

The pharmaceutical formulation used in study M21-572 was a capsule formulation, which contains 72 µg linaclotide with matching placebo capsules used in the double-blind Part 1 and open-label linaclotide 72 µg used in Part 2. It was administered orally once daily at approximately the same time each day, 30 minutes before any meal.

Protocol dosing allowed administration as a full intact capsule swallowed by the subject; capsule contents opened and sprinkled into approximately 2 to 4 tablespoons of water, with the subject consuming all of the water and no requirement to consume any remaining beads; or capsule contents opened and sprinkled onto approximately 1 to 2 teaspoons of applesauce, with the subject consuming all of the applesauce and beads.

## Methods

## Study participants

The target population for this study were male and female paediatric subjects from 2 to &lt;6 years of age. The main inclusion criterion refers to the presence of childhood functional constipation according to protocol-specific modified Rome IV criteria, supplemented by an eDiary.

-  For Part 1, subjects were 24 months to 5 years and 11 months (inclusive) at consent, and the caregiver/parent/guardian/legally authorised representative (LAR) had to be able and willing to comply with protocol procedures, including training and completion of the daily eDiary assessments.
-  Subjects had weight and body mass index (BMI) at or above the 3 rd percentile.
-  Subjects met the protocol-specific modified Rome IV entry criterion for FC:

For at least 1 month before Screening (Visit 1), the subject has had 2 or fewer defecations (with each defecation occurring in the absence of any laxative, suppository, or enema use during the preceding 24 hours) per week. In addition, at least once per week, the subject must meet 1 or more of the following:

-  History of retentive posturing or excessive volitional stool retention
-  History of painful or hard bowel movements (BMs)
-  Presence of a large faecal mass in the rectum
-  History of large diameter stools

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

-  At least 1 episode of faecal incontinence per week after the acquisition of toileting skills, if applicable
-  Based on the eDiary reports the subject did not have no more than one loose, mushy stool (Bristol Stool Form Scale [BSFS] 6), or watery stool (BSFS 7) with any spontaneous bowel movement (SBM) that occurred in the absence of laxative use on the calendar day of the BM or the calendar day before the BM during the 14 days before and up to randomisation. Based on the eDiary the subject had an average of ≤ 2 SBMs/week during the 14 days before and up to randomisation. A SBM is defined as a BM that occurs in the absence of laxative, enema, or suppository use on the calendar day of the BM or the calendar day before the BM.
-  For Part 2 rollover, the subject had to have met all Part 1 eligibility criteria from M21-572 or LIN-MD-67, including no faecal impaction before treatment, and to have completed the end-ofstudy treatment visit in the lead-in study.

Key exclusion criteria are non-exhaustive mentioned in this assessment report and included the following categories:

-  Organic or confounding diseases/conditions, including celiac disease, cystic fibrosis, untreated or unstable hypothyroidism, chromosomal disorders, active anal fissures, anatomic malformations, intestinal nerve or muscle disorders, neuropathic conditions, inflammatory bowel disease, childhood functional abdominal pain syndrome, and lactose intolerance with confounding symptoms.
-  Faecal impaction shortly before treatment or randomisation and required manual disimpaction within the prior year.
-  Unexplained and clinically significant alarm symptoms (lower gastrointestinal [GI] bleeding, iron-deficiency or any unexplained anaemia, or weight loss) and systemic signs of infection or colitis, or any neoplastic process.
-  Relevant gastrointestinal, abdominal, pelvic, retroperitoneal, or other major surgery, and known or suspected mechanical bowel obstruction or pseudo-obstruction.
-  Protocol-specified prohibited medication use, rescue medication on the calendar day before randomisation or before randomisation on Visit 3, recent participation in an interventional study, or recent/current investigational treatment.
-  Planned or ongoing toilet training at any time in Part 1 (Children who were fully toilet trained or who have not started the process were eligible to participate).

Prior and concomitant therapy handling was specified separately in Section 5.4 of the Protocol for Study M21-572. Medicines, vaccines, vitamins, and herbal supplements received at enrolment or during the study were to be recorded prior to study drug administration through the post-treatment visit (Week 12 for Part 1 or Week 24 for Part 2). Prohibited medications had to be safely discontinued 5 half-lives or 30 days before initial study drug administration. Protocol-permitted rescue medications were senna (oral), mineral oil enema (rectal), and magnesium hydroxide (oral), allowed to be taken when at least 72 hours had passed since the previous bowel movement or symptoms became intolerable. Rescue medication was not allowed on the calendar day before randomisation or before randomisation on Visit 3.

## Randomisation and blinding (masking)

Eligible subjects in Part 1 were assigned in a 1:1 ratio to linaclotide 72 µg or placebo. Treatment allocation was encoded by a randomisation number according to the randomisation schedule, with stratification by age group. All subjects were assigned a unique identification number by the interactive web response system (IWRS) at the Screening Visit (Visit 1). The IWRS assigned a randomization number that encoded the study subject's treatment group assignment according to the randomization schedule.

All AbbVie personnel with direct oversight of the conduct and management of the trial (except for the AbbVie Drug Supply Management Team), the investigator, study site personnel, and the subject/parent/guardian/LAR remained blinded to each subject's double-blind treatment throughout the study.

To maintain the blind, the linaclotide capsules and placebo capsules provided for the study were identical in appearance. The IWRS procedure allowed access to unblinded subject treatment information in a medical emergency. The unblinded safety review by the Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) was a planned controlled exception.

Part 2 was an open-label long-term safety extension period. All subjects received open-label linaclotide 72 µg. Therefore, blinding was not necessary.

## Assessor's comment:

The randomisation and blinding arrangements are acceptable for the purpose of this Article 46 assessment.

## Statistical Methods

In Part 1, efficacy analyses were conducted in the Intent-to-Treat part 1 (ITT1) population and by randomized treatment. The primary endpoint was the change from baseline in 12-week spontaneous bowel movement (SBM) frequency rate observed by the primary caregiver. Missing data were handled under the Missing Not at Random (MNAR) assumption. Missing postbaseline weeks were imputed using a pattern-mixture model with control-based imputation. The change from baseline was analysed using a Mixed Model for Repeated Measures (MMRM). The model included treatment, week, age group, treatment-by-week interaction and baseline value.

Baseline values for daily electronic diary (eDiary) endpoints were derived from 14 days before

randomization up to randomization. A postbaseline week was considered missing if fewer than four completed diary days were available. Secondary endpoints used the same analysis approach as the primary endpoint. Multiplicity control was prespecified only for the primary endpoint and the three secondary endpoints using a fixed-sequence procedure. The additional efficacy endpoints were outside this hierarchy and were analysed without multiplicity adjustment at a nominal two-sided 0.05 level. Safety analyses were descriptive in the Part 1 and Part 2 safety populations, with no statistical inference for safety assessments. This means, according to the protocol, that adverse events and other safety variables are summarized descriptively. No formal hypothesis testing was planned for safety. The safety results were not intended to prove a statistically significant difference between treatment groups and were used to describe the observed safety profile, such as how many subjects had adverse events and whether clinically relevant laboratory, vital-sign or electrocardiogram findings were observed.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

The final protocol and Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) used the MNAR and MMRM approach. Earlier protocol versions had planned an Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) model and a smaller sample size. The CSR states that protocol changes did not affect interpretation of study results. The CSR also lists post-hoc analyses conducted after SAP finalisation.

Part 2 was an open-label (treatment was not blinded) and non-comparative (there was no placebo/control group) study.

## Assessor's comment:

The statistical methods are acceptable considering the context of the trial.

## Baseline data

The randomised ITT1 population is the relevant baseline comparison for Part 1 of Study M21-572 (placebo N=61; linaclotide 72 ug N=62; total N=123) for children aged 2 to 5 years with functional constipation.

Part 1 demographic characteristics were broadly balanced between arms. Females comprised 37/61 (60.7%) placebo subjects and 39/62 (62.9%) linaclotide subjects; non-Hispanic or non-Latino ethnicity was reported for 38/61 (62.3%) and 41/62 (66.1%), respectively. Race distributions were similar, with White subjects 44/60 (73.3%) versus 45/62 (72.6%), Black or African American subjects 15/60 (25.0%) versus 16/62 (25.8%), and one subject in each arm reported as multiple race; one placebo subject had missing race (Table 3).

Age was comparable: mean age was 3.8 years with placebo and 3.6 years with linaclotide, medians were 4.0 years in both groups, and ranges were 2 to 5 years. Age categories were also balanced, with 25/61 (41.0%) versus 26/62 (41.9%) aged 2 to 3 years and 36/61 (59.0%) versus 36/62 (58.1%) aged 4 to 5 years (Table 3).

Anthropometric measures were similar: mean weight was 17.717 kg versus 17.390 kg, mean height 102.537 cm versus 101.836 cm, and mean BMI 16.749 versus 16.658 kg/m 2 for placebo and linaclotide, respectively. Regional distribution was similar but concentrated in North America (60/61 [98.4%] placebo; 60/62 [96.8%] linaclotide), with three European subjects overall (Table 3).

## Number analysed

Study M21-572 analysed 123 treated randomised subjects in Part 1 (placebo N=61; linaclotide 72 ug N=62) and 103 treated subjects in the open-label Part 2 linaclotide extension. ITT1 supported Part 1 efficacy/baseline analyses, SA1 supported Part 1 safety, and ITT2/SA2 were the corresponding Part 2 treated populations.

In Part 2, 103 subjects entered/received open-label linaclotide and 85/103 (82.5%) completed study treatment; 18/103 (17.5%) discontinued. The separate denominator of 86 in the Part 2 modified Rome IV analysis refers to subjects with paired screening and end-of-treatment modified Rome IV assessments (Table 11) and should not be interpreted as the number completing Part 2 study treatment.

No separate per-protocol or evaluable population is described; interpretation therefore rests on the predefined ITT and safety sets.

## Efficacy results

Part 1 efficacy results: In the ITT1 population, the primary caregiver-observed primary endpoint showed a numerically greater mean change from baseline in 12-week SBM frequency rate with linaclotide 72 ug than placebo, but the linaclotide-placebo difference was not statistically significant (least squares [LS] mean difference 0.319 SBMs/week; 95% CI -0.2758 to 0.9146; p=0.2929; Table 6).

The prespecified all-reported SBM supplementary analysis, including primary and secondary caregiver reports, was nominally favourable for linaclotide (difference 0.671 SBMs/week; 95% CI 0.0313 to 1.3108; p=0.0398; Table 7). Among secondary endpoints, stool consistency was nominally favourable (difference 0.376; 95% CI 0.0593 to 0.6918; p=0.0199; Table 8), while straining showed only directional improvement (difference -0.016; 95% CI -0.1763 to 0.1439; p=0.8425; Table 9) and faecal-incontinence proportions were low with no meaningful treatment difference in the toileting-skills subset (Table 10).

Supplementary, sensitivity, secondary, and additional efficacy findings are therefore supportive or nominal and do not override the non-significant prespecified primary endpoint; multiplicity and analysis-hierarchy limitations should remain explicit in the interpretation.

## Safety results

Part 1 safety results: In the SA1 population, exposure was similar between placebo and linaclotide 72 ug, with mean treatment duration of 80.4 and 82.2 days and 13.4 and 13.9 patient-years, respectively (Table 12). Overall treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE) incidence was comparable between placebo and linaclotide (15/61, 24.6%, and 16/62, 25.8%; Table 14). Treatment-related TEAEs are the subset assessed as having a reasonable possibility of relationship to study treatment; these were uncommon and balanced overall (2/61, 3.3%, and 2/62, 3.2%; Table 14). Diarrhoea occurred as an all-causality TEAE in 2/61 placebo subjects (3.3%) and 1/62 linaclotide subject (1.6%; Table 13), but as a treatment-related TEAE in 2/61 placebo subjects and 0/62 linaclotide subjects (Table 15), so the single linaclotide diarrhoea TEAE was not assessed as reasonably related. No severe TEAEs, serious adverse events, deaths, or adverse events leading to withdrawal of study treatment were reported in Part 1 (Table 14). No adverse events of special interest were reported.

Part 2 safety results: Part 2 was open-label and non-comparative with longer linaclotide exposure (mean duration 156.0 days; 44.0 patient-years; Table 19). In the SA2 population, 39/103 subjects (37.9%) reported a TEAE and 4/103 subjects (3.9%) had a treatment-related TEAE (Table 18). The most frequently (≥ 3% subjects) reported treatment-emergent adverse events with linaclotide were influenza (5/39; 4.9%), diarrhoea (4/39; 3.9%), ear infection and viral upper respiratory tract infection (4/39; 3.9%), (Table 16). Diarrhoea was reported as an all-causality TEAE in 4/103 subjects (3.9%; Table 16), while treatment-related TEAEs were diarrhoea in 3/103 subjects (2.9%) and upper abdominal pain in 1/103 subject (1.0%; Table 17). Therefore, one Part 2 diarrhoea TEAE was not assessed as reasonably related to study treatment. One serious event of worsening constipation was reported, resolved, and was assessed as not related to linaclotide. Three subjects (2.9%) discontinued study treatment because of adverse events and 4/103 subjects (3.9%) had treatment interruption (Table 18). No deaths or adverse events of special interest were reported.

Overall safety conclusion for both parts: The current CSR reports no unexpected clinically relevant laboratory changes, no potential Hy's Law cases in linaclotide groups, and no unexpected vital-sign or ECG changes or trends in either part. Diarrhoea rates were low and there were no diarrhoea-related adverse events of special interest involving significant volume depletion, significant electrolyte abnormalities, or ECG abnormalities. The diarrhoea onset and duration summaries did not suggest a new duration signal, with all diarrhoea events having end dates and none ongoing at end of study. The CSR safety conclusion was that linaclotide was well tolerated and that no new safety signals were observed in paediatric functional-constipation subjects aged 2 to 5 years.

## Assessor's comment:

The safety profile in Study M21-572 is acceptable for this Article 46 assessment. In Part 1, the controlled safety database comprised 61 placebo-treated and 62 linaclotide-treated subjects, with similar exposure and similar overall TEAE incidences, and no severe AEs, serious AEs, deaths or AErelated study-treatment discontinuations. Part 2 provided longer open-label linaclotide exposure in 103 subjects, with no deaths, no diarrhoea-related AESIs and no unexpected clinically relevant laboratory, vital-sign or ECG trends. However, the paediatric safety database remains limited for detecting uncommon events, and the open-label, non-comparative Part 2 design does not allow relative risk estimation versus placebo. Overall, the CSR does not identify an unexpected clinically relevant safety finding or new safety signal compared with the previously established linaclotide safety profile in adults and the available paediatric linaclotide data. However, the possibility of rare risks cannot be ruled out due to the limited sample size and the Part 2 design. Therefore, no safety update of the product information is recommended.

## 2.3.3. Discussion on clinical aspects

Part 1 of Study M21-572 was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled 12-week comparison of linaclotide 72 ug with placebo, and Part 2 was a 24-week open-label long-term safety extension.

The efficacy evidence from Part 1 is mixed but supportive. The primary caregiver-observed primary endpoint showed a numerical advantage for linaclotide in the change from baseline in 12-week SBM frequency rate, but the treatment difference was not statistically significant (LS mean difference 0.319 SBMs/week; 95% CI -0.2758 to 0.9146; p=0.2929). The prespecified all-reported SBM supplementary analysis was nominally favourable (difference 0.671 SBMs/week; 95% CI 0.0313 to 1.3108; p=0.0398), and stool consistency was also nominally favourable (difference 0.376; 95% CI 0.0593 to 0.6918; p=0.0199), while straining and faecal-incontinence results are more limited and directional.

Part 2 contributes supportive longer-term information only. It was open-label, non-comparative and included eligible completers from M21-572 Part 1. Therefore, it does not provide a confirmatory placebo-controlled efficacy evidence. The modified Rome IV end-of-treatment summary may be considered as descriptive supportive information, but the efficacy interpretation should remain driven by Part 1.

The safety findings do not indicate a new clinically relevant safety concern. In Part 1, exposure was similar between placebo and linaclotide and treatment-emergent adverse-event incidence was comparable (15/61, 24.6%, and 16/62, 25.8%). No severe treatment-emergent adverse events, serious adverse events, deaths, adverse events of special interest, or adverse events leading to withdrawal of study treatment were reported. Part 2 provided longer open-label exposure. 39/103 subjects (37.9%) reported a treatment-emergent adverse event, 4/103 (3.9%) had related events, no deaths or adverse events of special interest were reported, and the single serious worsening constipation event resolved and was assessed as unrelated to linaclotide.

Across both parts, the clinical study report does not identify unexpected clinically relevant laboratory, vital-sign, ECG, dehydration/electrolyte, Hy's Law, or diarrhoea-duration findings. Diarrhoea was infrequent in Part 1 and was reported in 4/103 subjects (3.9%) in Part 2, without diarrhoea-related adverse events of special interest.

Overall, the submitted data indicate an acceptable safety profile for the purpose of this Article 46 assessment, and no new safety signal was identified compared with the established linaclotide safety profile in adults and the available paediatric linaclotide data.

The interpretation is limited by the small paediatric sample for uncommon safety events, the nonsignificant primary efficacy result, and the open-label, non-comparative Part 2 extension. Generalisability to EU clinical practice is also limited by the small number of European subjects enrolled. These limitations do not identify a new clinically relevant safety issue in the current dossier. As no product-information update is proposed and the submitted data do not support an additional regulatory consequence, no further clinical regulatory action is proposed on the basis of the data reviewed in this Article 46 procedure.

## 3. Rapporteur's overall conclusion and recommendation

The final clinical study report for Study M21-572, submitted as the PIP-required study in children aged 2 to 5 years with functional constipation, has been assessed under Article 46.

The efficacy results are supportive but non-confirmatory, as the prespecified Part 1 primary endpoint numerically favoured linaclotide 72 micrograms but was not statistically significant. Supplementary, secondary, additional and post-hoc findings are therefore considered supportive or descriptive and do not provide independent confirmatory evidence.

The submitted safety data do not indicate a safety signal beyond the established linaclotide safety profile in adults and the available paediatric linaclotide data.

The MAH proposed no product-information update as part of this Article 46 submission. Based on the submitted data, no regulatory action is required at this stage. The submitted results do not change the benefit-risk balance. No product-information update is recommended, and the PAM is fulfilled.
