## 1. NAME OF THE MEDICINAL PRODUCT

Lynavoy 40 mg film-coated tablets

## 2. QUALITATIVE AND QUANTITATIVE COMPOSITION

Each film-coated tablet contains 40 mg linerixibat.

For the full list of excipients, see section 6.1.

## 3. PHARMACEUTICAL FORM

Film-coated tablet.

Purple, round, biconvex film-coated tablets (9 mm in diameter), debossed with \"GS 3JG\" on one side.

## 4. CLINICAL PARTICULARS

## 4.1 Therapeutic indications

Lynavoy is indicated for the treatment of cholestatic pruritus in adult patients with primary biliary cholangitis (PBC).

## 4.2 Posology and method of administration

## Posology

The recommended dose of Lynavoy is 40 mg twice daily.

Alternative treatment should be considered in patients for whom no treatment benefit can be established following 3 months of continuous daily treatment with linerixibat.

## Concomitant administration with bile acid binding resins

It is recommended that Lynavoy is taken at least 4 hours before or 4 hours after taking a bile acid binding resin (see sections 4.5 and 5.2).

## Missed doses

If a dose is missed, it may be taken as soon as possible. However, a double dose must not be taken to make up for a missed dose.

## Special populations

Elderly

No dose adjustment is required for patients 65 years of age or older (see section 5.2). Limited data are available in patients over 65 years of age.

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## Renal impairment

No dose adjustment is required for patients with renal impairment (see section 5.2).

## Hepatic impairment

No dose adjustment is required for patients with mild or moderate (Child-Pugh A or B) hepatic impairment. Linerixibat is not recommended in severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C) or in patients at high risk of liver decompensation (see sections 4.4 and 5.2).

## Paediatric population

The safety and efficacy of Lynavoy have not been established in children and adolescents less than 18 years of age. No data are available.

## Method of administration

Oral use.

It is recommended that Lynavoy is swallowed whole with water and taken approximately 30 minutes before any food or beverage (other than water). This is to allow linerixibat to enter the gastrointestinal lumen prior to release of bile acids (see section 5.2).

## 4.3 Contraindications

Hypersensitivity to the active substance or to any of the excipients listed in section 6.1.

## 4.4 Special warnings and precautions for use

## Transaminase elevations

In clinical studies, treatment emergent transaminase elevations were observed in some patients receiving linerixibat (see section 4.8). It is recommended that baseline transaminase (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]) levels are obtained prior to initiating treatment with linerixibat and transaminases monitored at a frequency aligned with standard clinical practice for PBC patients. For transaminase elevations, more frequent monitoring may be required. If transaminase elevations persist, weigh the potential risks against the benefit of continuing treatment.

## Patients with hepatic impairment

No dose adjustment is required for patients with mild or moderate (Child-Pugh A or B) hepatic impairment (see section 5.2). Prior to initiation of treatment with linerixibat the patient's hepatic status should be assessed including whether the patient has liver decompensation or has had a prior decompensation event. No data are available for patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C). Use of linerixibat is not recommended in these patients or those at high risk of decompensation. If used, close monitoring is advised (see sections 4.2 and 5.2).

## Diarrhoea

Diarrhoea has been reported as a very common adverse reaction when taking linerixibat (section 4.8). If diarrhoea occurs, advise patients to monitor for signs and symptoms of dehydration. Treatment with antidiarrheal medication may be required. Consider interrupting or discontinuing linerixibat treatment if diarrhoea persists.

## Fat-soluble vitamin deficiency

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

Assessment of fat-soluble vitamin (FSV) levels (vitamins A, D, E and K) and international normalised ratio (INR) are recommended for all patients prior to initiating linerixibat, with monitoring per standard clinical practice. If FSV deficiency is diagnosed, supplemental therapy should be prescribed.

## Excipients

This medicinal product contains less than 1 mmol sodium (23 mg) per film-coated tablet, that is to say essentially 'sodium-free'.

## 4.5 Interaction with other medicinal products and other forms of interaction

## Bile acid binding resins

Bile acid binding resins (such as colestyramine, colesevelam, or colestipol) may bind to other medicinal products, including linerixibat, in the gut when administered concurrently, and therefore may reduce the efficacy of linerixibat (see section 5.2). It is recommended that patients take Lynavoy at least 4 hours before or 4 hours after taking a bile acid binding resin (see section 4.2).

## Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA)

UDCA can be coadministered with linerixibat, and no dose adjustments are required. In clinical studies with concomitant administration of UDCA and linerixibat no clinically relevant increases in ALP were observed. However, the overall plasma profile of UDCA and its conjugates was relevantly altered with concomitant administration of linerixibat. Clinical relevance of these changes are currently unclear. Patients should be monitored for changes in ALP during the initial treatment phases and periodically thereafter, aligned with standard clinical practice for PBC patients (see section 5.2).

## 4.6 Fertility, pregnancy and lactation

## Pregnancy

There are no or limited amount of data from the use of linerixibat in pregnant women.

Animal studies do not indicate direct or indirect harmful effects with respect to reproductive toxicity (see section 5.3). Systemic absorption following oral administration of linerixibat in humans is minimal (see section 5.2). Therefore, foetal exposure to linerixibat is also expected to be minimal.

As a precautionary measure, it is preferable to avoid the use of Lynavoy during pregnancy.

## Breast-feeding

It is unknown whether linerixibat is excreted in human milk.

Animal studies have shown excretion of linerixibat in milk; however, at the recommended clinical dose, neonatal exposure via breast milk is expected to be minimal (see section 5.3). No effects on the breastfed suckling child are anticipated since the systemic exposure of the breast-feeding woman to linerixibat is negligible.

A risk to the suckling child cannot be excluded. A decision must be made whether to discontinue breast-feeding or to discontinue/abstain from Lynavoy therapy, taking into account the benefit of breast-feeding for the child and the benefit of therapy for the mother.

## Fertility

No fertility data are available in humans. Animal studies do not indicate any direct or indirect effects on fertility or reproduction at clinically relevant exposures (see section 5.3).

## Description of selected adverse reactions

## Diarrhoea

In the pooled safety dataset, diarrhoea was observed in 59 % of patients in the linerixibat treatment group. Discontinuation of treatment due to diarrhoea occurred in 4 % of study patients in the linerixibat treatment group. Most events occurred within 1 week of initiating treatment, were mild in intensity, and most resolved during the study. None were serious. The majority of patients who experienced diarrhoea were able to continue linerixibat without treatment discontinuation or interruption. In some patients, diarrhoea persisted or recurred during treatment with linerixibat. Some patients received antidiarrhoeals (e.g., loperamide) to manage symptoms (see section 4.4).

## Reporting of suspected adverse reactions

Reporting suspected adverse reactions after authorisation of the medicinal product is important. It allows continued monitoring of the benefit/risk balance of the medicinal product. Healthcare professionals are asked to report any suspected adverse reactions via the national reporting system listed in Appendix V.

## 4.9 Overdose

## Symptoms

An overdose may result in symptoms from an exaggeration of the known adverse reactions of the medicinal product, mainly gastrointestinal effects.

In clinical studies, linerixibat was given to adults with cholestatic pruritus in PBC at doses up to 180 mg daily (single and/or divided doses), and a dose dependent increase in the incidence of gastrointestinal adverse reactions was observed (see section 4.8).

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## Management

There is no specific antidote. In the event of an overdose, linerixibat must be discontinued, the patient monitored for any signs and symptoms and general supportive measures instituted if needed. Further management should be as clinically indicated or as recommended by the national poisons centre, where available.

## 5. PHARMACOLOGICAL PROPERTIES

## 5.1 Pharmacodynamic properties

Pharmacotherapeutic group: Bile and liver therapy, other drugs for bile therapy, ATC code: A05AX08

## Mechanism of action

Linerixibat is a locally acting, minimally absorbed reversible inhibitor of the ileal bile acid transporter (IBAT). It decreases the reabsorption of bile acids in the ileum leading to increased faecal elimination. Linerixibat improves pruritus in PBC patients which is assumed to be due to a decrease in mediators of pruritus including serum bile acids, lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) [via measurement of autotaxin as a surrogate] and interleukin 31 (IL-31).

## Pharmacodynamic effects

Linerixibat reduces total serum bile acids in healthy volunteers and patients with cholestatic pruritus in PBC. In healthy volunteers, serum bile acids were reduced within 1 day of treatment with linerixibat compared to placebo treatment.

## Clinical efficacy

The efficacy and safety of linerixibat in the treatment of cholestatic pruritus in patients with PBC were evaluated in a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre, global Phase 3 study, GLISTEN. Patients (N = 238) were randomised to Lynavoy 40 mg twice daily (N = 119) or placebo (N = 119) for 24 weeks. Seventeen patients who participated remotely (fully decentralised site) were not included in the efficacy analyses (Lynavoy N = 111, placebo N = 110). One patient randomised to placebo withdrew before initiating treatment.

Eligible patients had a confirmed diagnosis of PBC and moderate or severe pruritus (assessed by the Worst Itch Numerical Rating Scale [Worst Itch NRS] in the 4 weeks prior to randomisation. Patients who were on stable UDCA, fibrates, bile acid binding resins or other antipruritic therapy at baseline were eligible to enrol and to continue their PBC and/or pruritus treatment regimen unchanged throughout the duration of the study. Exclusion criteria included hepatic decompensation, chronic viral hepatitis, symptomatic cholelithiasis, active cholecystitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, alcoholic liver disease, current malignancies, active inflammatory ileal disease, bariatric surgery with ileal bypass, skin disorders with itch, clinically significant diarrhoea, severe renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] &lt; 30 mL/min/1.73 m 2 ) and treatment with obeticholic acid (OCA).

The mean age of patients was 55.5 years (range 30 to 80), 95 % were female, 56 % were Caucasian. The mean (range) ALP at baseline was 234 IU/L (46.5-1102): 24 % of subjects were ≤ 1 times the upper limit of normal (x ULN), 28 % were &gt; 1 x ULN and &lt; 1.67 x ULN, and 48 % were ≥ 1.67 x ULN. Mean (SD) baseline itch severity was 7.4 (1.5) based on Worst Itch NRS with 41 % categorized as having moderate itch and 59 % as having severe itch. At baseline 97 % of patients were receiving UDCA. A total of 43 % of patients randomised to linerixibat and 48 % randomised to placebo were receiving concomitant fibrates, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, bile acid binding resins, gabapentin, antihistamines, opiate antagonists, rifampicin and/or pregabalin which could impact itch.

## Pruritus-related sleep interference

Patients randomised to linerixibat had significantly improved pruritus-related sleep interference compared with placebo over 24 weeks of treatment (-2.70 linerixibat vs -2.16 placebo; LS mean difference -0.54 [95 % CI: -1.02, -0.06], p = 0.027).

## Additional pruritus-related assessments

At baseline, patients randomised to both linerixibat and placebo reported similar levels of pruritus severity based on the Patient Global Impression of Severity (PGI-S) item (recall: past 7 days; score range: 1 [absent] to 5 [very severe]). Treatment with linerixibat resulted in more improvement in the PGI-S score when compared to placebo over 24 weeks (LS mean change from baseline -1.17 vs. -0.80; LS mean difference -0.37 [95 % CI: -0.55, -0.18]). At Week 24, a larger proportion of the linerixibat group (66 %) compared to placebo (48 %) reported 'absent' or 'mild' itch.

Patients randomised to linerixibat reported more itch improvement over 24 weeks compared to placebo as measured by Patient Global Impression of Change (PGI-C) scores (range: 1 [very much improved] to 7 [very much worse]) (LS mean 1.97 vs. 2.51; LS mean difference -0.53 [95 % CI: -0.81, -0.25]). At Week 24, a larger proportion of the linerixibat group (54 %) compared to placebo (36 %) reported itch severity to be 'very much improved', and a larger proportion of patients in the placebo group (26 %) reported 'no change' compared to the linerixibat group (3 %).

## Maintenance of efficacy

In the open label, long term extension study (LLSAT), the majority of responders following 24 weeks of linerixibat treatment maintained their response at Week 52: 72.7 % of those with ≥ 2-point response, 80.8 % with a ≥ 3-point response and 81.8 % with a ≥ 4-point response.

## Paediatric population

The European Medicines Agency has waived the obligation to submit the results of studies with Lynavoy in all subsets of the paediatric population in the treatment of cholestatic pruritus with PBC (see section 4.2 for information on paediatric use).

## 5.2 Pharmacokinetic properties

Linerixibat inhibits the ileal bile acid transporter in the lumen of the small intestine; therefore, plasma levels of linerixibat are not relevant to its clinical efficacy. Following single and repeated oral administration of linerixibat, plasma concentrations were often below the limit of quantification (10 pg/mL).

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## Absorption

Linerixibat is minimally absorbed following oral administration. The absolute oral bioavailability of linerixibat is 0.05 %. Following multiple doses of linerixibat 40 mg twice daily in PBC patients with cholestatic pruritus, plasma concentrations are often below the limit of detection (10 pg/mL) and highly variable.

## Effect of food

The effect of food on linerixibat plasma concentrations is not clinically relevant. Due to the minimal absorption of linerixibat, pharmacokinetic parameters in the food effect study were highly variable. Peak systemic concentrations of linerixibat (Cmax) were lower when 40 mg linerixibat was administered orally under fed compared to fasted conditions (geometric least squares mean ratio 0.663, 90 % CI 0.443 to 0.991). Despite the lack of clinically important pharmacokinetic effects with food, it is recommended to administer linerixibat approximately 30 minutes prior to food or beverage (other than water) to allow linerixibat to enter the gastrointestinal lumen prior to release of bile acids (see section 4.2).

## Distribution

The in vitro plasma protein binding range of linerixibat was 71.0 to 76.4 %.

## Biotransformation

Following administration of oral radiolabelled linerixibat, no linerixibat metabolites have been detected in plasma. Three minor oxidative metabolites were detected in faeces, and each accounted for negligible radioactivity (less than 1 %), demonstrating that linerixibat is minimally metabolised in humans.

## Elimination

Following oral administration of radiolabelled linerixibat, approximately 97 % of the dose was excreted in faeces; approximately 0.04 % of the dose was excreted in urine. More than 99 % of faecal radioactivity was determined to be unchanged and unabsorbed linerixibat.

## Other special populations

No clinically meaningful differences in the pharmacokinetics of linerixibat were observed based on body weight, age, sex, or race/ethnicity.

## Renal impairment

Linerixibat has not been studied in patients with severe renal impairment or end stage renal disease (ESRD) including those on haemodialysis. Renal impairment is not expected to impact linerixibat exposure, because linerixibat is minimally absorbed and has negligible recovery in urine (&lt; 0.04 % of the oral dose).

## Hepatic impairment

Hepatic metabolism is not a major component of the elimination of linerixibat. In a pharmacokinetic study in participants with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh B), the mean AUC(0-) and Cmax values were approximately 13 times and 9.4 times higher than matched healthy subjects. However, given that linerixibat is minimally absorbed, the systemic exposures in participants with moderate hepatic impairment following a 40 mg dose were still exceedingly low (Cmax 1 980 pg/mL) and were of a similar magnitude to exposures following a 40 mg dose in patients with PBC (Cmax 922 pg/mL). Linerixibat has not been studied in patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C).

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

## Drug interactions

## Bile acid binding resins

Colestyramine and colesevelam bind linerixibat in vitro (78-95 % bound); therefore, these resins have the potential to inhibit the effect of linerixibat on its target, IBAT (see sections 4.2 and 4.5).

## IBAT substrates

## Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA)

In linerixibat clinical studies, concomitant administration of UDCA with linerixibat had no effect on UDCA plasma concentrations and no clinically relevant increases in ALP were observed (see section 4.5). The geometric mean ratio (90 % CI) for dose-normalized UDCA AUC(0-24) was 0.99 (0.83, 1.19) when comparing co-administration of UDCA with linerixibat vs. with placebo. However, there was a significant decrease of the UDCA conjugates, glyco-UDCA and tauro-UDCA, of which the clinical relevance remains unclear (see section 4.5).

## Effects of linerixibat on cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYPs) and transporters

In in vitro studies, linerixibat did not inhibit CYPs 1A2, 2A6, 2B6, 2C8, 2C9, 2C19, 2D6, and 2E1, but was an inhibitor of CYP3A4. Due to the low fraction absorbed, an interaction based on CYP3A4 inhibition is not likely. Linerixibat did not induce CYPs 1A2, 2B6, and 3A4.

Linerixibat does not inhibit the transporters P-glycoprotein, breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), organic anion transporters (OAT) 1 and 3, organic cation transporter (OCT) 2, and multi drug and toxin extrusion protein (MATE) 1 and 2-K. In vitro studies suggest that there is a potential for linerixibat to inhibit OATP1B1 and OATP1B3. However, due to the low fraction absorbed, an interaction is not likely.

## 5.3 Preclinical safety data

Non-clinical data reveal no special hazard for humans based on studies of safety pharmacology, repeat-dose toxicity, genotoxicity, carcinogenic potential, or toxicity to reproduction.

Linerixibat did not cause developmental toxicity in rat or rabbit studies at doses up to 11-times (for rats) and 4.5-times (for rabbits) the AUC at the maximal recommended human dose (MRHD). Linerixibat had no effect on male or female fertility or early embryonic development in rats or on offspring during gestation and lactation when administered to dams at exposures approximately 12-times the AUC at the MRHD. Linerixibat was present in the plasma of offspring (up to 15.3 ng/mL) via lactational exposure.

## 1. What Lynavoy is and what it is used for

Lynavoy contains the active substance linerixibat.

Lynavoy is used in adults with primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) to treat itching caused by the buildup of bile acids (cholestatic pruritus). PBC is a condition where bile ducts in the liver become damaged over time, leading to a build-up of bile acids in the blood. This build-up is thought to cause itching in PBC.

Linerixibat works by blocking a protein in the small intestine known as ileal bile acid transporter (IBAT). Bile acids are made by the liver and released into the intestine to help digest food. Most bile acids released into the intestine are reabsorbed by IBAT and then sent back to the liver. By blocking IBAT, linerixibat, increases the amount of bile acids that are removed from the body through bowel movements (stool). This helps to reduce the build-up of bile acids in your body and so reduce itching.

## 2. What you need to know before you take Lynavoy

## Do not take Lynavoy

- if you are allergic to linerixibat or any of the other ingredients of this medicine (listed in section 6).
- ➔ Check with your doctor before taking Lynavoy if you may be allergic to any of its ingredients.

## Warnings and precautions

Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before taking Lynavoy if you have or had in the past:

- severely reduced liver function.

## While you are taking Lynavoy

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

- You may develop diarrhoea, which is a very common side effect of Lynavoy (see section 4, 'Possible side effects'). Talk to your doctor or pharmacist if you develop diarrhoea that interferes with your daily activities while taking Lynavoy. Drinking enough liquid is recommended in patients with diarrhoea to prevent dehydration.
- Taking Lynavoy may increase the level of liver enzymes (proteins) which can be a sign of liver problems. Your doctor will do blood tests to monitor your liver function before you start treatment with Lynavoy and also regularly during treatment.
- Before and during treatment, your doctor may also check your blood levels of vitamin A, D, E and K and your INR (international normalised ratio), which measures your risk for bleeding.
- ➔ Your doctor will tell you if Lynavoy is no longer suitable for you.

## Children and adolescents

Do not give Lynavoy to children and adolescents under 18 years of age, as it is not known if the medicine is safe and effective in this age group.

## Other medicines and Lynavoy

Tell your doctor or pharmacist if you are taking, have recently taken or might take any other medicines.

## Tell your doctor about all the medicines you take including:

- medicines known as bile acid binding resins (used to reduce absorption of bile acids from the intestine), such as colestyramine, colesevelam, or colestipol. These may reduce the effectiveness of Lynavoy.
- ➔ If you take any of these medicines, take Lynavoy at least 4 hours before or 4 hours after taking a bile acid binding resin.
- ➔ If you need more advice, talk to your doctor, pharmacist or nurse.

You can take ursodeoxycholic acid ('urso') while you are taking Lynavoy. Your dose will not normally need to be changed.

## Pregnancy and breast-feeding

If you are pregnant or breast-feeding, think you may be pregnant or are planning to have a baby:

- ➔ Talk to your doctor or pharmacist for advice before taking this medicine.

Lynavoy has not been studied in pregnant or breast-feeding women. If you become pregnant during treatment with Lynavoy

## ➔ Tell your doctor.

It is not known if the active substance of Lynavoy is passed into human breast milk. Your doctor will advise you if you can breast-feed during your treatment or if you should interrupt breast-feeding or treatment.

## Driving and using machines

Lynavoy is not expected to affect your ability to drive or use machines.

## Lynavoy contains sodium

This medicine contains less than 1 mmol sodium (23 mg) per tablet, that is to say essentially 'sodiumfree'.

## 3. How to take Lynavoy

Always take this medicine exactly as your doctor or pharmacist has told you. Check with your doctor or pharmacist if you are not sure.

The recommended dose of Lynavoy is one 40 mg tablet taken twice a day (one in the morning and one in the evening).

<div style=\"page-break-after: always\"></div>

Lynavoy is a tablet that is taken by mouth. Swallow the tablet whole, with some water, as follows.

- Take Lynavoy on an empty stomach, except for water, which you can drink any time as needed (but no other drink)
- After taking Lynavoy, wait about 30 minutes before you have any food or drink (except water).

If you also need to take a bile acid binding resin (such as colestyramine, colesevelam, or colestipol):

- take it at least 4 hours before or 4 hours after you take Lynavoy (see also 'Other medicines and Lynavoy' in section 2).

Do not remove or swallow the desiccant canister found in the bottle (see 'What Lynavoy looks like and contents of the pack' in section 6).

## If you take more Lynavoy than you should

If you take too many tablets of Lynavoy, contact your doctor or pharmacist for advice. The most likely effects of taking too much Lynavoy are diarrhoea and stomach pain.

## If you forget to take Lynavoy

Do not take a double dose to make up for a forgotten tablet. Take the missed dose as soon as you remember. Then continue taking your tablet at regular times as usual. If you are not sure what to do, ask your doctor or pharmacist.

## If you stop taking Lynavoy

Take Lynavoy for as long as your doctor recommends. Don't stop unless your doctor advises you to.

If you have any further questions on the use of this medicine, ask your doctor or pharmacist.

## 4. Possible side effects

Like all medicines, this medicine can cause side effects, although not everybody gets them.

Very common side effects (may affect more than 1 in 10 people)

- diarrhoea
- belly (abdominal) pain

Common side effects (may affect up to 1 in 10 people)

- increases in enzymes (proteins) made by the liver (ALT, AST), as seen in blood tests
